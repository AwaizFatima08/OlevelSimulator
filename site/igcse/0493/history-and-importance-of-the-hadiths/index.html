Q1The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Core · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the Hadiths are the second source of Islamic law after the Qur'an, referring to what the Qur'an says about the Prophet (pbuh). [10]
  2. (b) Give two examples of how a Muslim uses the Hadiths in daily life. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The Qur'an commands believers to obey God and obey the Messenger (e.g. Sura 4.59); it tells them to take whatever the Messenger gives and avoid what he forbids (Sura 59.7); it calls him an excellent example to follow (Sura 33.21); it says he does not speak from his own desire but from revelation (Sura 53.3–4); his role was to explain the Qur'an, so his words and actions are needed to practise it; but the Hadiths come second because they are reported by people and must agree with the Qur'an, which is God's own word.
    Indicative content: Obey the Messenger (4.59 or similar); take what he gives (59.7); excellent example (33.21); not from desire (53.3–4); he explains the Qur'an; needed to practise worship; second because reported by humans / must agree with the Qur'an. Marked by levels out of 10. Level 3 (6–8): a mostly accurate answer covering the main points, with some detail but gaps or little supporting evidence. Level 2 (3–5): a general answer with some relevant points but limited detail or some errors. Level 1 (1–2): a few basic or partly relevant points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
  2. (b) e.g. following his practice in prayer and wudu; his manners in eating (saying God's name, eating with the right hand); greeting others with salam; kindness to neighbours and parents; honest trade.
    Mark points to credit: Correct example and credit explaining how the Hadith guides the action. Model answer: e.g. following his practice in prayer and wudu; his manners in eating (saying God's name, eating with the right hand); greeting others with salam; kindness to neighbours and parents; honest trade. Q2The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Core · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) The Sunna includes the sayings, actions and silent approval of the Prophet (pbuh). Explain each of these, giving an example. [10]
  2. (b) Why is the Prophet's silent approval an important source of law? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sayings: his words teaching or commanding, e.g. his teaching that actions are judged by intentions. Actions: what he did, which Companions observed and copied, e.g. how he performed prayer, wudu and hajj. Silent approval: something done in his presence or known to him which he did not object to, showing it is allowed, e.g. Companions eating a desert lizard in front of him although he himself did not eat it.
    Indicative content: Sayings (qawl) — his words, e.g. 'actions are judged by intentions'; actions (fi'l) — what he did, e.g. how he prayed or performed hajj ('pray as you have seen me pray'); silent approval (taqrir) — what was done in his presence that he did not forbid, e.g. eating the lizard (dabb), or the Companions' different times for 'Asr on the way to Banu Qurayza. All three types, each explained with an example, are needed for Level 4. Marked by levels out of 10. Level 4 (9–10): a full, accurate and well-organised answer with precise detail (names, dates, places, sequence) and supporting references to the Qur'an or Hadith where relevant. Level 3 (6–8): a mostly accurate answer covering the main points, with some detail but gaps or little supporting evidence. Level 2 (3–5): a general answer with some relevant points but limited detail or some errors. Level 1 (1–2): a few basic or partly relevant points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
  2. (b) He would not stay silent about something wrong, as his task was to guide; so his silence shows that the practice is permitted, which gives Muslims a wider range of lawful actions and shows Islam's ease.
    Mark points to credit: He would correct wrong; silence = permission / ease. Model answer: He would not stay silent about something wrong, as his task was to guide; so his silence shows that the practice is permitted, which gives Muslims a wider range of lawful actions and shows Islam's ease. Q3The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by the Sunna and the Hadith. [2]
  2. (b) Describe two ways in which the Companions used the Hadiths to make decisions after the death of the Prophet (pbuh). [4]
  3. (c) What does the plague example teach Muslims about following the Sunna in a modern health emergency? [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sunna: the practice / way of life of the Prophet (pbuh), his sayings, actions and approvals. Hadith: a report that records what he said, did or approved.
    1 mark for each correct definition.
  2. (b) When 'Umar was travelling to Syria and heard there was plague there, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf told him the Prophet (pbuh) had said not to enter a land with plague or leave it if you are there; 'Umar turned back. When Abu Bakr was asked about a grandmother's share of inheritance, he asked the Companions and accepted the report of al-Mughira, confirmed by Muhammad ibn Maslama, of what the Prophet (pbuh) had given. Companions consulted Mothers of the Believers about his practice at home.
    Up to 2 marks per example: situation (1) and use of the Hadith (1).
  3. (c) The Sunna supports sensible public-health measures such as quarantine; Muslims should follow medical advice to protect others, as many did during the COVID-19 pandemic.
    1 mark: link to quarantine/health rules; 1 mark: modern application.

Q6The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Standard · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Describe ijma' (consensus) as a source of Islamic law and explain how it is linked to the Qur'an and Hadith. [10]
  2. (b) Some people say ijma' is impossible today because Muslims are spread across the world. How far do you agree? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ijma' is the agreement of the qualified scholars of the community (first the Companions) on a ruling after the Prophet's death; it is used where the Qur'an and Hadith are not explicit; it gets its authority from the Qur'an's command to obey those in authority and to hold together and not divide, and from the Hadith that the community will not agree on an error; it must be based on and agree with the Qur'an and Sunna; an example is the Companions' agreement to collect the Qur'an into one book.
    Indicative content: Definition; first by Companions, later by scholars; used when texts are not explicit; Qur'anic basis; Hadith basis; must agree with Qur'an and Sunna; example. Level 2 (3–5): a general answer with some relevant points but limited detail or some errors. Level 1 (1–2): a few basic or partly relevant points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
  2. (b) Full agreement of every scholar is hard to prove today; but councils of scholars meet internationally and reach collective rulings on new questions, and long-standing consensus on basic matters (e.g. five daily prayers) still guides Muslims; so ijma' remains useful in a modified form.
    Mark points to credit: Argument for difficulty; counter-argument with example; judgement. Q7The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Standard · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the Hadiths explain the details of two of the following: zakat; fasting; hajj. [10]
  2. (b) Why does this show that the Qur'an and the Hadiths cannot be separated? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Zakat: the Qur'an commands it and names who receives it; the Hadiths give the minimum amount of wealth (nisab), the period of a year, and the rates (e.g. one-fortieth of savings and trade goods, and set amounts for crops and livestock). Fasting: the Qur'an commands fasting in Ramadan; the Hadiths explain the pre-dawn meal, breaking the fast promptly at sunset, what breaks a fast (accidental eating does not), and the extra night prayers. Hajj: the Qur'an commands hajj and mentions some rites; the Prophet (pbuh) performed it in front of the Companions and told them to learn their rites from him, so the order and details of the rites (ihram, tawaf, standing at 'Arafa, stoning) come from his practice.
    Indicative content: For each chosen pillar, what the Qur'an says and the details from Hadith: zakat — Qur'an commands it with prayer (2.43) and names recipients (9.60); Hadith gives the nisab, rate (2.5%) and items. Fasting — Qur'an commands it (2.183–185); Hadith gives suhur and iftar, what breaks the fast, Laylat al-Qadr, voluntary fasts. Hajj — Qur'an commands it (3.97); Hadith gives the rites in detail (Farewell Hajj: 'take your rites from me'). Both chosen pillars must be covered for Level 3 and above. Marked by levels out of 10.   2. (b) Without the Hadiths Muslims could not perform basic duties commanded in the Qur'an; the Qur'an gives the command and the Hadiths show how to obey it.

Q8The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Standard · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Explain the differences between the Qur'an, a Hadith Qudsi and an ordinary (Prophetic) Hadith. [10]
  2. (b) Why is it important for Muslims to know these differences? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The Qur'an: God's own words, both meaning and wording, revealed through Jibril, recited in prayer, preserved in one text, and transmitted by vast numbers. A Hadith Qudsi: meaning from God but expressed in the Prophet's own words, in which he reports what God said; it is not part of the Qur'an, is not recited in prayer as Qur'an, and is checked through its chain like other Hadiths. A Prophetic Hadith: the Prophet's own words, actions or approvals, guided by God but not reported as God speaking; its authenticity varies and is graded by scholars.
    Indicative content: Qur'an — God's words and wording, revealed through Jibril, recited in prayer, mutawatir, a miracle. Hadith Qudsi — meaning from God in the Prophet's words, not recited in prayer, may be graded. Prophetic Hadith — the Prophet's own words and actions, graded for authenticity. All three must be covered and contrasted for Level 4. Marked by levels out of 10.   2. (b) So that they give each the correct level of authority and do not treat a Hadith as Qur'an or reject an authentic Hadith; it protects the Qur'an's unique status.
    Credit a reasoned answer such as: So that they give each the correct level of authority and do not treat a Hadith as Qur'an or reject an authentic Hadith; it protects the Qur'an's unique status. Q9The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Describe the instructions given by the Prophet (pbuh) about how his Hadiths should be passed on accurately. [4]
  2. (b) Some of the closest Companions, such as Abu Bakr and 'Umar, narrated far fewer Hadiths than younger Companions such as Abu Hurayra and Ibn 'Abbas. Suggest reasons for this and what it shows about their attitude to the Hadiths. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) He warned that anyone who deliberately lies about him will take his place in Hell; he prayed for the one who hears his words, memorises them and passes them on exactly as heard; he told those present to convey to those absent; Companions such as 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr were allowed to write his words; Companions were careful, some reporting little for fear of error.
    1 mark each for any four of: warning against lying about him; prayer for those who convey accurately; present to inform absent; permission to write (example); Companions' caution.
  2. (b) They feared making a mistake and so falsely attributing words to the Prophet (pbuh), in view of his warning; they were busy leading and governing the community; they died earlier, before later generations came seeking Hadiths, while younger Companions lived longer and taught many students; 'Umar also discouraged too much narration so that people would concentrate on the Qur'an. It shows deep respect for the Prophet's words and a strong sense of responsibility for accuracy.
    1 mark per reason, up to 3: fear of error / warning against lying about him; occupied with leadership; died earlier while younger Companions lived longer and taught; 'Umar's wish to keep focus on the Qur'an. 1 mark: what it shows (respect, caution, responsibility).
Q10The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Explain how qiyas (analogy) works, using an example from the life of the Prophet (pbuh) or from later scholars. [10]
  2. (b) Why do you think the Prophet (pbuh) explained his answer by comparison instead of simply giving a ruling? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Qiyas compares a new case with an existing ruling from the Qur'an or Sunna that has the same underlying cause, and applies the same ruling; the Prophet (pbuh) himself used reasoning of this kind: when a woman asked whether she could perform hajj for her mother who had died having vowed to do it, he asked whether she would pay her mother's debt, and said a debt to God is even more deserving to be paid; later scholars applied the ban on wine (because it intoxicates) to all intoxicating drugs; qiyas is used only when there is no clear text or consensus.
    Indicative content: Definition — analogy; the common cause ('illa) between the original case and the new case; the Prophet's example (performing hajj for a dead mother compared with paying her debt) or another correct example with original case, cause and new case; used only after the Qur'an, Sunna and ijma'; carried out by qualified scholars. An accurate example with original case, cause and new case is needed for Level 4. Marked by levels out of 10. Level 2 (3–5): a general answer with some relevant points but limited detail or some errors. Level 1 (1–2): a few basic or partly relevant points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
  2. (b) To teach the Companions how to reason so they could answer new questions after him; to make the ruling easy to understand and accept by linking it to something familiar; it shows Islam encourages careful, reasoned thinking within the guidance of revelation.
    Mark points to credit: Developed reason (teach method; understanding; Islam values reason within revelation). Q11The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain what ijtihad is and describe the qualities that a person needs in order to carry it out. [4]
  2. (b) The Prophet (pbuh) said that a sincere judge who makes a mistake still receives a reward. What does this teach Muslims about honest effort in their own work? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ijtihad is the effort of a qualified scholar to work out a ruling from the sources when there is no clear text; it includes methods such as qiyas; the scholar needs deep knowledge of the Qur'an and Hadith (including which Hadiths are authentic), of Arabic, of earlier rulings and points of consensus, of the methods of law, and good character and piety; the Prophet (pbuh) taught that a judge who strives and is right gets two rewards and one who strives and is wrong gets one reward.
    1 mark each for any four of: definition; used where texts are silent; knowledge of Qur'an/Hadith; Arabic; previous rulings/consensus; methodology; piety/integrity; Hadith of rewards for the judge.
  2. (b) God values sincere, careful effort, not only results; so a student, doctor or judge should study thoroughly and try their best; this removes fear of trying, but it does not excuse carelessness or lack of qualification; it also encourages tolerance of differing scholarly opinions.
    Level marking. 1: simple lesson. 2: lesson explained. 3: applied to an example from work/study today. 4: developed with a limit (needs preparation/qualification) or link to tolerance of differences.
Q12The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks

Q13The Hadiths and other sources of Islamic law · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Explain, with examples, why the Hadiths are needed to understand the meaning of some verses of the Qur'an. [10]
  2. (b) A classmate in Lahore says he follows 'only the Qur'an' and ignores the Hadiths. How would you respond? [4]
Show answer
