Read the text carefully, then answer the question.Text: Growing up with more than one languageWalk into almost any classroom in Lahore and you will hear children switching between Punjabi, Urdu and English without a second thought. Some studies suggest they are also better at solving puzzles that require them to change rules halfway through.There are social rewards too. A child who speaks a grandparent's language can share stories, jokes and songs that would be lost in translation, keeping a family's history alive. Speaking the language of a neighbourhood or playground helps a newcomer make friends more quickly.Many bilingual children develop a keen awareness of how language works. They notice that the same idea can be expressed in different ways and that some words cannot be translated exactly. Teachers report that this curiosity can make learning a third or fourth language easier later in life.Later on, the practical benefits become obvious. Employers in tourism, trade, health care and international business value staff who can speak to customers in their own language. Travel, too, becomes richer when you can ask directions, read signs and chat to people rather than relying on gestures.Some researchers even believe that a lifetime of using several languages may help keep the brain active in old age, though they stress that more evidence is needed. What seems clear is that, for most children, an extra language is a gift rather than a burden.
