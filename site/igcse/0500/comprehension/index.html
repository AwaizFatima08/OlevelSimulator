Q1Explaining ideas (3-mark) · Core · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Night at the fish harbour(1) Most of the city is asleep when the fish harbour wakes up. By two in the morning, the first trawlers are nosing their way past the breakwater, their decks lit by a single bulb and their crews hoarse from a week of shouting over the engines. Within an hour, the concrete quay has become a crowded marketplace where nobody seems to stand still.(2) The catch is unloaded in plastic crates and carried on heads and shoulders to the auction floor. Here the middlemen, known locally as bolidars, call out prices so quickly that a newcomer hears only noise. Buyers signal with a raised finger or a nod; a whole crate of pomfret can change hands in less than ten seconds. Speed matters because fish lose value every minute they sit in the warm air.(3) Ice is the harbour's real currency. Large blocks arrive on lorries from factories on the edge of the city and are crushed in roaring machines before being shovelled over the fish. A boat owner who cannot afford enough ice on the way out to sea may have to sell his whole catch cheaply on his return, because no buyer trusts fish that has been kept warm for days.(4) The harbour also supports hundreds of people who never go near a boat. Women sit in long rows peeling prawns for export, paid by the kilogram. Boys mend torn nets with plastic shuttles, and tea sellers move between the crowds with kettles balanced on charcoal stoves. For many families, the few hours before dawn bring in the only income of the day.(5) By nine o'clock it is over. The auction floor is hosed down, gulls quarrel over the scraps and the crews sleep in the shade of their boats. The trawlers will be refuelled, re-iced and sent out again before the week is out.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 2, explain why buyers and sellers at the auction work so quickly. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 3, explain why ice is described as the harbour's "real currency". [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fish lose value the longer they are left out; the air is warm so fish spoil quickly; a quick sale gets a better price / keeps the fish fresh for the buyer. (Accept: so the catch can be iced and moved on before it deteriorates.)
    1 mark each for three of: fish lose value with every minute they wait; the air is warm / heat spoils fish; selling fast keeps the price high; buyers want fresh fish; a quick system lets a whole crate change hands in seconds so the large catch can be cleared. Own words not required but lifting the whole sentence earns 1 only.
  2. (b) Without ice fish cannot be sold at a good price; ice is bought and paid for like money / it must be purchased before a trip; a boat owner without enough ice is forced to sell cheaply because buyers do not trust warm fish, so ice determines how much money is made.
    1 mark each for three of: ice keeps the fish fresh; fresh fish keeps its value; ice must be bought before going to sea (it costs money); lack of ice forces a cheap sale; buyers will not trust fish kept warm; so ice effectively decides a crew's earnings (as valuable as money).
Q2Explaining ideas (3-mark) · Core · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Forests that grow in the sea(1) Along the coast near Karachi, where the River Indus finally meets the Arabian Sea, there are forests that spend half of each day partly underwater. These are mangroves: tough, low trees that can survive in salty mud where almost no other tree could live.(2) Mangroves have developed clever ways of coping with their difficult home. Some species push up roots like small pencils through the mud so that they can take in air when the tide is out. Others get rid of excess salt through their leaves, which can taste salty if you touch them to your tongue.(3) For coastal villages, these forests are extremely valuable. The tangled roots form a nursery where young fish, crabs and shrimp can shelter from larger predators, so a healthy mangrove forest means better catches for fishermen further out at sea. The trees also act as a living wall: when storms push huge waves towards the land, mangroves slow the water down and reduce the damage to homes behind them.(4) In the past, large areas of mangrove disappeared. Less fresh water now reaches the sea because so much of the river is taken for farming upstream, and the trees were also cut for firewood and to feed camels. Some patches were cleared to make way for building.(5) Recently there has been better news. Local people, many of them from fishing families, have been employed to plant millions of young mangroves in the mudflats, working from small boats at low tide. Villagers who once saw the trees mainly as fuel now guard them, because they have noticed that the fish and crabs are returning. Experts warn, however, that planting alone is not enough: unless enough river water reaches the delta, the new forests may struggle to survive.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 3, explain why mangrove forests are valuable to coastal villages. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 4, explain why large areas of mangrove disappeared in the past. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) They shelter young fish, crabs and shrimp, so fishermen catch more; they slow storm waves; this protects villagers' homes from damage.
    1 mark each for three of: roots are a nursery/shelter for young sea creatures; protects them from predators; leads to better catches/income for fishermen; slow down storm waves; reduce damage to homes.
  2. (b) Less fresh river water reaches the coast because it is used for farming upstream; trees were cut for firewood and camel fodder; some areas were cleared for building.
    1 mark each for: less fresh water reaching the sea (taken for farming); cutting for fuel / animal feed; clearance for construction.
Q3Explaining ideas (3-mark) · Core · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Champions of remembering(1) In a quiet hall, competitors sit at long tables staring at shuffled packs of playing cards. They have five minutes to memorise the exact order of fifty-two cards, and the best of them will need far less. Welcome to a memory championship, where people who often describe their natural memory as "completely ordinary" perform feats that look almost magical.(2) The secret, the competitors insist, is technique rather than talent. Most use a method that is more than two thousand years old, often called the memory palace. They imagine a familiar building, such as their own home, and place vivid images at points along a route through it. To recall the information, they simply walk through the building in their minds and look at what they have left there.(3) The images work best when they are strange, funny or even disgusting. A plain list of words slips away quickly, but a picture of a camel wearing sunglasses sitting in your bath is hard to forget. Competitors spend months building a personal set of images for every card and every number from 00 to 99.(4) Training is demanding. Top memorisers practise for an hour or more each day, timing themselves and recording their mistakes. Many say the hardest part is not remembering but staying calm, because a moment of panic during a competition can wipe out an entire route.(5) Researchers who have studied these champions found that their brains are not unusually large or differently shaped. What changes is the way different parts of the brain work together after training. Teachers have taken an interest, too: some students now use memory palaces to learn vocabulary or historical dates. Critics point out, however, that memorising facts is not the same as understanding them, and that a card champion may still forget where he left his house keys.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 2, explain how the memory palace method works. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 3, explain why competitors use strange or funny images. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) You imagine a building you know well; you place vivid pictures of the information at points along a route through it; to remember, you walk the route in your mind and 'see' the pictures again.
    1 mark each for: familiar building imagined; images placed at points along a route; recall by mentally walking the route and looking at the images.
  2. (b) Ordinary lists are quickly forgotten; unusual, amusing or disgusting pictures stand out and stick in the mind; so the information is easier to recall later.
    1 mark each for: plain words slip away; odd/funny/disgusting images are memorable (e.g. camel in a bath); this makes recall easier / more reliable.

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Bees on the balcony(1) Beekeeping was once seen as a country pursuit, practised by farmers with hives at the edge of their orchards. Today, some of the most productive hives can be found on the flat roofs of city hotels, offices and apartment buildings.(2) It may seem surprising that bees thrive in cities, but there are good reasons. Urban parks, gardens, street trees and window boxes contain a wide variety of flowers that bloom at different times of year. In many farming areas, by contrast, huge fields are planted with a single crop that flowers for only a few weeks, and the use of chemical sprays can harm insects.(3) Hotels and restaurants have been quick to see the appeal. Honey produced on their own roof gives them a story to tell customers, and guests can sometimes join guided visits to the hives. A jar labelled with the name of a district sells for far more than ordinary honey from a supermarket.(4) Not everyone is delighted. Neighbours sometimes worry about being stung, although honeybees away from their hive are generally not aggressive. A more serious concern is that too many hives in one area may leave too little food for the wild bees and other insects that also pollinate flowers. Some experts argue that planting more flowers would help nature more than adding more hives.(5) Beginners also discover that keeping bees requires real commitment. Hives must be checked regularly for disease and for signs that the colony is preparing to swarm, when thousands of bees leave together in search of a new home. A swarm settling on a busy street corner can cause alarm, and keepers need to be ready to collect it quickly. For those willing to learn, however, the reward is more than honey: many say that caring for bees has made them notice the seasons for the first time.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 2, explain why bees can do well in cities. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 4, explain why some people are concerned about city beekeeping. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cities have many different flowers in parks, gardens and streets; they bloom at different times so food lasts all year; farms often grow one crop that flowers briefly and may use harmful sprays.
    1 mark each for three of: variety of flowers in urban spaces; flowering spread through the year; farmland often has a single crop; that crop flowers for only a few weeks; farm chemicals can harm insects.
  2. (b) Neighbours fear stings; too many hives may take food from wild bees and other insects; some experts think planting flowers would help nature more than adding hives.
    1 mark each for: fear of being stung; competition for food with wild pollinators; more hives may not help nature as much as more flowers.

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Farms above the traffic(1) Look up in some of Asia's most crowded cities and you may see something unexpected: rows of lettuces, chillies and spinach growing on the roofs of car parks, shopping centres and apartment blocks. Rooftop farming, once a hobby for a few enthusiasts, has become a serious business.(2) The attraction is obvious once you think about distance. Most vegetables sold in big cities travel hundreds of kilometres by lorry before they reach a shop. During the journey they lose freshness, and some are thrown away before anyone buys them. A farm on the roof of a supermarket can deliver its produce by lift in a few minutes.(3) Roof gardens also change the buildings beneath them. A layer of soil and plants absorbs heat that would otherwise pass straight through the concrete, so the floors below stay cooler and need less air-conditioning. In heavy storms, the plants and soil hold back rainwater, which reduces the sudden rush into drains that causes flooding in the streets.(4) Not every roof is suitable, however. Wet soil is extremely heavy, and many older buildings were never designed to carry the weight. Engineers often have to check a structure before a single seed is planted. To reduce the load, some farms use no soil at all: the plants grow in shallow trays while water containing dissolved nutrients is pumped past their roots.(5) The farmers themselves are often young people with little background in agriculture. Many learned from online videos and from their own mistakes. One grower in a busy commercial district admits that her first crop of tomatoes was destroyed in a single afternoon by pigeons. "Nobody tells you that birds are the biggest problem on a roof," she says. She now covers every bed with fine netting and sells salad leaves to six restaurants within walking distance.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 2, explain why rooftop farms can supply better vegetables than farms in the countryside. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 3, explain how a roof garden helps the building and the street below. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Countryside vegetables travel long distances by lorry; they lose freshness on the journey / some are wasted; rooftop produce reaches the shop in minutes so it is fresher.
    1 mark each for three of: most city vegetables come from hundreds of km away; long lorry journeys reduce freshness; some produce is spoiled/thrown away before sale; rooftop farm is in or on the building; produce arrives by lift within minutes, so it is fresher/less is wasted.
  2. (b) Soil and plants absorb heat so the building stays cooler; less air-conditioning is needed (saving energy/money); in storms rain is held back so drains do not overflow and streets flood less.
    1 mark each for three of: absorbs heat that would pass through concrete; lower floors stay cooler; less air-conditioning needed; plants/soil store rainwater; slows the sudden flow into drains; reduces street flooding.
Q6Explaining ideas (3-mark) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Why teenagers cannot get up(1) Every school morning, in homes across the world, the same battle takes place. A parent calls, then calls again; the teenager groans, pulls the blanket higher and insists that five more minutes will make all the difference. It is easy to put this down to laziness, but sleep scientists now argue that biology is largely to blame.(2) During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts. The hormone that makes us feel sleepy is released later in the evening than it was in childhood, so many teenagers simply do not feel tired until well after eleven o'clock. If they still have to wake at six for school, they lose several hours of the sleep their growing bodies need.(3) The effects of this shortfall go beyond yawning in class. Researchers have linked regular lack of sleep in young people with poorer memory, slower reactions and greater difficulty controlling emotions. Information learned during the day is sorted and stored while we sleep, which means that a student who stays up revising until two in the morning may remember less than one who goes to bed early.(4) Screens make matters worse. Phones and tablets give off bright light that tells the brain it is still daytime, and the messages and videos on them keep the mind alert. Many teenagers also feel that switching off means missing out on conversations with friends.(5) Some schools have experimented with starting the day later, and several report better attendance and fewer students falling asleep at their desks. Others cannot change their timetables because of bus routes or parents' working hours. For individual families, experts suggest simpler steps: a fixed bedtime, phones charged outside the bedroom, and weekend lie-ins of no more than an hour or two, so that the body clock is not thrown into confusion every Monday.

  1. (a) Using paragraph 2, explain why many teenagers do not get enough sleep on school nights. [3]
  2. (b) Using paragraph 3, explain why staying up late to revise may be counter-productive. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Their body clock moves later during adolescence; the sleep hormone is released later so they do not feel tired until after 11 p.m.; but school still requires early waking, so hours of needed sleep are lost.
    1 mark each for: internal clock shifts in adolescence; sleepiness/hormone comes later in the evening so they cannot fall asleep early; early school start (6 a.m.) cuts sleep short / they need more sleep because they are growing.
  2. (b) Learning is sorted and stored during sleep; cutting sleep means less is stored so the student remembers less; lack of sleep also harms memory and reactions in general, so the extra study time is wasted.
    1 mark each for three of: memories are organised/stored during sleep; revising late shortens this; so less is remembered than by an early sleeper; regular sleep loss linked with poorer memory/slower reactions/emotional control; so the extra hours of revision do not pay off.

Read the text carefully, then answer the questions. Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Watching the ice(1) High in the mountains of northern Pakistan, thousands of glaciers feed the rivers on which millions of people depend. As temperatures rise, many of these glaciers are melting faster, and the water collects in lakes held back by loose walls of rock and ice. If one of these natural dams gives way, a wall of water, mud and boulders can rush down the valley within minutes.(2) Villages beneath such lakes cannot simply move away; their fields, orchards and houses have been there for generations. Instead, some communities have been trained to keep a close eye on the danger themselves. Volunteers take turns to check water levels and look for cracks or unusual sounds from the ice, reporting anything worrying by radio or mobile phone.(3) The volunteer system works because the volunteers know the land. An outside expert might visit a lake once a year, but a shepherd who grazes his animals nearby every summer notices small changes immediately: a stream that is suddenly muddy, or a rumble at night that was not there before. Local knowledge, combined with simple equipment, has given some valleys precious extra minutes of warning.(4) Warnings are useless unless people know what to do. Villages have marked safe routes to higher ground with painted stones, and schools hold practice drills so that children can lead elderly relatives uphill without panic. Some families keep a small bag packed with documents, medicines and dried food beside the door.(5) The volunteers are realistic about their limits. They cannot stop a glacier from melting, and a sudden collapse may still destroy bridges and crops. But in a valley where a flood once took everyone by surprise, an old farmer says the difference is clear. "Before, we were waiting for the mountain to decide," he says. "Now at least we are watching it together."

  2. (b) Using paragraph 4, explain how villages make sure that warnings save lives. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) They are there all the time rather than once a year; they know the land so well that they notice small changes such as a muddy stream or a new rumble; this gives earlier warning.
    1 mark each for three of: experts visit rarely (once a year); locals are present every day/season; familiarity with the land; notice small changes immediately; examples (muddy stream, night rumble) only if linked to noticing change; gives extra minutes of warning.
  2. (b) Safe routes uphill are marked so people know where to go; schools practise drills so children stay calm and can help elderly relatives; families keep emergency bags ready so they can leave at once.
    1 mark each for: marked routes (painted stones) to high ground; drills / practice so no panic and the elderly are helped; emergency bags (documents, medicine, food) for a fast departure.

Paragraph numbers are shown in brackets.Text: Running across the sand(1) Each year a small group of runners gathers at the edge of a desert to begin a race that most people would consider madness. Over six or seven days they will cover around two hundred and fifty kilometres of sand dunes, dry riverbeds and stony plains, carrying on their backs everything they need except water and a tent.(2) The heat is only the most obvious difficulty. Daytime temperatures may climb above forty degrees, but nights can be bitterly cold, so runners must carry warm clothing that adds to the weight of their packs. There are checkpoints every ten kilometres or so where runners collect a measured amount of water and have their condition assessed by medical staff. Anyone who appears confused or unable to drink is removed from the race, however strongly they protest. Every competitor must also carry a whistle, a mirror for signalling and a small amount of emergency food.(4) Preparation starts months earlier. Many runners train by running with a loaded backpack, and some sit in hot rooms or saunas to help their bodies cope with high temperatures. Equally important is learning to eat while exhausted: the body burns huge amounts of energy, yet hard effort and heat often destroy the appetite.(5) Why do people do it? Few are professional athletes; the field includes teachers, nurses and retired soldiers. Some are raising money for charity, but most talk about the experience itself: the silence of the desert at dawn, the friendships formed around campfires and the discovery that they are capable of far more than they believed.
