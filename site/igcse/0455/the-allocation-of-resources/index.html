Q2Mixed economic system and intervention · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a subsidy. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why a city government might subsidise public bus services in Lahore. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A payment made by the government to producers (or consumers) to lower the cost of producing or buying a product.

  2. (b) e.g. to reduce traffic congestion and pollution (external costs); to make travel affordable for low-income workers

Q3Mixed economic system and intervention · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

The table shows the weekly market for wheat flour in a city.Price (Rs per kg): 80, 90, 100, 110, 120Quantity demanded (thousand kg): 700, 650, 600, 550, 500Quantity supplied (thousand kg): 300, 400, 480, 550, 610

  1. (a) State the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity of wheat flour. [2]
  2. (b) The government sets a maximum price of Rs 90 per kg. Calculate the shortage that results. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two possible consequences of this maximum price. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 110 per kg; 550 thousand kg per week
    B1 price Rs 110; B1 quantity 550 thousand kg (where quantity demanded = quantity supplied).
  2. (b) 250 thousand kg per week
    M1 at Rs 90: quantity demanded 650, quantity supplied 400; A1 650 − 400 = 250 thousand kg.
  3. (c) e.g. queues/shortages leading to rationing; a black (illegal) market at higher prices
    1 mark for each consequence identified + 1 mark for each explanation, any two of: shortage so queues form/some consumers go without; rationing or first-come-first-served allocation needed; black market develops where flour is sold above the legal price; producers earn less so may cut output or quality in the long run; poorer households can buy flour more cheaply (if they can obtain it).
Q4Mixed economic system and intervention · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A government sets a minimum price for cotton of $1.60 per kg. The free-market equilibrium price is $1.40 per kg. At the minimum price, farmers supply 900 tonnes and buyers demand 650 tonnes (1 tonne = 1000 kg).

  1. (a) Calculate the surplus of cotton at the minimum price. [1]
  2. (b) The government buys the whole surplus at the minimum price. Calculate the cost to the government. [2]
  3. (c) Discuss whether a minimum price is the best way for the government to help cotton farmers. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) 250 tonnes
    B1 900 − 650 = 250 tonnes.
  2. (b) $400 000
    M1 250 × 1000 kg × $1.60; A1 $400 000.
  3. (c) Balanced answer: guarantees farmers a higher, stable income, but creates a surplus, costs the government money, raises prices for textile firms and may encourage over-production; alternatives such as subsidies for better seeds or direct income support may be better.
    Up to 3 marks for why it may help: higher and more certain income for farmers; reduces risk so farmers keep growing cotton; protects rural jobs. Up to 3 marks for why it may not: surplus must be bought and stored at a cost ($400 000) – opportunity cost of government spending; higher input price for textile mills reduces their competitiveness; encourages inefficient farmers to stay; alternatives (subsidy on inputs, training, direct cash support) may be cheaper. Max 5; a conclusion is needed for full marks.
Q5Mixed economic system and intervention · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A government places a specific (per-unit) tax of 30 cents on each can of sugary drink. Before the tax, the price was $1.00 and 2.0 million cans were sold each week. After the tax, the price is $1.20 and 1.7 million cans are sold each week.

  1. (a) Calculate the government's weekly revenue from the tax. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of the tax per can that is paid by consumers. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the tax might not reduce the consumption of sugary drinks very much. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) $510 000
    M1 tax per can × cans sold after tax = $0.30 × 1.7 million; A1 $510 000.
  2. (b) 66.7 % (2/3)
    M1 price rise for consumers = $1.20 − $1.00 = $0.20; A1 0.20 ÷ 0.30 × 100 = 66.7 %.
  3. (c) Demand for sugary drinks is price inelastic (e.g. they are habit-forming, a can takes a small share of income and consumers see few satisfactory substitutes), so the price rise causes a smaller percentage fall in quantity demanded.
    1 mark: demand is price inelastic; up to 3 more marks for development: drinks are habit-forming; spending on a can is a small share of income; few substitutes that consumers see as close; tax only partly passed on to consumers (price rose by 20 cents, not 30); consumers may switch to other untaxed sugary foods, so total sugar intake changes little; here a 20 % price rise cut quantity by only 15 % (PED −0.75).
Q6Price changes · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) The price of chicken feed falls. State what happens to the supply curve of chicken meat. [1]
  2. (b) Explain, describing a demand and supply diagram, the effect of this change on the equilibrium price and quantity of chicken meat. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) It shifts to the right (supply increases).
    B1 shifts right/outwards.
  2. (b) Supply curve shifts right; at the old price there is excess supply; price falls and quantity rises to a new equilibrium.
    1 mark: lower costs of production so supply curve shifts from S1 to S2 (right); 1 mark: excess supply at original price; 1 mark: equilibrium price falls (P1 to P2); 1 mark: equilibrium quantity rises (Q1 to Q2), a movement along the demand curve.

  3. (c) State one other cause of an increase in the demand for cars. [1]

  1. (a) Goods that are used together, so a change in the price of one changes the demand for the other in the opposite direction (joint demand).

  2. (b) Running a car becomes cheaper, so demand for cars rises (curve shifts right).

Q8Price changes · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Floods in Sindh destroy part of the mango crop. Explain the effect on the market for mangoes. [4]
  2. (b) At the same time, incomes in the country rise so the demand for mangoes increases. Explain why the equilibrium price of mangoes will definitely rise but the change in equilibrium quantity is uncertain. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Supply falls (curve shifts left), creating excess demand at the old price, so price rises and quantity bought and sold falls.
    1 mark: supply decreases/curve shifts left; 1 mark: fewer mangoes available at each price; 1 mark: price rises; 1 mark: equilibrium quantity falls (contraction along demand curve).
  2. (b) Both a fall in supply and a rise in demand push price up; the fall in supply lowers quantity while the rise in demand raises it, so the net effect depends on which shift is larger.
    1 mark: both shifts raise price; 1 mark: supply shift reduces quantity, demand shift increases it; 1 mark: final quantity depends on relative size of the two shifts.

  2. (b) Identify whether a shift in demand or a shift in supply best explains the change in price and quantity, and give one possible cause. [3]

Q10Price changes · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A medical report says that a high intake of sugar harms health. At the same time, the price of honey, a substitute for sugar, rises.

  1. (a) Explain the effect of each event on the demand for sugar. [4]
  2. (b) Discuss whether the equilibrium price of sugar will rise or fall. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Health report: tastes change, demand falls (shifts left). Rise in honey price: honey is a substitute so consumers switch to sugar, demand rises (shifts right).
    Health report: 1 mark demand curve shifts left, 1 mark consumers see sugar as harmful/tastes change. Honey: 1 mark demand for sugar shifts right, 1 mark sugar now relatively cheaper substitute.
  2. (b) It depends on which shift is larger: if the health effect is stronger, demand falls overall and price falls; if the substitution effect is stronger, price rises; if equal, price unchanged. Honey may be a weak substitute, so the health effect may dominate.
    1 mark: net effect depends on relative size of shifts; 1 mark: larger fall → price falls; 1 mark: larger rise → price rises; 1 mark: judgement, e.g. honey is much more expensive/used differently so is a weak substitute; or supply unchanged so price follows net demand.
Q11Price elasticity of demand · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define price elasticity of demand. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why the demand for salt is price inelastic. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the product's price.
    1 mark: responsiveness/sensitivity of quantity demanded; 1 mark: to a change in its own price (or the formula % change in Qd ÷ % change in price).
  2. (b) Few close substitutes; it takes a very small share of income; it is a necessity.
    1 mark each for two reasons + 1 mark each for explanation: no close substitutes so consumers keep buying; small proportion of income so a price rise is hardly noticed; necessity used in cooking every day; bought in small amounts infrequently.
Q12Price elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The price of a product rises from $8 to $10. The quantity demanded falls from 500 units to 450 units. What is the price elasticity of demand? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A −0.4
    % change in quantity demanded = −50/500 = −10 %; % change in price = 2/8 = +25 %; PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4. B inverts the formula (price change ÷ quantity change).
Q13Price elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A bus company in Karachi raises its fare from Rs 50 to Rs 60. The number of passengers falls from 12 000 to 9 000 per day.

  1. (a) Calculate the price elasticity of demand for bus travel. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the change in the company's daily total revenue. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why your answer to (b) is consistent with your answer to (a). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) −1.25
    M1 %ΔQd = (9000 − 12 000) ÷ 12 000 = −25 %; %ΔP = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 = +20 %; A1 −25 ÷ 20 = −1.25.
  2. (b) Falls by Rs 60 000 (from Rs 600 000 to Rs 540 000)
    M1 50 × 12 000 = 600 000 and 60 × 9 000 = 540 000; A1 fall of Rs 60 000.
  3. (c) Demand is price elastic (PED > 1 in size), so the percentage fall in passengers is greater than the percentage rise in fare and revenue falls.
    1 mark: demand is elastic; 1 mark: %ΔQd larger than %ΔP so total revenue falls when price rises.
Q14Price elasticity of demand · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A cinema estimates that the price elasticity of demand is −0.6 for weekend tickets and −1.8 for weekday tickets.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in the quantity demanded of weekday tickets if the cinema cuts the weekday price by 10 %. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether the cinema should cut the price of both weekday and weekend tickets. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) +18 %
    M1 %ΔQd = PED × %ΔP = −1.8 × (−10 %); A1 +18 %.
  2. (b) Cut weekday prices (elastic: revenue rises) but not weekend prices (inelastic: revenue falls); consider costs, capacity, competitors and accuracy of estimates.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: weekday demand elastic, 10 % cut raises Qd by 18 % so total revenue rises; weekend demand inelastic, cut raises Qd by only 6 % so revenue falls; may consider raising weekend prices instead. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: extra viewers raise snack sales; limited seats at weekends; estimates may be wrong or change; rivals may react; costs per extra viewer. Max 6.
Q15Demand · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define demand. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the difference between a movement along a demand curve and a shift of a demand curve. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The willingness and ability to buy a product at a given price over a period of time.
    1 mark: willingness and ability (effective demand); 1 mark: at a given price/in a given time period.
  2. (b) A movement (extension/contraction) is caused only by a change in the product's own price; a shift is caused by a change in a non-price factor such as income, tastes or the price of related goods.
    1 mark: movement caused by change in own price; 1 mark: extension when price falls/contraction when price rises; 1 mark: shift caused by change in other (non-price) factors; 1 mark: example, e.g. rise in income shifts demand right.
Q16Demand · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Three consumers make up the market for a snack bar. Their weekly demand is:Price $2: Ali 10, Bina 6, Chen 8Price $3: Ali 7, Bina 5, Chen 6Price $4: Ali 5, Bina 3, Chen 2

  1. (a) Calculate the market quantity demanded at a price of $3. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in the market quantity demanded when the price rises from $2 to $4. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two factors, other than price, that could shift the market demand curve for this snack to the right. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 18 units
    B1 7 + 5 + 6 = 18.
  2. (b) −58.3 %
    M1 market Qd at $2 = 24, at $4 = 10; A1 (10 − 24) ÷ 24 × 100 = −58.3 %.
  3. (c) e.g. a rise in consumers' incomes; a successful advertising campaign
    1 mark each for two factors + 1 mark each for explanation, any two of: higher incomes (normal good); advertising/change in tastes; rise in price of a substitute; fall in price of a complement; more consumers (population growth).
Q17Demand · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Identify two products for which demand is likely to rise as the population ages. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse how an ageing population could change the pattern of demand in an economy such as Japan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. health care/medicines and retirement homes

Q18Market failure · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define external cost. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two external costs that a new cement factory could create for a nearby village in Punjab. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A cost of an economic activity that falls on third parties who are not involved in it and is not paid by the producer or consumer.
    1 mark: cost to a third party; 1 mark: not paid for/not included in the price.
  2. (b) e.g. dust pollution causing breathing illnesses; heavy lorries causing noise and damaging roads
    1 mark each for two costs + 1 mark each for explanation: air/dust pollution harming health and raising medical bills; noise; damage to crops from dust reducing farm income; congestion and road damage from lorries; water use lowering wells.
Q19Market failure · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two characteristics of a public good. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why a market system might not provide street lighting. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Non-excludable: people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting. Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others.
    1 mark: non-excludable + 1 mark explanation; 1 mark: non-rival + 1 mark explanation.
  2. (b) Because of the free-rider problem, consumers would not pay, so private firms could not make a profit.
    1 mark: free-rider problem/people can benefit without paying; 1 mark: firms cannot charge so no profit, so it is not provided (missing market).
Q20Market failure · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A chemical plant has private costs of $12 million a year and causes external costs of $5 million a year. Its private benefits are $15 million a year and it creates external benefits of $1 million a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the social cost and the social benefit of the plant. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the plant would operate in a free market even though this may be a market failure. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest and explain one government policy that could correct this market failure. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Social cost $17 million; social benefit $16 million
    B1 social cost = private cost + external cost = 12 + 5 = $17m; B1 social benefit = 15 + 1 = $16m.
  2. (b) The owner compares only private benefit ($15m) with private cost ($12m) and makes a profit; but social cost ($17m) exceeds social benefit ($16m), so society is worse off and resources are misallocated.
    1 mark: private benefit > private cost so profitable; 1 mark: external costs ignored by the firm; 1 mark: social cost > social benefit so over-production/inefficient allocation.
  3. (c) e.g. a tax on pollution equal to the external cost
    1 mark: policy (pollution tax, regulation/emission limits, tradable permits, fines); up to 2 marks explaining how it makes the firm pay for or reduce the external cost.
Q21Market economic system · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In a market economic system, what mainly decides which goods are produced? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A consumer demand working through the price mechanism
    In a market system resources are allocated by the price mechanism: firms produce what consumers are willing and able to buy at a profit. B describes a planned economy.
Q22Market economic system · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a market economic system is better than a mixed economic system for a developing economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Balanced: market system gives incentives, choice and efficiency; but may under-provide public and merit goods, ignore external costs and create inequality, which a mixed system can address – though government failure is possible.
    Up to 4 marks for the market system: profit motive encourages efficiency and innovation; wide consumer choice; resources respond quickly to price signals; no costly bureaucracy. Up to 4 marks for the mixed system: government provides public goods (roads, street lights) and merit goods (education, health); corrects external costs; reduces inequality through taxes and benefits; protects consumers from monopolies. Evaluation (needed for top marks): depends on government efficiency/corruption, level of development, size of markets. Max 8.
Q23Price determination · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The table shows the daily market for notebooks in a town.Price (Rs): 40, 50, 60, 70, 80Quantity demanded: 900, 800, 700, 600, 500Quantity supplied: 300, 500, 700, 900, 1100

  1. (a) Identify the equilibrium price. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the excess demand at a price of Rs 50. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how the market would move to equilibrium if the price were Rs 50. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 60
    B1 Rs 60 (quantity demanded = quantity supplied = 700).
  2. (b) 300 units
    M1 800 − 500; A1 300.
  3. (c) Shortage → consumers compete/sellers raise price → quantity supplied extends, quantity demanded contracts until Rs 60.
    1 mark: shortage/excess demand at Rs 50; 1 mark: price is bid up; 1 mark: Qs rises and Qd falls until they are equal at Rs 60 (700 units).
Q24Price determination · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) At a price of $6, the quantity demanded of a product is 300 units and the quantity supplied is 500 units. What is the situation in this market? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A excess supply of 200 units; the price will tend to fall
    Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded by 500 − 300 = 200, so there is a surplus; sellers cut prices to clear stocks. B confuses surplus with shortage.

  1. (a) Which of the following is a macroeconomic issue? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A the national unemployment rate
    Macroeconomics studies the whole economy, e.g. national unemployment, inflation, growth. B, C and D concern individual markets or decision makers, which is microeconomics.
Q26Microeconomics and macroeconomics · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between microeconomics and macroeconomics. [2]
  2. (b) Identify three decision makers studied in microeconomics and give an example of a decision each makes. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Microeconomics studies individual markets and decision makers; macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole.
    1 mark: micro – individual consumers, workers, firms or markets; 1 mark: macro – whole economy, e.g. inflation, unemployment, economic growth.
  2. (b) e.g. households/consumers – what to buy; workers – which job to take; firms – what and how much to produce
    1 mark each for three decision makers with a matching decision: consumers/households (spending, saving), workers (occupation, hours), firms/producers (output, prices, methods), government (as a decision maker in a market, e.g. setting a tax on one product).
Q27Supply · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which of the following would cause a movement along the supply curve of wheat rather than a shift of the curve? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a rise in the price of wheat
    Only a change in the product's own price causes a movement (extension or contraction) along the supply curve. B, C and D change conditions of supply and shift the curve.
Q28Supply · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A farm's weekly supply of dates is 100 tonnes at $10 per kg, 200 tonnes at $20 per kg and 300 tonnes at $30 per kg.

  1. (a) New technology increases the quantity supplied by 25 % at every price. Calculate the new quantity supplied at $20 per kg. [1]
  2. (b) Farm workers' wages rise at the same time as the government introduces a subsidy for date farmers. Analyse the possible effect on the supply of dates. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 250 tonnes
    B1 200 × 1.25 = 250 tonnes.
  2. (b) Higher wages raise costs and shift supply left; the subsidy lowers costs and shifts supply right; the overall effect depends on the relative size of the two changes.
    Up to 2 marks: higher wages raise costs of production, supply shifts left/less supplied at each price. Up to 2 marks: subsidy reduces costs/is extra revenue, supply shifts right. Up to 2 marks: net effect depends on which is larger; with demand unchanged, price falls if supply rises overall and rises if supply falls.
Q29Price elasticity of supply · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The price of a product rises from $20 to $25 and the quantity supplied rises from 400 to 460 units. What is the price elasticity of supply? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 0.6
    % change in Qs = 60/400 = 15 %; % change in price = 5/20 = 25 %; PES = 15 ÷ 25 = 0.6 (inelastic). B inverts the formula.
Q30Price elasticity of supply · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two reasons why the supply of fresh tomatoes is price inelastic in the short run. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how the building of cold-storage warehouses could change the price elasticity of supply of tomatoes. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Tomatoes take months to grow so output cannot be increased quickly; they are perishable so stocks cannot be stored and released when the price rises.
    1 mark each for two reasons + 1 mark each for explanation: time needed to grow crops; perishable – no stocks; land and other resources already committed; farmers may lack spare capacity/greenhouses.
  2. (b) PES becomes more elastic because tomatoes can be stored and supplied to the market when prices rise.
    1 mark: supply more elastic; 1 mark: stocks can be held and released quickly in response to price changes.
