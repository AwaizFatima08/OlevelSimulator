Q7Effect of temperature & pH · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Why does the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction fall when the temperature is raised well above the optimum? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C The shape of the active site changes, so the substrate no longer fits.
    High temperature denatures the enzyme: the active site loses its shape and is no longer complementary to the substrate. Enzymes are proteins, not living things, so they cannot be 'killed' (A); faster movement actually increases collisions (B).

Q16Effect of temperature & pH · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A student put a protease solution in two tubes.Tube A was kept at 2 °C for 30 minutes, then warmed to 37 °C and mixed with protein.Tube B was kept at 80 °C for 30 minutes, then cooled to 37 °C and mixed with protein.Tube A digested the protein normally. Tube B digested none of the protein.

  1. (a) Explain the results for tube A and tube B. [4]
  2. (b) Seeds kept dry in a freezer in a seed bank can still germinate many years later, but seeds that have been boiled never germinate. Use your knowledge of enzymes to suggest why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Tube A: at 2 °C the enzyme had little kinetic energy, so there were few collisions and little activity; it was not denatured, so when warmed to 37 °C it worked normally. Tube B: at 80 °C the enzyme was denatured; the active site permanently changed shape, so substrate could not bind even after cooling.
    1 mark each for: low temperature – low kinetic energy / few collisions (A); enzyme not denatured at low temperature, so activity returns on warming; high temperature denatures enzyme (B); active-site shape changed permanently / irreversibly, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form when cooled.
  2. (b) Freezing only makes the seeds' enzymes inactive (low kinetic energy) without denaturing them, so they work again when the seed is warmed; boiling denatures the enzymes irreversibly (active sites change shape), so the reactions needed for germination cannot take place.
    1 mark each for: freezing slows / stops enzyme activity temporarily (not denatured, activity returns on warming); boiling denatures enzymes irreversibly / active site permanently changed, so no germination.

Show answer
  1. (a) The bacterial enzyme has a shape (bonds holding its 3-D shape) that remains stable at high temperature, so its active site keeps its shape; the human enzyme's shape changes above about 40–45 °C.
    1 mark for: bacterial enzyme's active site / shape stays the same at 75 °C; 1 mark for: human enzyme's active site changes shape / is denatured at this temperature.
  2. (b) At 37 °C molecules have less kinetic energy, so fewer collisions between enzyme and substrate and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes per second.
    1 mark each for: less kinetic energy; fewer collisions / fewer enzyme–substrate complexes.
  3. (c) It is not denatured, so it keeps working at high temperatures where reactions are faster / it can be used for longer.
    Any one: works at high temperature without denaturing; faster reaction; lasts longer / reused; fewer contaminating microorganisms grow at high temperature.

Yeast suspension (contains catalase) was added to hydrogen peroxide at five temperatures. The time taken to collect 10 cm³ of oxygen in a gas syringe was recorded.temperature / °C: 15, 25, 35, 45, 55time to collect 10 cm³ / s: 125, 64, 40, 52, 160

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of oxygen production at 25 °C and at 45 °C in cm³ per second. Give your answers to 2 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) The student concluded that the optimum temperature is 35 °C. Explain why this conclusion may not be correct and suggest what further work would show the optimum more precisely. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why measuring the time to collect 10 cm³ of gas is better than collecting gas for a fixed long time, such as 10 minutes, at every temperature. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 25 °C: 0.16 cm³/s; 45 °C: 0.19 cm³/s
    rate = 10 ÷ time: 10 ÷ 64 = 0.156 → 0.16 (B1); 10 ÷ 52 = 0.192 → 0.19 (B1).
  2. (b) The optimum could be anywhere between 25 °C and 45 °C, because only 10 °C intervals were tested; repeat with temperatures at 2 °C (or smaller) intervals between 25 °C and 45 °C, with repeats.
    1 mark: only 10 °C steps / large intervals; 1 mark: optimum lies between 25 °C and 45 °C; 1 mark: test more temperatures at smaller intervals in this range (with repeats).
  3. (c) Over a long time the hydrogen peroxide may be used up at the faster temperatures, so the volumes would no longer show the rate / the cylinder could overflow.
    B1 substrate used up / cylinder overflows so later volumes do not reflect rate.
Q20Effect of temperature & pH · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Slices of mango were kept for 5 days at two temperatures. The mass lost because of decay was measured.kept at 4 °C (refrigerator): mass lost 3.2 gkept at 30 °C (kitchen in summer): mass lost 14.4 g

  1. (a) Calculate how many times greater the mass loss was at 30 °C than at 4 °C. [1]
  2. (b) Explain the difference in mass loss, in terms of the enzymes of decomposers on the mango. [3]
  3. (c) Predict what would happen to the rate of decay if the mango slices kept at 4 °C were moved to 30 °C. Give a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.5 times
    14.4 ÷ 3.2 = 4.5 (B1).
  2. (b) At 4 °C the decomposers' enzymes have less kinetic energy, so fewer collisions and slower digestion and respiration; the decomposers grow and reproduce slowly. At 30 °C enzymes are near their optimum, so decay is faster.
    1 mark each for: decomposers (bacteria/fungi) use enzymes to digest the mango; low temperature – less kinetic energy / fewer enzyme–substrate collisions; enzymes not denatured but activity / growth / reproduction slower at 4 °C; nearer optimum at 30 °C (any three).
  3. (c) The rate would increase to about the same as at 30 °C, because the enzymes were not denatured at 4 °C.
    B1 rate increases; B1 enzymes not denatured (activity returns).

Q29Enzyme action & specificity · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Some people cannot digest lactose, the sugar in milk. A dairy company in Lahore makes lactose-free milk by adding the enzyme lactase, which breaks lactose into glucose and galactose.

  1. (a) Explain why lactase breaks down lactose but not sucrose, even though both are disaccharides. [2]
  2. (b) The company adds a small amount of lactase to a large tank of milk. Explain why only a small amount of enzyme is needed. [2]
  3. (c) A mutation in the gene for lactase changes one amino acid in the active site. Suggest how this could affect the enzyme. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Lactase's active site is complementary only to lactose; sucrose has a different shape so cannot bind / form an enzyme–substrate complex.
    B1 active site complementary to lactose (specific); B1 sucrose different shape, does not fit.
  2. (b) Enzymes are not used up / are unchanged at the end of the reaction, so each enzyme molecule can break down many lactose molecules.
    B1 not used up / unchanged; B1 reused many times.
  3. (c) The active site may change shape so lactose no longer fits; fewer or no enzyme–substrate complexes; lactose not broken down.
    1 mark: active site shape changed; 1 mark: lactose no longer binds / enzyme does not work (or works more slowly).

  1. (a) Calculate the initial rate of reaction over the first 2 minutes in mg per minute. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why no more product forms after 5 minutes. [2]

Show answer
  1. (a) 8 mg per minute
    16 ÷ 2 = 8 mg/min (B1).
