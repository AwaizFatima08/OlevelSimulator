Q12Breathing mechanism & air composition · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A piece of food blocks the trachea of a man eating at a wedding. A first-aider stands behind him and makes a sudden, strong upward push just below his ribs, under the diaphragm. Explain, in terms of volume and pressure, how this can force the food out. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the man cannot breathe in while the food completely blocks his trachea, even though his diaphragm still contracts. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The push forces the diaphragm upwards, which suddenly reduces the volume of the thorax (lungs); the pressure of the air in the lungs rises sharply above atmospheric; the air rushes out through the trachea and pushes the food out.
    B1 diaphragm pushed up; B1 thorax / lung volume decreases; B1 pressure increases so air forced out carrying the food.
  2. (b) The volume of the thorax increases and the pressure falls, but no air can flow in through the blocked trachea, so the lungs cannot fill; the trachea is the only route for air to the lungs.
    B1 pressure falls but air cannot enter; B1 trachea is the only pathway to the lungs / no air reaches alveoli.

Q17Breathing mechanism & air composition · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what happens to breathing if the external intercostal muscles were paralysed but the diaphragm still worked. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why a puncture wound through the chest wall into the space around one lung (pleural cavity) can cause that lung to collapse. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Breathing in would still occur, by contraction of the diaphragm, but the ribs would not move up and out, so the increase in thoracic volume (and the volume of air inhaled) would be smaller.
    B1 diaphragm alone can still increase volume; B1 smaller volume change / shallower breaths.
  2. (b) Air enters the pleural cavity, so the pressure around the lung becomes equal to atmospheric pressure; when the thorax expands the pressure in the lung no longer falls below atmospheric, so the lung does not inflate and collapses (elastic recoil).
    B1 air enters / pressure equalises; B1 pressure in the lung cannot be reduced so no air enters / lung recoils.

Q19Breathing mechanism & air composition · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) In normal breathing, describe how air is made to enter the lungs. [2]
  2. (b) A patient whose breathing muscles are paralysed is put on a ventilator. The machine pushes air into the trachea through a tube at a pressure higher than atmospheric. Compare how air enters the lungs with the ventilator and in normal breathing. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest how the patient breathes out when the ventilator stops pushing air in. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract, increasing the volume of the thorax, so the pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric and air is drawn in.
    B1 muscles contract / thorax volume increases; B1 pressure falls below atmospheric so air enters.
  2. (b) Ventilator: pressure in the airway is raised above atmospheric so air is pushed in and the lungs and chest expand as a result. Normal breathing: the chest expands first and the pressure in the lungs is lowered below atmospheric, so air is pulled in. In both, air moves from higher to lower pressure.
    B1 ventilator raises pressure (positive pressure) / pushes air in; B1 normal breathing lowers pressure in thorax (air drawn in); B1 in both, air flows from higher to lower pressure / volume change of lungs in both.
  3. (c) The stretched lungs and chest wall recoil (elastic recoil), reducing the volume and raising the pressure, so air leaves.
    B1 elastic recoil of lungs/chest decreases volume and raises pressure.

  1. (a) During hard exercise the athlete absorbs 3.0 dm³ of oxygen per minute. Each dm³ of air breathed in gives 0.05 dm³ of oxygen to the blood. Calculate the volume of air the athlete must breathe each minute. [2]
  2. (b) Each breath has a volume of 2.0 dm³. Calculate the breathing rate needed. [1]
  3. (c) At rest the athlete absorbs 0.25 dm³ of oxygen per minute. Calculate how many times more oxygen is absorbed per minute during hard exercise. [1]
  4. (d) Explain why the muscles need so much more oxygen during hard exercise. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 60 dm³ per minute
    M1 3.0 ÷ 0.05; A1 60.
  2. (b) 30 breaths per minute
    B1 60 ÷ 2.0 = 30.
  3. (c) 12 times
    B1 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12.
  4. (d) The muscles contract more and need much more energy; this is released by aerobic respiration, which uses oxygen.
    B1 more energy needed for muscle contraction; B1 aerobic respiration uses oxygen.
Q60Exercise & control of breathing · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A student breathes in and out of a paper bag for one minute while sitting still. The percentage of carbon dioxide in the air in the bag rises. Explain why the student's breathing rate and depth increase, even though the student is not exercising. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why this experiment should be stopped after a short time. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The air breathed in from the bag has a higher carbon dioxide concentration, so the concentration gradient between the blood and the alveoli is smaller; less carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood, so its concentration in the blood rises; this is detected by the brain, which sends impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles to increase the rate and depth of breathing.
    B1 more CO₂ in inspired air; B1 smaller gradient / less CO₂ diffuses out of blood; B1 blood CO₂ rises and is detected by brain; B1 brain stimulates breathing muscles (diaphragm/intercostals) → faster, deeper breathing.
  2. (b) Carbon dioxide would keep rising and oxygen falling in the bag, which could make the student dizzy or ill.
    B1 CO₂ builds up / O₂ falls → unsafe.
