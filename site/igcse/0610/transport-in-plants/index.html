Q1Transpiration · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) What is transpiration?A the uptake of water by root hair cellsB the loss of water vapour from leavesC the movement of sucrose through the phloemD the evaporation of water from the soil surface [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B the loss of water vapour from leaves
    Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the leaves (water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces and diffuses out through the stomata). A is water uptake; C is translocation.
Q2Transpiration · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the pathway taken by water from the soil to the air around a leaf. Name the cells or tissues it passes through. [4]
  2. (b) State the name given to the loss of water vapour from leaves. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water enters root hair cells (by osmosis), passes through the root cortex cells to the xylem, moves up the xylem in the root and stem into the leaf veins, passes into mesophyll cells, evaporates from their surfaces into the air spaces and diffuses out through the stomata.
    1 mark each for any four in correct order: root hair cell; root cortex; xylem (root → stem → leaf); mesophyll cells; evaporation into air spaces; diffusion out through stomata.
  2. (b) Transpiration.
    B1.
Q3Transpiration · Core · Paper 6 style · 7 marks

A potted cotton plant was weighed every 2 hours during a sunny day in Faisalabad, and the rate of water loss was calculated.Time: 06:00, 08:00, 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, 16:00, 18:00Rate of water loss (g per hour): 0.4, 1.6, 3.2, 4.1, 3.6, 1.8, 0.5

  1. (a) State the time at which the rate of water loss was highest. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean rate of water loss for the seven readings. Give your answer to two decimal places. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two reasons why the rate was low at 06:00. [2]
  4. (d) State the most suitable type of graph to display these results and how the axes should be arranged. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 12:00 (4.1 g per hour).
    B1.
  2. (b) 2.17 g per hour
    M1: (0.4 + 1.6 + 3.2 + 4.1 + 3.6 + 1.8 + 0.5) ÷ 7 = 15.2 ÷ 7; A1: 2.17 g/h.
  3. (c) Low temperature; low light so stomata were (partly) closed; higher humidity in early morning.
    1 mark each for any two.
  4. (d) A line graph with time on the x-axis and rate of water loss (g per hour) on the y-axis.
    1 mark: line graph; 1 mark: time on x-axis, rate on y-axis with units.
Q4Transpiration · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Under which set of conditions will a well-watered maize plant with open stomata lose water vapour fastest?A 15 °C, humid air, still airB 15 °C, dry air, windyC 35 °C, humid air, still airD 35 °C, dry air, windy [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) D 35 °C, dry air, windy
    High temperature increases evaporation and diffusion; dry air gives a steep water vapour gradient; wind removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping the gradient steep. C has a high temperature but humid, still air reduces the gradient.
Q5Transpiration · Standard · Paper 6 style · 10 marks

A leafy shoot of a guava plant was set up in a potometer. The air bubble in the capillary tube moved 36 mm in 5 minutes in still air. A fan was then switched on and the bubble moved 62 mm in 5 minutes. The internal radius of the capillary tube is 0.5 mm.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of water taken up in still air in 5 minutes. Use the formula volume = πr²l, where r is the internal radius of the tube and l the distance moved. Give your answer in mm³ to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate of water uptake in still air in mm³ per minute. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage increase in the distance moved when the fan was switched on. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why the fan increased the rate of water uptake. [3]
  5. (e) State two precautions when setting up a potometer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 28.3 mm³
    M1: π × 0.5² × 36; A1: 28.3 mm³.
  2. (b) 5.7 mm³/min
    28.27 ÷ 5 = 5.7 mm³/min (allow follow-through).
  3. (c) 72 %
    (62 − 36) ÷ 36 × 100 = 72 %.
  4. (d) Moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf surface, keeping a steep water vapour concentration (diffusion) gradient between the air spaces and the outside air; more water vapour diffuses out through the stomata, so more water is pulled up the xylem (transpiration pull) and taken up from the potometer.
    1 mark each: wind removes water vapour near the leaf; steeper diffusion gradient; faster loss through stomata / more transpiration pull.
  5. (e) Cut the shoot under water; make all joints airtight (e.g. with petroleum jelly); dry the leaves; let the shoot settle for a few minutes before taking readings.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q6Transpiration · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the rate of transpiration increases when the temperature rises. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the rate of transpiration is lower on a humid day. [2]
  3. (c) State the feature of the leaf interior that provides a large surface for evaporation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water molecules gain more kinetic energy, so more water evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells; water vapour diffuses faster out through the stomata; warm air can also hold more water vapour, so the diffusion gradient is steeper.
    1 mark each for any three: more kinetic energy; faster evaporation from mesophyll surfaces; faster diffusion of water vapour through stomata; steeper gradient.
  2. (b) Humid air contains more water vapour, so the concentration gradient of water vapour between the air spaces in the leaf and the air outside is smaller; less water vapour diffuses out.
    1 mark: more water vapour in the air outside; 1 mark: smaller diffusion gradient, slower diffusion out of stomata.
  3. (c) The large internal surface area of the mesophyll cells facing the interconnecting air spaces.
    B1.
Q7Transpiration · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Plan an investigation to find out how wind speed affects the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. You are provided with a potometer, an electric fan and normal laboratory apparatus.In your plan, include: what you will vary and how; what you will measure; the variables you will keep constant; how you will make the results reliable. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Set up a potometer with a leafy shoot cut under water and airtight joints; vary wind speed by placing the fan at different distances (e.g. 25, 50, 75, 100 cm) or using fan speed settings, plus a no-fan control; measure the distance the bubble moves in a fixed time (e.g. 5 min) and calculate the rate; keep the same shoot, temperature, light intensity and humidity constant; allow 5 minutes to settle at each speed; repeat three times at each speed and calculate a mean.
    1 mark each, max 6: independent variable – wind speed changed by fan distance/setting with at least 4 values; dependent variable – distance moved by bubble (or volume) in a stated time; rate = distance ÷ time; two controlled variables (e.g. temperature, light intensity, same shoot/leaf area, humidity); acclimatisation time before readings; reset the bubble with the reservoir; repeats and mean; safety/precaution such as airtight seals or cutting under water.
Q8Transpiration · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) On a hot afternoon in Multan the leaves of a pumpkin plant droop, even though the soil is moist. By evening they have recovered. Explain why the leaves droop. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one advantage to the plant of wilting. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the leaves recover in the evening. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The rate of transpiration is greater than the rate of water uptake by the roots; the leaf cells lose water and become flaccid (lose turgor pressure); so the leaves are no longer supported and wilt.
    1 mark each: water loss faster than uptake; cells lose turgor / become flaccid; loss of support → wilting.
  2. (b) The leaves hang down so less surface faces the sun / less heat absorbed, reducing further water loss.
    B1.
  3. (c) Lower temperature and light, so transpiration falls (stomata close); water uptake now exceeds loss so cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid again.
    1 mark: less transpiration in evening; 1 mark: cells regain water / become turgid.
Q9Transpiration · Standard · Paper 6 style · 6 marks

Blue cobalt chloride paper turns pink when it absorbs water. A student clipped one dry square of cobalt chloride paper to the upper surface and one to the lower surface of a leaf on a hibiscus plant, and timed how long each took to turn pink.Upper surface: 240 sLower surface: 40 s

  1. (a) Calculate how many times faster the paper on the lower surface changed colour than the paper on the upper surface. [1]
  2. (b) Explain what the results show about the two leaf surfaces. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the cobalt chloride paper must be kept dry and handled with forceps. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest one way to improve the reliability of the results. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6 times
    240 ÷ 40 = 6.
  2. (b) The lower surface loses water vapour faster, so it has more stomata than the upper surface.
    1 mark: more water lost from lower surface; 1 mark: more stomata on lower surface.
  3. (c) Any moisture from the air or from fingers would turn the paper pink before the experiment starts, giving a false result.
    1 mark: paper is sensitive to any water; 1 mark: fingers/air moisture would change colour → invalid result.
  4. (d) Repeat on several leaves of the same plant and calculate means.
    B1.
Q10Transpiration · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A potometer is used to estimate the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. Why does it not measure the rate of transpiration exactly?A Some of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells, so uptake is not exactly equal to loss.B Water vapour lost from the leaves is absorbed back into the capillary tube.C The potometer measures the movement of sucrose instead of water.D Water can only enter the shoot by osmosis when the stomata are closed. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Some of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells, so uptake is not exactly equal to loss.
    A potometer measures water uptake. A small proportion of the water absorbed is used (e.g. in photosynthesis) or stays in cells to keep them turgid, so uptake is only approximately equal to water lost. B, C and D are not true.
Q11Transpiration · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how water moves up the xylem of a tall tree such as a shisham tree. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why the trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower in diameter at midday than at night. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out of the leaf; this creates a transpiration pull (tension) on the water in the xylem; water molecules are attracted to each other (cohesion), so a continuous column of water is pulled up from the roots; the xylem vessels are narrow, continuous, hollow tubes.
    1 mark each for any four: evaporation/transpiration from leaf; creates transpiration pull / tension; cohesion between water molecules; continuous column of water; pulled up through xylem (continuous hollow tubes) from roots.
  2. (b) At midday the transpiration rate is highest, so the tension in the xylem is greatest; the walls of the xylem are pulled inwards, slightly reducing the diameter.
    1 mark: high transpiration at midday; 1 mark: greater tension/pull on xylem walls draws them inwards.
Q12Transpiration · Challenge · Paper 6 style · 8 marks

Four similar leafy shoots of a bean plant were treated as follows and hung up in the same room for 48 hours.A: no petroleum jelly; B: jelly on upper leaf surfaces; C: jelly on lower leaf surfaces; D: jelly on both surfaces.Shoot | initial mass (g) | mass after 48 h (g)A | 25.0 | 21.2B | 24.6 | 21.3C | 25.4 | 24.6D | 24.8 | 24.5

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage loss in mass for each shoot. Give your answers to one decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) What do the results suggest about where the stomata are found on these leaves? Explain your answer. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why shoot D still lost a small amount of mass. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest how the investigation could be made more reliable. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A: 15.2 %; B: 13.4 %; C: 3.1 %; D: 1.2 %
    (initial − final) ÷ initial × 100. 3 marks all four correct; 2 marks three correct; 1 mark two correct.
  2. (b) Most stomata are on the lower surface: covering the lower surface (C) reduced mass loss far more than covering the upper surface (B), whose loss was almost the same as uncovered shoot A; the jelly blocks the stomata so water vapour cannot diffuse out.
    1 mark: most stomata on lower surface; 1 mark: comparison using data B vs C (or A vs C); 1 mark: jelly blocks stomata / prevents diffusion of water vapour.
  3. (c) Water vapour was lost through the stem or through the jelly / cuticle, or the jelly did not cover the leaves completely.
    B1 for any valid suggestion.
  4. (d) Repeat with several shoots for each treatment and calculate mean percentages.
    B1.
Q13Transpiration · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A researcher measured the rate of water loss from a leafy shoot of a mulberry tree in still air and in moving air.Still air: 2.4 g per hourMoving air: 4.2 g per hour

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in the rate of water loss in moving air compared with still air. [2]
  2. (b) The researcher then covered the shoot with a clear plastic bag in moving air, and the rate fell to 0.6 g per hour. Explain this result. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why plants in very windy places, such as the northern mountain passes, often have small leaves. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 75 %
    M1: (4.2 − 2.4) ÷ 2.4 × 100; A1: 75 %.
  2. (b) Water vapour collects inside the bag, so the air around the leaf becomes very humid / saturated; the bag also prevents wind removing water vapour; the diffusion gradient between leaf air spaces and the air is very small, so little water vapour diffuses out.
    1 mark each: humidity in bag rises; wind cannot remove water vapour; small diffusion gradient → slow loss.
  3. (c) Smaller leaf surface area means fewer stomata exposed and less water vapour lost, which reduces the risk of wilting when wind increases transpiration.
    1 mark: smaller surface area / fewer stomata; 1 mark: reduces water loss by transpiration.
Q14Translocation · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which substances are transported in the phloem?A water and mineral ionsB sucrose and amino acidsC starch and glucoseD oxygen and carbon dioxide [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B sucrose and amino acids
    Phloem translocates sucrose and amino acids from sources to sinks. Water and mineral ions are carried in the xylem (A). Starch is insoluble and is not transported.
Q15Translocation · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define translocation. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by a source and a sink, and give one example of each in a flowering plant. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem from sources to sinks.
    1 mark: movement of sucrose and amino acids; 1 mark: in phloem from source(s) to sink(s).
  2. (b) Source: a part of the plant that releases sucrose or amino acids, e.g. a photosynthesising leaf (or a storage organ when its store is used). Sink: a part that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids, e.g. root, growing shoot tip, developing fruit or seed, storage organ.
    1 mark: definition of source; 1 mark: example; 1 mark: definition of sink; 1 mark: example.
Q16Translocation · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A pea plant is forming pods with developing seeds. State whether the developing seeds are a source or a sink, and explain why. [2]
  2. (b) Name the insoluble carbohydrate that the developing seeds make from sucrose for storage. [1]
  3. (c) When one of these seeds germinates, its stored food supplies the growing shoot and root before the new leaves can photosynthesise. Explain whether the seed's food store is now a source or a sink. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sink; they receive sucrose and amino acids from the leaves through the phloem and use them for growth or store them.
    1 mark: sink; 1 mark: receive (import) sucrose/amino acids via phloem for growth or storage.
  2. (b) Starch.
    B1.
  3. (c) Source; the stored starch is broken down to sugar, which is moved as sucrose in the phloem to the growing shoot and root (the sinks).
    1 mark: source; 1 mark: starch broken down and sucrose translocated to growing shoot/root (sinks).
Q17Translocation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In early spring, before any leaves have grown, the buds of a potato plant start to grow using food stored in the tuber. During this time, which statement is correct?A The tuber is a source and the growing shoots are sinks.B The tuber is a sink and the growing shoots are sources.C The tuber and the shoots are both sinks.D Sucrose moves from the shoots to the tuber in the xylem. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A The tuber is a source and the growing shoots are sinks.
    Stored starch in the tuber is converted to sucrose and sent in the phloem to the growing shoots, so the tuber is a source and the shoots are sinks. In summer the tuber becomes a sink (B describes summer, not spring). Sucrose moves in phloem, not xylem (D).
Q18Translocation · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) A farmer removed a complete ring of bark (including the phloem) from one branch of a mango tree, without damaging the xylem. After some weeks the bark just above the ring swelled. Explain why. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the leaves on the ringed branch did not wilt. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the fruits on the ringed branch grew larger than fruits on other branches. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Removing the bark removed the phloem, so sucrose made in the leaves could not be translocated past the ring down the branch; sucrose and other substances built up above the ring, causing swelling (extra growth).
    1 mark each: phloem removed / cut; sucrose from leaves cannot pass the ring; accumulates above ring → swelling.
  2. (b) The xylem was not damaged, so water and mineral ions could still travel up to the leaves.
    1 mark: xylem intact; 1 mark: water still reaches leaves.
  3. (c) Sucrose made by the leaves of that branch could not move down to the roots or trunk, so more was available to the fruits (sinks) on that branch.
    1 mark: sucrose kept in that branch; 1 mark: more sucrose to the fruits / fruits as sinks receive more.
Q19Translocation · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Aphids feed by pushing a fine mouthpart (stylet) into a plant stem. The liquid they drink contains a high concentration of sugar. Name the tissue the stylet enters and the sugar it contains. [2]
  2. (b) Aphid colonies on a young tomato plant cause the fruits to stay small. Explain why. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why aphids are often found feeding on the young growing tips of plants. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Phloem; sucrose.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) The aphids remove sucrose (and amino acids) from the phloem, so less reaches the fruits, which are sinks; less energy/raw material for growth.
    1 mark: aphids take sucrose/amino acids from phloem; 1 mark: less reaches fruits (sinks) for growth.
  3. (c) The phloem there carries sucrose and amino acids towards the growing tip (a sink), so the sap is rich in nutrients / tissues are soft.
    B1.
Q20Translocation · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) In sugarcane, sucrose is stored in the stem. Describe how sucrose made in a leaf reaches the stem. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why plants transport carbohydrate as sucrose rather than as starch. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why sugarcane fields need a supply of nitrate ions as well as sunlight. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Glucose is made by photosynthesis in the mesophyll cells and converted to sucrose; sucrose enters the phloem in the leaf veins; it is translocated through the phloem to the stem, which acts as a sink (store).
    1 mark each: made in leaf by photosynthesis (glucose → sucrose); loaded into phloem; translocated to stem (sink).
  2. (b) Sucrose is soluble so it can be carried in solution in the phloem sap; starch is insoluble and cannot be transported.
    1 mark: sucrose soluble; 1 mark: starch insoluble so cannot move in phloem.
  3. (c) Nitrate is needed to make amino acids (and proteins) for growth.
    B1.
Q21Translocation · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

One mature leaf of a young bean plant was enclosed in a clear bag containing carbon dioxide made with radioactive carbon. The plant was left in light for 6 hours, then parts of the plant were tested for radioactivity.

  1. (a) Name the tissue in which the radioactive substance was found in the stem. [1]
  2. (b) Name the radioactive substance most likely to be carried in this tissue, and explain how it was formed. [2]
  3. (c) Radioactivity was found both in the young leaves at the top of the plant and in the roots. Explain why the substance moved both upwards and downwards. [3]
  4. (d) Suggest why no radioactivity was found in the mature leaves below the treated leaf. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Phloem.
    B1.
  2. (b) Sucrose; the leaf used the radioactive carbon dioxide in photosynthesis to make glucose, which was converted to sucrose for transport.
    1 mark: sucrose; 1 mark: from radioactive CO₂ fixed in photosynthesis → glucose → sucrose.
  3. (c) Translocation moves sucrose from a source (the mature leaf) to sinks; sinks are found both above (growing shoot tip / young leaves) and below (roots); so phloem can carry substances in either direction depending on where the sinks are.
    1 mark each: mature leaf is a source; young leaves and roots are sinks; phloem transports in both directions, to wherever sinks are.
  4. (d) These mature leaves are themselves sources (photosynthesising), not sinks, so they do not import sucrose.
    B1.
Q22Translocation · Challenge · Paper 6 style · 7 marks

Radioactive carbon dioxide was supplied to a leaf near the top of a sunflower plant. A detector found that the radioactive sugar had travelled 72 cm down the stem in 1.5 hours.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean speed of translocation in cm per hour. [1]
  2. (b) Convert your answer to mm per minute. [2]
  3. (c) The experiment was repeated at night in darkness. The radioactivity travelled only 30 cm in 1.5 hours. Suggest why translocation was slower. [2]
  4. (d) State one hazard of using radioactive substances and one precaution. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 48 cm per hour
    72 ÷ 1.5 = 48 cm/h.
  2. (b) 8 mm per minute
    M1: 480 mm per hour; A1: 480 ÷ 60 = 8 mm/min.
  3. (c) Less photosynthesis in darkness, so less sucrose produced in the leaf / smaller concentration difference between source and sink; lower temperature at night slows the process.
    1 mark each for any two valid suggestions; one must refer to less sucrose from the source.
  4. (d) Hazard: ionising radiation can damage cells / DNA. Precaution: wear gloves, use forceps, keep exposure time short, dispose of material safely.
    1 mark each.
Q23Xylem & phloem structure · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which is a function of xylem?A transport of sucrose and amino acidsB transport of water and mineral ions and supportC absorption of light for photosynthesisD gas exchange with the atmosphere [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B transport of water and mineral ions and support
    Xylem transports water and mineral ions and its lignified walls help support the plant. Sucrose and amino acids travel in phloem (A).
Q24Xylem & phloem structure · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State the positions of xylem and phloem in a transverse section of a young dicotyledonous stem. [2]
  2. (b) State the positions of xylem and phloem in a transverse section of a root. [2]
  3. (c) In a leaf vein, state which tissue is nearer the upper surface. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Vascular bundles in a ring near the outside of the stem; in each bundle the xylem is towards the inside (centre) and the phloem towards the outside.
    1 mark: bundles in a ring; 1 mark: xylem inner, phloem outer.
  2. (b) Xylem in the centre of the root (in a star or X shape); phloem in groups between the arms of the xylem.
    1 mark: xylem central; 1 mark: phloem between xylem arms.
  3. (c) Xylem.
    B1.
Q25Xylem & phloem structure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe and explain three ways in which the structure of xylem vessels is related to their function. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) No cross walls (end walls broken down) – a continuous tube so water flows without obstruction; no cell contents (dead, hollow) – leaves a clear lumen for water; thick walls strengthened with lignin – support the plant and stop vessels collapsing under tension; narrow tubes – help maintain a continuous water column.
    For each of three features: 1 mark feature, 1 mark explanation. Features: no cross walls; no cytoplasm / hollow / dead; lignified thick walls; narrow continuous tubes; pits allow sideways movement.
Q26Xylem & phloem structure · Standard · Paper 6 style · 8 marks

A celery stalk with leaves was stood in water containing red dye for 2 hours. A thin transverse slice was then cut. Red dots could be seen arranged in a ring near the outside of the slice. A student drew the slice; the width of the drawing is 84 mm and the actual width of the slice is 14 mm.

  1. (a) Name the tissue that was stained red and explain why. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the magnification of the drawing. Show your working. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the dye moved up faster in the celery stalk that still had its leaves than in a stalk with the leaves removed. [2]
  4. (d) State two features of a good biological drawing of this section. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Xylem; the dye solution was drawn up the stalk in the xylem with the water by transpiration pull.
    1 mark: xylem; 1 mark: water (and dye) carried up in xylem.
  2. (b) ×6
    M1: magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size = 84 ÷ 14; A1: ×6.
  3. (c) Leaves carry out transpiration; the loss of water vapour from leaves creates the transpiration pull that draws water and dye up the xylem; without leaves there is little pull.
    1 mark: leaves transpire; 1 mark: transpiration pull drives movement up xylem.
  4. (d) Any two of: clear continuous outlines with no shading; correct proportions; large (uses most of the space); labelled with ruled label lines that do not cross.
    1 mark each.
Q27Xylem & phloem structure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A student says 'xylem vessels are living cells that pump water up the stem'. Explain why this statement is wrong. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the lignin in xylem walls is important for a tall tree. [2]
  3. (c) State one difference between the contents of xylem and phloem. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mature xylem vessels are dead – they have no cytoplasm or cell contents; they cannot pump; water is pulled up by transpiration pull (with cohesion between water molecules), not pumped.
    1 mark each: xylem vessels are dead / hollow; no contents to do work / no pumping; water moved by transpiration pull and cohesion.
  2. (b) Lignin makes the walls strong and rigid, giving support to the stem/trunk and stopping the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension of the transpiration pull.
    1 mark: support/strength for the plant; 1 mark: prevents collapse under tension.
  3. (c) Xylem carries water and mineral ions; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids (in solution).
    B1.
Q28Water uptake & root hairs · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how water enters a root hair cell from the soil. [2]
  2. (b) Describe the route taken by water from the root hair cell to the leaf. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) By osmosis: the soil water has a higher water potential than the cell sap, so water moves in through the partially permeable cell membrane.
    1 mark: osmosis; 1 mark: from higher water potential (soil) to lower (cell) through partially permeable membrane.
  2. (b) Through the root cortex cells; into the xylem in the centre of the root; up the xylem in the stem to the leaf; into mesophyll cells.
    1 mark each for any three in order: root cortex; xylem; stem xylem to leaf; mesophyll cells.
Q29Water uptake & root hairs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Why is it an advantage for a root hair cell to have a long, thin extension?A It increases the surface area for absorption of water and mineral ions.B It contains chloroplasts for photosynthesis in the soil.C It reduces the distance that sucrose has to travel to the leaves.D It lets the root hair cell take in water by active transport. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A It increases the surface area for absorption of water and mineral ions.
    The extension greatly increases the surface area in contact with soil water, so more water (by osmosis) and ions (by active transport) can be absorbed. Root hair cells have no chloroplasts (B), and water enters by osmosis, not active transport (D).
Q30Water uptake & root hairs · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Chilli seedlings were pulled from a nursery bed and replanted in a field. Many of them wilted for several days. Explain why. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest two things the farmer could do to reduce wilting after transplanting. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the seedlings recover after about a week. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pulling up the seedlings damaged or tore off many root hairs; the surface area for water absorption was reduced; transpiration from the leaves continued, so water loss was greater than uptake and the cells lost turgor.
    1 mark each: root hairs damaged/lost; less surface area for absorption; water loss by transpiration exceeds uptake → wilting.
  2. (b) Lift seedlings with soil around the roots; transplant in the evening or on a cool, cloudy day; remove some leaves; water the field well; give shade for the first few days.
    1 mark each for any two, each linked to protecting root hairs or reducing transpiration.
  3. (c) New root hairs grow, restoring the surface area for water uptake.
    B1.
