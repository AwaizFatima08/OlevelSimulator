  1. (a) Name the river on which each of these dams is built: Tarbela; Mangla. [2]
  2. (b) State two purposes of large dams in Pakistan, other than storing water for irrigation. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why Pakistan needs more water storage. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Tarbela: Indus; Mangla: Jhelum
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Generating hydroelectricity; flood control.
    1 mark each for any two: hydroelectricity; flood control; drinking water for cities; fishing; tourism/recreation.
  3. (c) Most river flow comes in the summer months from snowmelt and the monsoon, so without storage much of it flows to the sea and there is a shortage in winter for rabi crops; existing reservoirs are losing capacity as they fill with silt, while the growing population needs more water for food, homes and industry.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to'). Reasons: seasonal flow (summer peak, winter shortage); low storage compared with annual flow (only about a month of average flow); siltation of Tarbela and Mangla; rising population and demand; drought years; protection against floods.
Q2Water supply, shortage and pollution · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A water-testing survey of 250 drinking-water samples from hand pumps and wells in rural districts found that 155 samples were unsafe because of bacteria or harmful chemicals (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of samples that were unsafe. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the number of samples that were safe. [1]
  3. (c) Name two diseases that are spread by polluted drinking water. [2]
  4. (d) Explain two ways of providing safer drinking water in rural areas of Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 62%
    155 ÷ 250 × 100 = 62%.
  2. (b) 95
    250 − 155 = 95.
  3. (c) Cholera and typhoid
    1 mark each for any two: cholera; typhoid; diarrhoea / dysentery; hepatitis A or E; gastroenteritis.
  4. (d) Community water filtration plants can be set up in villages, which remove bacteria and harmful chemicals so that people collect clean water instead of using ponds or canals; hand pumps and tubewells can be sited away from latrines and drains and made deeper, so that sewage does not seep into the water people drink.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to'). Ways: filtration plants; chlorination / boiling and awareness campaigns; deeper tubewells away from sewage; piped supply from protected sources; covered storage tanks; regular water testing; improved sanitation and drainage.

Q8Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A city has a population of 3.2 million. Its planners assume that each person needs 150 litres of water a day. The water board supplies 330 million litres a day (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Calculate the city's total daily water demand in million litres. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the daily shortfall. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage of demand that is supplied. Give your answer to one decimal place. [1]
  4. (d) Explain two ways in which demand for water in the city could be reduced. You should develop your answer. [4]
  5. (e) Suggest one way in which the city's supply could be increased. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 480 million litres per day
    3.2 million × 150 L = 480 million litres per day.
  2. (b) 150 million litres per day
    480 − 330 = 150 million litres per day.
  3. (c) 68.8%
    330 ÷ 480 × 100 = 68.75% ≈ 68.8%.
  4. (d) Fitting water meters and charging by the amount used would encourage households and businesses to waste less, because higher use would cost them more; repairing leaking pipes and taps and running awareness campaigns would cut waste, and industries could be required to recycle their process water.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to'). Ways: metering and charging by volume; awareness campaigns; repairing leaks in homes; water-saving taps and fittings; recycling water in industry; reusing treated waste water for parks; stopping illegal connections.
  5. (e) Build rainwater harvesting systems on roofs.
    1 mark for any one: new pipeline from a river or reservoir; rainwater harvesting; new tubewells (if aquifer allows); repairing leaks in mains; treating and reusing waste water; desalination (coastal city).
Q9Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) is a measure of organic pollution in water: the higher the BOD, the more polluted the water. Readings along a river passing a large city (illustrative figures):Upstream of the city: 4 mg/L; where the city's main drain enters: 68 mg/L; 20 km downstream: 41 mg/L; 60 km downstream: 22 mg/L.In this question, water with a BOD above 6 mg/L is taken as unsuitable for fish.

  1. (a) Calculate the increase in BOD from upstream of the city to the point where the city's drain enters the river. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage fall in BOD between the drain outlet and 60 km downstream. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Using the data, state at which sites the water is unsuitable for fish. [1]
  4. (d) Explain two effects of this pollution on people living downstream of the city. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 64 mg/L
    68 − 4 = 64 mg/L.
  2. (b) 67.6%
    68 − 22 = 46 (M1); 46 ÷ 68 × 100 = 67.6% (A1).
  3. (c) At the drain outlet, 20 km downstream and 60 km downstream
    1 mark: all three sites below the city (BOD above 6 mg/L); only the upstream site is suitable.
  4. (d) Fish die or become unsafe to eat, so fishermen lose their income and families lose a cheap source of protein; farmers who irrigate vegetables with the polluted water may spread disease to consumers, and people who drink or bathe in the water can catch diarrhoea, typhoid or skin infections.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to'). Effects: loss of fish and fishermen's income; contaminated crops irrigated with waste water; water-borne diseases; polluted groundwater near the river; cost of treating drinking water; bad smells and loss of recreation.
Q10Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 9 marks

Mean monthly flow of a large river in the north of Pakistan (illustrative figures, billion m³):Jan 2.1, Feb 2.0, Mar 2.8, Apr 4.5, May 8.6, Jun 13.9, Jul 17.8, Aug 16.2, Sep 8.1, Oct 3.9, Nov 2.6, Dec 2.2

  1. (a) Calculate the total annual flow. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of the annual flow that occurs from June to September. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate how many times greater the flow is in July than in February. Give your answer to one decimal place. [1]
  4. (d) Explain two reasons why the flow of the river is highest in summer. You should develop your answer. [4]
  5. (e) Suggest why this pattern of flow causes problems for water supply. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 84.7 billion m³
    Sum of the twelve months = 84.7 billion m³.
  2. (b) 66.1%
    13.9 + 17.8 + 16.2 + 8.1 = 56.0 (M1); 56.0 ÷ 84.7 × 100 = 66.1% (A1).
  3. (c) 8.9 times
    17.8 ÷ 2.0 = 8.9.
  4. (d) Rising temperatures in summer melt snow and glaciers in the Karakoram and Himalayas, which release large amounts of water into the river; the monsoon brings heavy rain in July and August, especially in the foothills, which adds to the river's flow.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to'). Reasons: snowmelt; glacier melt; monsoon rainfall in July–August; relief rain on mountain slopes in the catchment.
  5. (e) Much summer water flows to the sea because storage is limited, leaving too little in winter for rabi crops and towns.
    1 mark for linking high summer flow / low winter flow with a shortage in winter or the need for storage (or floods in summer).

Q16Water supply, shortage and pollution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) What is meant by 'desalination'? [1]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why desalination is not widely used to supply water in Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) 'Desalination is the best solution to the water shortages of coastal cities such as Karachi and Gwadar.' Evaluate this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Removing salt from sea water (or brackish groundwater) to make fresh water.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Desalination plants use large amounts of energy, and electricity in Pakistan is expensive and in short supply, so the water produced costs much more than river or groundwater; the plants are expensive to build and maintain, and they produce very salty waste (brine) that must be disposed of without harming the sea and mangroves.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').   3. (c) For: both cities are beside the sea, which is an unlimited source; Gwadar has very low rainfall and its reservoirs dry up in droughts, and Karachi's supply from the Indus and Hub dam is not enough for its growing population. Against: desalination is very costly in energy and money; Karachi loses a large share of its supply through leaks, theft and illegal hydrants, so reducing losses, building bulk supply schemes from the Indus, rainwater harvesting and recycling waste water may be cheaper. Judgement, e.g. useful for Gwadar but for Karachi better management comes first.

L3 (5–6): developed points on both sides, at least one named example from Pakistan, and a judgement. Points to credit: For: unlimited sea water, independent of rainfall and river flow, useful in droughts, Gwadar's lack of alternatives. Against: cost, energy, brine; Karachi's losses (leaks, theft, tanker mafia); other options such as new bulk supply schemes, rainwater harvesting, recycling, small dams.
Q17Water supply, shortage and pollution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

  2. (b) 'Pakistan's water shortage is caused more by poor management than by a lack of water.' Do you agree? Give reasons to support your answer. [4]

Q20Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a karez works. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages of the karez system in Balochistan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A karez is an underground channel dug into the foot of a hill or alluvial fan. It carries groundwater by gravity to the surface at a lower level. Vertical shafts at intervals are used for digging and cleaning; water is used in fields and villages, for example in Balochistan.
    1 per point, max 3.
  2. (b) Because the water flows underground, little is lost by evaporation in the hot, dry climate. It works by gravity, so no fuel or electricity is needed and it can supply water all year.
    1 + 1 development each: low evaporation; no pumping cost; reliable supply; community-managed.
Q21Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) What is the difference between a perennial canal and an inundation canal? [2]
  2. (b) Explain two disadvantages of inundation canals. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A perennial canal takes water from a barrage or dam and flows all year; an inundation canal flows only when the river is high in summer floods.

  2. (b) They have no water in winter when the river is low, so rabi crops cannot be irrigated. Their supply depends on the size of the flood: a poor flood brings too little water and a big flood can damage fields.
    1 + 1 development each.

  2. (b) Explain two problems caused by the rapid growth in the number of private tube wells. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.03 × 8 × 3600 = 864 m³.
    M1 convert hours to seconds; A1 864 m³.
  2. (b) Over-pumping lowers the water table, so wells must be deepened and pumping costs rise. In some areas the deeper water is saline, and using it damages soil; high fuel and electricity costs also burden farmers.
    1 + 1 development each: falling water table; saline groundwater; energy cost/load-shedding; inequality between rich and poor farmers.
Q28Irrigation methods · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the Indus Basin irrigation system is important to Pakistan's economy. [4]
  2. (b) 'Modern irrigation methods should replace traditional methods everywhere in Pakistan.' Do you agree? Give reasons to support your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most of Pakistan's crops, including wheat, rice, cotton and sugarcane, are grown on irrigated land because rainfall is too low. These crops feed the population and supply raw materials to industries such as textiles and sugar, which earn much of Pakistan's export income and provide many jobs.
    1 + 1 development each: food security; raw materials for industry; exports; employment.
  2. (b) For: drip and sprinkler save water and raise yields; lined canals reduce waterlogging. Against: modern methods are costly and need power; traditional methods such as karez are sustainable and suit local conditions, cost little and are community-run; small farmers cannot afford equipment. Judgement required.
    Levels: L1 (1–2); L2 (3–4) both sides developed with judgement.
Q29Waterlogging and salinity · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define 'waterlogging'. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how salinity develops in irrigated areas. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) When the water table rises to (or near) the surface so the soil is saturated and roots cannot breathe.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) When the water table is high, water rises to the surface through the soil by capillary action. In the hot climate it evaporates quickly, leaving the dissolved salts behind as a white crust (kallar) on the surface, which harms crops.
    1 per point, max 4: high water table; capillary action; evaporation; salts left / crust; reduced fertility.
Q30Waterlogging and salinity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

On a farm in Sindh, wheat yields fell from 3.2 tonnes per hectare to 2.4 tonnes per hectare as salinity increased.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage fall in yield. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of waterlogging in the Indus Plain. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.2 − 2.4 = 0.8; 0.8 ÷ 3.2 × 100 = 25%.
    M1; A1 25%.
  2. (b) Water seeps from unlined canals and watercourses into the ground, raising the water table. Farmers over-irrigate with flood methods and the flat land has poor natural drainage, so excess water cannot drain away.
    1 + 1 development each: canal seepage; over-irrigation; poor drainage; flat land; heavy monsoon/floods.
Q31Waterlogging and salinity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) What do the letters SCARP stand for? [1]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which waterlogging and salinity can be reduced. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Salinity Control and Reclamation Project(s).
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Tube wells (as in SCARP) pump out groundwater to lower the water table, and the water can be reused for irrigation. Lining canals stops seepage; drains such as the Left Bank Outfall Drain carry saline water away to the sea.
    1 + 1 development each: tube wells; canal lining; drainage; leaching with extra water; salt-tolerant crops; gypsum; tree planting.
Q32Waterlogging and salinity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain two effects of waterlogging and salinity on farmers. [4]
  2. (b) 'Waterlogging and salinity are caused more by people than by nature.' Do you agree? Give reasons to support your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Crop yields fall and some land becomes too salty to farm, so farmers' incomes drop and some must leave the land. Treating the land (gypsum, drainage, extra water) is costly, and houses and roads can be damaged by the high water table.
    1 + 1 development each.
  2. (b) People: unlined canals, over-irrigation, poor drainage design, cutting trees, poor maintenance. Nature: flat land with little natural drainage, hot climate with high evaporation, saline rocks and groundwater, heavy rain/floods. Judgement required.
    Levels: L1 (1–2); L2 (3–4) both sides developed with judgement.
