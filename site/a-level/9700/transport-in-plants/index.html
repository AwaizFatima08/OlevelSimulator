Q1Translocation (phloem) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by the terms 'source' and 'sink' in translocation. [2]
  2. (b) In a sugarcane plant, state one example of a source and one example of a sink. [2]
  3. (c) Name the main carbohydrate transported in the phloem and suggest why it is transported in this form rather than as glucose. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Source: a part of the plant where assimilates (sucrose) are loaded into the phloem, e.g. a photosynthesising leaf or a storage organ being used. Sink: a part where assimilates are removed from the phloem and used or stored, e.g. a growing root tip, fruit or developing tuber.
    B1 source – where sucrose enters phloem/is produced or released; B1 sink – where sucrose leaves phloem/is used or stored.
  2. (b) Source: mature photosynthesising leaf. Sink: the stem (storage of sucrose), growing shoot tip or roots.
    B1 each.
  3. (c) Sucrose; it is non-reducing/less reactive, so it is not used up (respired) on the way, and it is soluble so it can be carried in solution.
    B1 sucrose; B1 less reactive/not metabolised in transit (accept: soluble).
Q2Translocation (phloem) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by the term assimilates. [1]
  2. (b) In summer a potato tuber is a sink, but when it sprouts in spring it acts as a source. Explain this change. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why phloem is said to transport in both directions in a stem, even though sap in a single sieve tube flows one way at a time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Substances made by the plant (e.g. sucrose, amino acids) that are transported in the phloem.
    B1 substances made by the plant / products of photosynthesis and metabolism, e.g. sucrose or amino acids, moved in phloem.
  2. (b) Summer: sucrose arrives from leaves and is unloaded and converted to starch for storage, so the tuber is a sink. Spring: starch is hydrolysed to sugars, loaded into phloem as sucrose and transported to the growing shoots (sinks), so the tuber is a source.
    1 mark each for: summer – sucrose unloaded from phloem and stored as starch; spring – starch hydrolysed / converted to sucrose; sucrose loaded into phloem and moved to growing shoot/buds, which have no leaves yet / cannot photosynthesise enough.
  3. (c) Different sieve tubes in the same stem can carry sap upwards (to young leaves, flowers, fruits) and downwards (to roots, storage organs) at the same time, depending on where the sources and sinks are.
    B1 different sieve tubes can flow in opposite directions at the same time; B1 because sinks are both above (shoot tip, fruits) and below (roots) the source leaves.
Q3Translocation (phloem) · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In a leaf, sucrose is first moved into a companion cell. How does sucrose then move from the companion cell into the sieve tube element? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B by diffusion through plasmodesmata
    Sucrose is at a higher concentration in the companion cell than in the sieve tube element, so it diffuses through the many plasmodesmata linking the two cells; no carrier protein or ATP is needed for this step. The main distractor A is wrong for that reason. Distractor D is wrong because H⁺ are not pumped out of sieve tube elements; co-transport with H⁺ moves sucrose into companion cells from the leaf.
Q4Translocation (phloem) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the name of the process by which phloem sap moves along a sieve tube. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why water enters the sieve tubes near a source and leaves them near a sink. [3]
  3. (c) Give two differences between transport in the phloem and the transpiration stream in the xylem. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mass flow.
    B1 mass flow (accept pressure flow).
  2. (b) At the source sucrose is loaded, lowering the water potential of the sieve tube sap, so water enters by osmosis from the xylem/surrounding cells. At the sink sucrose is removed (used or stored), so the water potential of the sap rises and water leaves by osmosis.
    B1 loading sucrose at source lowers water potential (more negative); B1 water enters by osmosis (from xylem); B1 unloading at sink raises water potential so water moves out by osmosis.
  3. (c) Phloem: living cells, needs energy (ATP) for loading, can move up or down, carries sucrose/amino acids. Xylem: dead vessels, passive, only upwards, carries water and mineral ions.
    1 mark each for any two contrasts: living v dead cells; active loading/needs ATP v passive; two directions v upwards only; sucrose/amino acids v water/mineral ions; driven by hydrostatic pressure gradient (positive pressure) v tension (negative pressure).
Q5Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how sucrose is loaded into a sieve tube element from a companion cell in a leaf. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how loading sucrose at the source causes mass flow of phloem sap towards a sink. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Companion cells use ATP to actively pump H⁺ ions out (proton pump); this sets up a H⁺ concentration gradient; H⁺ diffuses back into the companion cell through a cotransporter protein carrying sucrose with it (cotransport); sucrose then passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata (down its concentration gradient).
    1 mark each for: H⁺ pumped out using ATP; H⁺ gradient; cotransporter protein with sucrose; via plasmodesmata into sieve tube.
  2. (b) Sucrose loading lowers the water potential in the sieve tube; water enters by osmosis from the xylem/surrounding cells, raising hydrostatic pressure at the source; at the sink sucrose is removed, water leaves, so pressure is lower; sap flows down the hydrostatic pressure gradient from high to low pressure.
    1 mark each for any three of: water potential lowered; water enters by osmosis; high hydrostatic pressure at source; lower pressure at sink; flow down pressure gradient.
Q6Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which process in translocation uses ATP directly? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B pumping of hydrogen ions out of companion cells
    Proton pumps in companion cell membranes hydrolyse ATP to move H⁺ out; the H⁺ gradient then drives sucrose cotransport. Movement of water into the sieve tubes (C) is osmosis, a passive process.
Q7Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A gardener in Mirpurkhas removed a complete ring of bark (including the phloem but not the xylem) from a branch of a mango tree. After four weeks the bark just above the ring had swollen and the fruits below the ring on that branch were small.

  1. (a) Explain the swelling above the ring. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the leaves on the branch did not wilt. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the fruits below the ring were small. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The phloem has been removed, so sucrose/assimilates moving down from the leaves cannot pass the ring; they accumulate above it, and water enters by osmosis/the tissue grows, causing swelling.
    B1 phloem removed, translocation blocked; B1 sugars accumulate above ring.
  2. (b) The xylem was not removed, so water and mineral ions still reach the leaves.
    B1.
  3. (c) Fruits are sinks; the sucrose they need for growth/storage comes from leaves above the ring through the phloem, which is now cut, so less sucrose reaches them.
    B1 fruits are sinks; B1 supply of sucrose from source cut off.
Q8Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A mango fruit on a tree in Multan gained 36 g of dry mass over 30 days. The total cross-sectional area of phloem in the fruit stalk was 0.12 cm². Assume all the dry mass arrived through the phloem.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean rate at which dry mass entered the fruit, in g h⁻¹ per cm² of phloem. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why the true rate of translocation into the fruit is higher than the value you calculated. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the fruit is described as a sink. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.417 g h⁻¹ cm⁻²
    M1 36 ÷ (30 × 24) = 0.05 g h⁻¹; A1 ÷ 0.12 = 0.417 g h⁻¹ cm⁻².
  2. (b) Some of the sucrose arriving is used in respiration by the fruit cells (releasing CO₂), so it does not appear as dry mass gain; some water/assimilates may also move out or be used for other processes.
    B1 some sucrose respired by the growing fruit; B1 so mass lost as CO₂ / not counted in dry mass gain. Accept: some assimilate used for synthesis of compounds lost, e.g. volatile scent.
  3. (c) It receives and uses or stores assimilates (sucrose) delivered by the phloem.
    B1 site where assimilates are unloaded and used/stored.
Q9Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Discs cut from bean leaves were floated on a sucrose solution at pH 5.5 and the uptake of sucrose into the companion cells was measured.Treatment – relative sucrose uptakecontrol, pH 5.5 – 24cyanide added, pH 5.5 – 3no cyanide, pH 8.0 – 9

  1. (a) Explain the result with cyanide, which stops aerobic respiration. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why raising the pH of the bathing solution to 8.0 also reduced sucrose uptake. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why some sucrose uptake still occurred with cyanide. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Without respiration, no ATP is made, so proton pumps in companion cell membranes stop; no H⁺ gradient forms, so sucrose co-transport into companion cells almost stops.
    B1 no / little ATP produced; B1 proton pumps (H⁺–ATPase) cannot pump H⁺ out, so no H⁺ gradient for co-transport of sucrose.
  2. (b) pH 8 means a low H⁺ concentration outside the cells, so the H⁺ concentration gradient into the companion cells is small; fewer H⁺ move in through the co-transporter, so less sucrose is carried in with them.
    B1 fewer H⁺ outside / smaller H⁺ (proton) gradient; B1 less H⁺ diffusion through co-transporter proteins so less sucrose moved with them.
  3. (c) Some sucrose diffused in / some ATP still made by anaerobic respiration or remaining H⁺ gradient for a short time.
    B1 any one reasonable suggestion: diffusion; a little ATP from anaerobic respiration/glycolysis; existing H⁺ gradient not yet run down.
Q10Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Sap was collected from aphid stylets inserted into the stem of a sunflower plant at three distances below the mature leaves.distance below leaves / cm – sucrose concentration / mol dm⁻³10 – 0.8240 – 0.6470 – 0.47

  1. (a) Describe the pattern shown by the data. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how this concentration gradient produces mass flow of sap down the stem. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why the sucrose concentration is still well above zero at the base of the stem. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sucrose concentration in the sieve tube sap falls with distance from the source leaves (0.82 → 0.64 → 0.47 mol dm⁻³).
    B1 concentration decreases the further down / away from the leaves, with figures.
  2. (b) High sucrose concentration near the leaves gives a low water potential, so water enters by osmosis and hydrostatic pressure is high; lower sucrose near the roots means less water enters/water leaves, so pressure is lower; sap flows down the hydrostatic pressure gradient.
    B1 high solute concentration at top → lower water potential → water enters by osmosis; B1 high hydrostatic (turgor) pressure near source; B1 sap moves by mass flow from high to low pressure (towards the sink).
  3. (c) Sucrose is still being carried on to sinks in the roots; it is only unloaded where it is used or stored.
    B1 sucrose not yet unloaded / still in transit to the root sinks.
Q11Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Sugar beet root cells store sucrose at a concentration higher than that in the phloem sap. Explain why unloading sucrose into these cells needs energy. [2]
  2. (b) In a potato tuber, sucrose is unloaded down a concentration gradient. Explain how the tuber keeps this gradient. [2]
  3. (c) State one other way in which sucrose is used at a sink such as a root tip. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sucrose must move against its concentration gradient, so it is moved by active transport (using ATP / a proton gradient made with ATP).
    B1 against the concentration gradient; B1 active transport / co-transport needing ATP.
  2. (b) Sucrose entering the tuber cells is converted to starch (via glucose/fructose), keeping the sucrose concentration in the cells low, so sucrose keeps diffusing in.
