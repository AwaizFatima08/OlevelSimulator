Q1Membrane structure & cell signalling · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which components of the cell surface membrane are mainly involved in cell recognition?A the fatty acid tails of phospholipidsB glycoproteins and glycolipidsC cholesterol moleculesD channel proteins [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B glycoproteins and glycolipids
    The carbohydrate chains of glycoproteins and glycolipids (the glycocalyx) act as recognition sites / antigens. Cholesterol (C) only regulates fluidity and stability; channel proteins (D) let ions and polar molecules through.
Q2Membrane structure & cell signalling · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the structure of the cell surface membrane is described as a 'fluid mosaic'. [2]
  2. (b) Fish living in very cold water under polar ice have cell membranes with a different composition from fish in tropical rivers such as the Indus. State the role of cholesterol in a cell membrane. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why sodium ions cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer by simple diffusion. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fluid: phospholipids (and many proteins) can move about / diffuse sideways within the bilayer. Mosaic: proteins are scattered among the phospholipids in a pattern, like tiles of a mosaic.
    1 mark: fluid – molecules move laterally within their layer. 1 mark: mosaic – proteins (of different kinds) scattered/embedded through the phospholipid bilayer.
  2. (b) Cholesterol regulates membrane fluidity: it stops phospholipids packing too closely at low temperature (keeps the membrane fluid) and restricts their movement at high temperature (keeps it stable); it also reduces permeability to ions/polar molecules.
    1 mark each for any two of: regulates/buffers fluidity; prevents tight packing / keeps membrane fluid at low temperature; reduces fluidity / adds stability at high temperature; reduces permeability to water-soluble ions/polar molecules; mechanical stability.
  3. (c) Sodium ions are charged (and surrounded by water molecules); the interior of the bilayer is made of hydrophobic fatty acid tails, which repel/do not let charged particles through, so they must use channel or carrier proteins.
    1 mark: ions are charged / hydrated / water-soluble. 1 mark: core of bilayer is hydrophobic (fatty acid tails), so ions need channel/carrier proteins.
Q3Membrane structure & cell signalling · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Proteins in the membrane of a human cell were labelled with a green fluorescent dye, and proteins of a mouse cell with a red dye. The two cells were fused. After 40 minutes at 37 °C the red and green labels were evenly mixed over the whole hybrid cell. At 5 °C the labels stayed in separate halves.What do these results show?A Membrane proteins are fixed in position by the cytoskeleton at all temperatures.B Membrane proteins can move sideways in the bilayer, and this movement is faster at higher temperature.C Membrane proteins are made faster at 37 °C than at 5 °C.D Phospholipids flip between the two layers of the membrane at 37 °C. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B Membrane proteins can move sideways in the bilayer, and this movement is faster at higher temperature.
    Mixing shows lateral movement of proteins (the 'fluid' in fluid mosaic); it needs kinetic energy so it is slowed at 5 °C. C is wrong because the labels were on existing proteins; D is about phospholipids, which were not labelled.
Q4Membrane structure & cell signalling · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Outline the stages by which a water-soluble hormone such as glucagon, arriving in the blood, causes a specific response inside a liver cell. [4]
  2. (b) Steroid hormones, such as oestrogen, have receptors inside the cell rather than on the cell surface membrane. Suggest why. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why only some cells in the body respond to glucagon even though all cells are exposed to it in the blood. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Glucagon binds to a specific receptor on the cell surface membrane (complementary shape); binding changes the receptor's shape; this activates a G protein / transduction inside the cell; an enzyme makes a second messenger (cyclic AMP); the second messenger activates a chain of enzymes (amplification); the final enzyme causes the response (glycogen broken down to glucose).
    1 mark each for any four of: hormone (ligand) binds to specific receptor on cell surface membrane; receptor has complementary shape / only target cells have the receptor; receptor changes shape / signal transduction; G protein activated; second messenger (cAMP) produced; cascade/amplification by enzymes; specific response, e.g. glycogen breakdown.
  2. (b) Steroids are lipid-soluble (non-polar), so they can diffuse through the hydrophobic phospholipid bilayer and reach receptors in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
    1 mark: steroids are lipid-soluble / non-polar / hydrophobic. 1 mark: so they pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer (by simple diffusion).
  3. (c) Only target cells (e.g. liver cells) have receptors with a shape complementary to glucagon.
    1 mark: only target cells have the specific receptor.
Q5Membrane structure & cell signalling · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the structure of a phospholipid molecule and explain why phospholipids form a bilayer when mixed with water. [4]
  2. (b) A species of bacterium lives in hot springs at 80 °C. Its membrane phospholipids contain a higher proportion of saturated fatty acids than those of bacteria living at 25 °C. Suggest how this helps the bacterium to survive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Glycerol joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate group; the phosphate head is hydrophilic (polar) and the fatty acid tails are hydrophobic (non-polar); in water the heads face the water on both sides and the tails point inwards, away from water, forming a bilayer.
    1 mark: glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate (group). 1 mark: phosphate head hydrophilic/polar. 1 mark: fatty acid tails hydrophobic/non-polar. 1 mark: heads face aqueous surroundings, tails face inwards / away from water, so two layers form.
  2. (b) Saturated fatty acid tails are straight (no kinks), so they pack closely together; this reduces fluidity / keeps the membrane stable and not too leaky at high temperature.
    1 mark: saturated tails straight / no C=C kinks, pack tightly. 1 mark: membrane less fluid / more stable / less permeable at high temperature (does not become too leaky).
Q6Diffusion, osmosis & water potential · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Two neighbouring plant cells, X and Y, have water potentials of −450 kPa and −620 kPa respectively.Which statement is correct?A Water moves from Y to X because Y has the lower water potential.B Water moves from X to Y because X has the higher water potential.C There is no net movement of water because both values are negative.D Water moves from X to Y by active transport. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B Water moves from X to Y because X has the higher water potential.
    Water moves by osmosis from a higher (less negative) water potential to a lower (more negative) one: −450 kPa is higher than −620 kPa. A reverses the direction; osmosis is passive, so D is wrong.
Q7Diffusion, osmosis & water potential · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A plant epidermal cell is placed in a sucrose solution that has a lower water potential than the cell. Describe and explain what happens to the cell. [3]
  2. (b) A red blood cell and a plant cell are both placed in distilled water. Explain why the red blood cell bursts but the plant cell does not. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water leaves the cell by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable cell surface membrane (and tonoplast); the vacuole and protoplast shrink; the membrane pulls away from the cell wall (plasmolysis).
    1 mark: water moves out by osmosis. 1 mark: from higher to lower water potential / down water potential gradient through partially permeable membrane. 1 mark: protoplast/vacuole shrinks; membrane pulls away from wall = plasmolysis (cell becomes flaccid).
  2. (b) In both cells water enters by osmosis because distilled water has the higher water potential. The red blood cell has no cell wall, so it swells until the membrane bursts (haemolysis). The plant cell has a strong cellulose cell wall that resists expansion, so the cell becomes turgid but does not burst.
    1 mark: water enters both cells by osmosis (water has higher water potential). 1 mark: animal cell has no cell wall, membrane bursts / haemolysis. 1 mark: plant cell wall (cellulose) resists expansion / exerts pressure, cell becomes turgid.
Q8Diffusion, osmosis & water potential · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The rate of glucose uptake by human red blood cells was measured at different external glucose concentrations.External glucose / mmol dm⁻³: 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20Rate of uptake / arbitrary units: 0, 7.5, 12.0, 16.2, 18.1, 18.9, 19.0

  1. (a) Describe how the rate of glucose uptake changes as the external glucose concentration increases. [2]
  2. (b) Glucose enters red blood cells by facilitated diffusion through carrier proteins. Explain the shape of the curve at concentrations above 16 mmol dm⁻³. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest how the curve would differ for a substance that crosses the membrane only by simple diffusion through the phospholipid bilayer. Explain your answer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The rate increases steeply at first (roughly in proportion) up to about 4 mmol dm⁻³, then increases more slowly and levels off at about 19 arbitrary units from about 16 mmol dm⁻³.
    1 mark: rate increases as concentration increases (steeply at low concentration). 1 mark: levels off / plateaus at about 19 a.u. (from about 16 mmol dm⁻³) – data quote needed.
  2. (b) All the carrier proteins are occupied (saturated), so the number of carrier proteins is now the limiting factor and increasing concentration cannot increase the rate further.
    1 mark: carrier proteins all in use / saturated. 1 mark: number of carriers limits the rate (not the concentration gradient).
  3. (c) The rate would keep rising in proportion to concentration (a straight line, no plateau), because no carrier proteins are involved, so there is nothing to become saturated; rate depends only on the concentration gradient.
    1 mark: straight line / rate continues to increase / no plateau. 1 mark: no proteins to saturate; rate proportional to concentration gradient.
Q9Diffusion, osmosis & water potential · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) The rate of diffusion across an exchange surface is proportional to (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of the surface.In one tissue the surface area doubles and the thickness of the surface halves, while the concentration difference stays the same. What happens to the rate of diffusion?A It stays the same.B It doubles.C It increases four times.D It halves. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C It increases four times.
    Rate ∝ (2 × area) ÷ (0.5 × thickness) = 4 × original rate. B forgets the effect of thickness; A wrongly assumes the two changes cancel out.
Q10Active transport, endo- & exocytosis · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define active transport. [2]
  2. (b) Cells in the pancreas release the enzyme amylase, a protein, into a duct. Name the process by which amylase leaves the cell and describe how it happens. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The movement of molecules or ions across a membrane against their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration), using carrier proteins and energy from ATP (respiration).
    1 mark: against a concentration gradient / low to high concentration. 1 mark: uses carrier proteins and ATP / energy from respiration.
  2. (b) Exocytosis: amylase is packaged by the Golgi body into secretory vesicles; vesicles move to the cell surface membrane (along microtubules); the vesicle membrane fuses with the cell surface membrane and the contents are released outside; this needs ATP.
    1 mark: exocytosis. 1 mark each for any two of: protein packaged into (secretory) vesicles by Golgi; vesicles move to cell surface membrane; vesicle fuses with membrane, contents released; requires ATP.
Q11Active transport, endo- & exocytosis · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Root tissue from a wheat seedling was placed in a solution containing 0.5 mmol dm⁻³ nitrate ions. The concentration of nitrate inside the root cells was already 12 mmol dm⁻³. The rate of nitrate uptake was 4.8 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹. Cyanide, which inhibits aerobic respiration, was then added, and the rate fell to 0.6 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage decrease in the rate of nitrate uptake caused by adding cyanide. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how these results show that most nitrate ions are taken up by active transport. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why some nitrate uptake still occurs in the presence of cyanide. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 87.5 %
    M1: (4.8 − 0.6) ÷ 4.8 × 100. A1: 87.5 %.
  2. (b) Cyanide stops aerobic respiration, so little ATP is made; uptake falls greatly, so uptake needs ATP; nitrate is taken up although its concentration is higher inside the cells, i.e. against the gradient – both features of active transport.
    1 mark: cyanide inhibits respiration / ATP production. 1 mark: uptake depends on ATP (falls without it). 1 mark: ions moved against concentration gradient (higher inside cells) with data quote.
  3. (c) Some nitrate enters by (facilitated) diffusion, or some ATP is still made by anaerobic respiration / from stored ATP.
    1 mark for either: some passive uptake (facilitated diffusion) or some ATP still available (anaerobic respiration / reserves).
Q12Active transport, endo- & exocytosis · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which pair of processes both require ATP from respiration?A exocytosis and active transportB osmosis and phagocytosisC facilitated diffusion and pinocytosisD simple diffusion and exocytosis [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A exocytosis and active transport
    Exocytosis and active transport both use ATP (vesicle movement/fusion; carrier protein shape change). Osmosis, facilitated diffusion and simple diffusion are passive, so B, C and D each contain a passive process.
Q13Active transport, endo- & exocytosis · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A nerve cell membrane contains sodium–potassium pumps. Each pump moves 3 sodium ions out of the cell and 2 potassium ions into the cell for each molecule of ATP hydrolysed. The pumps in one cell hydrolyse 1.5 × 10⁸ molecules of ATP per second. Calculate the number of sodium ions moved out of this cell in one minute. Give your answer in standard form. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the sodium–potassium pump is an example of active transport rather than facilitated diffusion. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why cells that carry out a lot of active transport, such as those lining kidney tubules, have many mitochondria. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.7 × 10¹⁰ sodium ions
    M1: 3 × 1.5 × 10⁸ × 60. A1: 2.7 × 10¹⁰.
  2. (b) The pump moves both ions against their concentration gradients (Na⁺ out to where it is more concentrated, K⁺ in to where it is more concentrated) and uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to change the shape of the carrier protein.
