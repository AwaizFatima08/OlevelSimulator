Q1Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which tissue is present in the wall of the trachea but absent from the wall of a bronchiole? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B cartilage
    Bronchioles have no cartilage; their walls are mainly smooth muscle and elastic fibres, and larger bronchioles are still lined by ciliated epithelium. Smooth muscle (A) and elastic fibres (D) occur in both.
Q2Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is the function of the elastic fibres in the walls of the airways and alveoli? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B to stretch during inspiration and recoil during expiration, helping to push air out
    Elastic fibres stretch as the lungs fill and recoil to help expel air. Contraction (A) is done by smooth muscle; keeping the trachea open (D) is the role of cartilage.
Q3Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the cartilage differs in the trachea, the bronchi and the bronchioles. [3]
  2. (b) Explain the function of the cartilage in the trachea. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Trachea: C-shaped rings of cartilage. Bronchi: irregular plates/blocks of cartilage. Bronchioles: no cartilage.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) It supports the trachea and keeps it open, preventing it collapsing when air pressure inside falls during inspiration.
    1 mark: support / keeps airway open. 1 mark: prevents collapse when pressure falls (inspiration).
Q4Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how goblet cells and ciliated cells work together to protect the lungs from infection. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why there are no goblet cells or cilia in the alveoli. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Goblet cells secrete mucus, which traps dust, bacteria and viruses; cilia on ciliated cells beat in a coordinated (wave-like) way; they move the mucus up towards the throat (larynx) where it is swallowed, so pathogens are removed before reaching the alveoli.
    1 mark: goblet cells secrete mucus that traps particles/pathogens. 1 mark: cilia beat (in waves/synchrony). 1 mark: mucus moved up to throat and swallowed / away from alveoli.
  2. (b) A layer of mucus or cilia would increase the diffusion distance (and ciliated cells are thicker than squamous cells), slowing gas exchange; mucus could also fill the small air spaces.
    1 mark: would increase diffusion distance / thicker barrier. 1 mark: would reduce rate of gas exchange / block air spaces.
Q5Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the cartilage in the trachea is C-shaped rather than a complete ring. [2]
  2. (b) State the function of the smooth muscle in the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles. [1]
  3. (c) Name the structure in the airways where gas exchange takes place. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The gap at the back allows flexibility / lets the oesophagus, which lies behind the trachea, expand when food is swallowed.
    1 mark: gap at back next to oesophagus. 1 mark: allows oesophagus to expand / food bolus to pass, or allows trachea to flex.
  2. (b) Contracts to narrow (constrict) the airways, controlling the diameter of the lumen and the flow of air.
    1 mark: contraction changes diameter / constricts airways.
  3. (c) The alveoli.
    1 mark.
Q6Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the cilia in the trachea move mucus. [2]
  2. (b) Whooping cough is caused by bacteria that release a toxin which damages ciliated cells. Suggest why people with whooping cough cough violently and often. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The cilia beat (move back and forth) in a coordinated, wave-like rhythm; this sweeps the mucus upwards towards the larynx/throat.
    1 mark: cilia beat in coordinated waves. 1 mark: move mucus upward / towards throat.
  2. (b) Cilia no longer move mucus, so mucus builds up in the airways; coughing is the only way to remove it.
    1 mark: mucus accumulates because cilia damaged. 1 mark: coughing needed to clear the mucus/airways.

Q8Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which structures produce the mucus that lines the trachea? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A goblet cells and mucous glands
    Mucus is secreted by goblet cells in the epithelium and by mucous glands below it. Ciliated cells (B) move mucus but do not secrete it; squamous cells (D) line alveoli, not the trachea.
Q9Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) During an asthma attack the airways narrow and breathing becomes difficult. Which tissue contracts to cause this narrowing? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C smooth muscle
    Smooth muscle in the walls of bronchi and especially bronchioles contracts, reducing the diameter of the lumen. Elastic fibres (B) recoil but do not actively contract.
Q10Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The wall of the trachea was examined from the lumen outwards. Four structures were described:P: a layer of tall cells with tiny hair-like projections on their free surfaceQ: single cup-shaped cells scattered among the cells of P, filled with pale-staining vesiclesR: clusters of secretory cells in the connective tissue beneath P, with ducts opening onto the surfaceS: a thick C-shaped band of bluish, glassy tissue with cells in small spaces (lacunae)

  1. (a) Name the tissues P, Q, R and S. [4]
  2. (b) State the function of tissue S and one other tissue named in your answer to (a). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) P ciliated (pseudostratified columnar) epithelium; Q goblet cell; R mucous gland (in submucosa); S cartilage (hyaline).
    1 mark each. Accept 'ciliated epithelium' for P.
  2. (b) S: keeps trachea open / prevents collapse. P: cilia move mucus up to the throat (or Q/R: secrete mucus to trap particles).
    1 mark for S; 1 mark for a correct function of P, Q or R.
Q11Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In cystic fibrosis the mucus in the airways is much thicker and stickier than normal. Suggest why people with cystic fibrosis suffer from frequent lung infections. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why people with cystic fibrosis may also become breathless. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cilia cannot move the thick mucus (mucociliary escalator fails); mucus and trapped bacteria stay in the airways; bacteria multiply in the warm, moist mucus, causing infection.
    1 mark: cilia unable to move/clear thick mucus. 1 mark: bacteria/pathogens trapped and not removed. 1 mark: bacteria multiply in the mucus / reach lower airways.
  2. (b) Mucus blocks or narrows bronchioles, so less air reaches the alveoli; less oxygen diffuses into the blood (smaller surface area for gas exchange).
    1 mark: mucus blocks/narrows airways. 1 mark: less ventilation of alveoli / less gas exchange.
Q12Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 3 style · 5 marks

A student viewed a transverse section of trachea with a light microscope. With the objective used, one eyepiece graticule division equals 4.0 µm. The ciliated epithelium measured 12 divisions thick and the cartilage measured 85 divisions thick.

  1. (a) Calculate the actual thickness of the ciliated epithelium and of the cartilage. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate how many times thicker the cartilage is than the epithelium. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. [1]
  3. (c) On a photomicrograph of a bronchiole, a scale bar 20 mm long represents 100 µm. The diameter of the bronchiole on the photomicrograph is 96 mm. Calculate the actual diameter of the bronchiole in µm. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Epithelium 48 µm; cartilage 340 µm
    1 mark each: 12 × 4.0 = 48 µm; 85 × 4.0 = 340 µm.
  2. (b) 7.1 times
    340 ÷ 48 = 7.08 → 7.1.
  3. (c) 480 µm
    M1: 96 ÷ 20 × 100 (or magnification = 20 000 µm ÷ 100 µm = ×200; 96 000 µm ÷ 200). A1: 480 µm.
Q13Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) An electron micrograph of the surface of a ciliated cell has a magnification of ×6000. A cilium on the micrograph is 45 mm long. Calculate the actual length of the cilium in µm. [2]
  2. (b) Convert your answer to nanometres. [1]
  3. (c) Ciliated cells contain many mitochondria close to the bases of the cilia. Explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 7.5 µm
    M1: 45 mm = 45 000 µm; 45 000 ÷ 6000. A1: 7.5 µm.
  2. (b) 7500 nm
    7.5 × 1000 = 7500 nm (error carried forward).
  3. (c) Mitochondria produce ATP by aerobic respiration; ATP provides the energy for the cilia to beat (continuously).
    1 mark: mitochondria make ATP / site of aerobic respiration. 1 mark: cilia beating needs energy.
Q14Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In a rare inherited condition, enzymes released by white blood cells in the lungs break down elastic fibres faster than normal. Suggest and explain how this would affect breathing out. [3]
  2. (b) Alveolar walls damaged in this way may break down so that several alveoli merge into one larger air space. Explain why this reduces the rate of gas exchange. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fewer elastic fibres, so the walls of the alveoli and airways recoil less; less air is pushed out during expiration; air remains trapped in the alveoli (so the concentration gradient for gas exchange is reduced); breathing out needs more effort.
    1 mark: less elastic recoil. 1 mark: less air expelled / air trapped / harder to exhale. 1 mark: stale air reduces concentration gradient / gas exchange or extra muscular effort needed.
  2. (b) Merging alveoli reduces the total surface area available for diffusion, so less oxygen can diffuse into the blood per unit time.
    1 mark: smaller (total) surface area. 1 mark: less diffusion of O₂/CO₂ per unit time.

Q16Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Mucus contains glycoproteins. Name two organelles that you would expect to be abundant in a goblet cell and explain the role of each in producing mucus. [4]

    1 mark.
Q17Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which statements about the wall of a bronchus are correct?1 It contains irregular plates of cartilage.2 It is lined by ciliated epithelium with goblet cells.3 It contains smooth muscle. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 1, 2 and 3
    A bronchus has irregular blocks of cartilage (not complete C-rings), ciliated epithelium with goblet cells, and smooth muscle. B is a common error: smooth muscle is present in bronchi as well as bronchioles.
Q18Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe four differences between the wall of the trachea and the wall of a bronchiole. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why bronchioles can change their diameter but the trachea cannot do so to the same extent. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Trachea has C-shaped cartilage rings, bronchiole has no cartilage; trachea has many goblet cells, bronchiole few or none; trachea has mucous glands, bronchiole none; trachea has relatively little smooth muscle, bronchiole has a relatively thick layer of smooth muscle; trachea lined by tall (pseudostratified) ciliated epithelium, bronchiole by shorter (cuboidal) ciliated epithelium (none in the smallest); trachea wall much thicker / lumen wider.
    1 mark for each comparative difference, max 4: cartilage; goblet cells; mucous glands; proportion of smooth muscle; height/type of epithelium; wall thickness/diameter.
  2. (b) Bronchioles have no cartilage and a high proportion of smooth muscle, so when the muscle contracts or relaxes the lumen narrows or widens; the trachea is held open by rigid cartilage rings.
    1 mark: bronchioles have smooth muscle and no cartilage. 1 mark: trachea's cartilage is rigid / prevents narrowing.
Q19Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State three rules a student should follow when making a plan diagram of a transverse section of a bronchus. [3]
  2. (b) In the bronchus section the total wall thickness was 60 eyepiece divisions, of which 38 divisions were cartilage. Calculate the percentage of the wall thickness made up of cartilage. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [1]
  3. (c) A second section was of a bronchiole. Suggest two features that the student would see in the bronchiole that would allow her to distinguish it from the bronchus. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Draw only the outlines of tissue layers (no individual cells); use clear, continuous thin lines with no shading; draw tissues in the correct proportions/relative thickness; use a large drawing (over half the space); label lines ruled, not crossing, touching the tissue.
    1 mark each for any three of: no cells drawn; sharp continuous lines, no shading; correct proportions of layers; large size; ruled label lines to the correct tissues.
  2. (b) 63 %
    38 ÷ 60 × 100 = 63.3 → 63 %.
  3. (c) No cartilage; smaller diameter / thinner wall; relatively thicker smooth muscle layer; few or no goblet cells / no mucous glands; epithelium shorter (cuboidal).
    1 mark each for any two.
Q20Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A woman in Karachi with asthma measured her peak flow (the fastest rate at which she could breathe out) before and 15 minutes after using an inhaler.Before: 220 dm³ min⁻¹After: 380 dm³ min⁻¹

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in peak flow after using the inhaler. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) The inhaler contains a drug that causes smooth muscle to relax. Explain how this increases peak flow. [2]
  3. (c) Her reading before using the inhaler was 55 % of the expected peak flow for a healthy woman of her age and height. Calculate her expected peak flow. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 72.7 %
    M1: (380 − 220) ÷ 220 × 100. A1: 72.7 %.
  2. (b) Relaxed smooth muscle in the walls of bronchioles (and bronchi) allows the airways to widen; the larger lumen means less resistance, so air can flow out faster.
    1 mark: bronchioles widen / dilate when smooth muscle relaxes. 1 mark: larger lumen / less resistance, faster airflow.
  3. (c) 400 dm³ min⁻¹
    M1: 220 ÷ 0.55 (or 220 × 100 ÷ 55). A1: 400 dm³ min⁻¹.
Q21Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

The table shows the percentage of inhaled particles of different diameters that are trapped in the trachea and bronchi.Particle diameter / µm: 0.5, 2.5, 5, 10Trapped in trachea and bronchi / %: 5, 40, 70, 90

  1. (a) Describe and explain the pattern shown by the data. [3]
  2. (b) Smog in Lahore contains many particles smaller than 2.5 µm. Suggest why these particles are more harmful to the lungs than larger particles. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The larger the particle, the greater the percentage trapped in the trachea and bronchi (90 % at 10 µm, 5 % at 0.5 µm); large particles hit and stick to the mucus in the upper airways and are removed by cilia; small particles stay suspended in the air and are carried deep into the bronchioles and alveoli.
    1 mark: larger particles more trapped (data quote). 1 mark: trapped by mucus (from goblet cells) and removed by cilia. 1 mark: small particles follow air flow into bronchioles/alveoli.
  2. (b) They reach the alveoli, where there is no mucus or cilia to remove them; they can damage the thin alveolar walls / cause inflammation, reducing gas exchange (they must be removed by phagocytes/macrophages).
    1 mark: reach alveoli. 1 mark: no mucus/cilia there so not removed / cause inflammation or damage to exchange surface.
Q22Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Samples of bronchus lining were taken during routine medical procedures from adults living in a large industrial city and from adults in rural villages. The number of goblet cells per mm of epithelium was counted.City group: mean 41 goblet cells per mmVillage group: mean 25 goblet cells per mm

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage by which the mean number of goblet cells in the city group is greater than in the village group. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest an explanation for the difference between the two groups. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one disadvantage to the city group of having more goblet cells. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 64 %
    M1: (41 − 25) ÷ 25 × 100. A1: 64 %.
  2. (b) City air has more particles/pollutants; the epithelium responds by producing more goblet cells, so more mucus is secreted to trap the extra particles.
    1 mark: more pollution/particles in city air. 1 mark: more goblet cells → more mucus to trap particles.
  3. (c) Excess mucus may be too much for the cilia to remove, so it collects, narrows airways and allows bacteria to multiply (more infections, coughing).
    1 mark: excess mucus blocks airways / harbours bacteria / causes cough.
Q23Alveoli & gas exchange · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State three features of alveoli and explain how each increases the rate of gas exchange. [3]
  2. (b) Name the process by which oxygen moves from the alveolar air into the blood. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Very many alveoli give a large surface area; walls one cell thick of squamous epithelium give a short diffusion distance; close network of capillaries carries O₂ away/brings CO₂, keeping a steep concentration gradient; ventilation renews air, maintaining the gradient; moist lining lets gases dissolve.
    1 mark each for three feature + explanation pairs: large surface area; thin wall/short distance; good blood supply maintains gradient; ventilation maintains gradient; moist surface.
  2. (b) Diffusion.
    1 mark (not active transport).
Q24Alveoli & gas exchange · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe the route taken by a molecule of carbon dioxide as it leaves the blood and passes out of the body. [3]
  2. (b) Alveolar walls contain macrophages. Suggest the role of these cells. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) From plasma (or red blood cell) through the capillary endothelium, through the alveolar epithelium into the alveolar air; then out through the bronchiole, bronchus and trachea during expiration.
    1 mark: through capillary wall/endothelium. 1 mark: through alveolar wall/epithelium into alveolus. 1 mark: bronchiole → bronchus → trachea (in correct order) during expiration.
  2. (b) They engulf (by phagocytosis) bacteria and dust particles that reach the alveoli.
    1 mark: phagocytosis of pathogens/particles.
Q25Alveoli & gas exchange · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how breathing (ventilation) and blood flow both help to maintain a steep concentration gradient of oxygen between alveolar air and blood. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why the capillaries around the alveoli are so narrow that red blood cells pass through them in single file, pressed against the capillary wall. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ventilation regularly replaces alveolar air with fresh air, keeping the oxygen concentration in the alveoli high (and removing CO₂). Blood flow continuously carries oxygenated blood away from the alveoli and brings deoxygenated blood (low O₂) to them, keeping the oxygen concentration in the capillaries low. Oxygen binding to haemoglobin also keeps the plasma oxygen concentration low.
    1 mark: ventilation brings fresh air / keeps alveolar O₂ high. 1 mark: removes CO₂-rich air. 1 mark: blood flow removes oxygenated blood / brings deoxygenated blood. 1 mark: keeps blood O₂ low (helped by uptake by haemoglobin).
  2. (b) This reduces the diffusion distance between the alveolar air and the haemoglobin; red blood cells move more slowly, giving more time for gas exchange.
    1 mark: short diffusion distance. 1 mark: slows red cells / more time for diffusion / larger area of contact.
Q26Alveoli & gas exchange · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In pulmonary fibrosis, scar tissue forms in the walls of the alveoli. In one patient the distance between alveolar air and blood increased from 0.5 µm to 3.0 µm. Assuming the rate of diffusion is inversely proportional to the diffusion distance, calculate how many times slower diffusion became. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest why patients with pulmonary fibrosis become breathless, especially during exercise. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6 times slower
    3.0 ÷ 0.5 = 6.
  2. (b) Oxygen diffuses into the blood more slowly, so the blood is less fully oxygenated; less oxygen reaches respiring muscle cells; during exercise the demand for oxygen rises, so breathing rate increases to try to compensate. Scar tissue may also make the lungs stiffer, so less air is breathed in.
    1 mark: slower diffusion of O₂ into blood / less O₂ absorbed. 1 mark: less O₂ to cells for aerobic respiration. 1 mark: exercise raises O₂ demand → faster/deeper breathing (or stiff lungs reduce ventilation).
Q27Alveoli & gas exchange · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Climbers on their way to K2 spend time at a base camp at about 5000 m above sea level. The partial pressure of oxygen in the air is 21.2 kPa at sea level and 11.2 kPa at 5000 m.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage decrease in the partial pressure of oxygen between sea level and the base camp. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why climbers at the base camp become breathless even when resting. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 47.2 %
    M1: (21.2 − 11.2) ÷ 21.2 × 100. A1: 47.2 %.
  2. (b) Lower partial pressure of oxygen in the air means a lower partial pressure in the alveoli; the gradient between alveolar air and blood is smaller, so oxygen diffuses into the blood more slowly; less oxygen is carried to the tissues, so breathing rate increases to compensate.
    1 mark: lower pO₂ in alveoli. 1 mark: smaller diffusion gradient / slower diffusion into blood. 1 mark: less O₂ delivered / haemoglobin less saturated, so breathing faster/deeper.

Q29Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A pair of adult human lungs contains about 480 million alveoli. Assume that each alveolus has a diameter of 0.2 mm.

  1. (a) Calculate the surface area of one alveolus, treating it as a sphere (surface area = πd²). Give your answer in mm² to 3 significant figures. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total surface area of the alveoli in m², to 2 significant figures. (1 m² = 10⁶ mm²) [2]
  3. (c) State one assumption in this calculation that may make the answer inaccurate, and explain why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.126 mm²
    π × 0.2² = 0.1257 mm².
  2. (b) 60 m²
    M1: 480 × 10⁶ × 0.1257 = 6.03 × 10⁷ mm². A1: ÷ 10⁶ = 60 m².
  3. (c) Alveoli are not complete, separate spheres: part of each opens into an alveolar duct and neighbouring alveoli share walls, so not all of the calculated surface is in contact with air (or alveoli vary in size, so 0.2 mm is only an average).
    1 mark: alveoli not complete/separate spheres (open to ducts, share walls) or diameters vary / not all alveoli the same size / not all surface covered by capillaries – with a reason.
Q30Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

The table shows partial pressures of gases (kPa).Gas: inspired air | alveolar air | deoxygenated blood arriving at lungsOxygen: 21.0 | 13.3 | 5.3Carbon dioxide: 0.04 | 5.3 | 6.1

  1. (a) Explain why the partial pressure of oxygen in alveolar air is lower than in inspired air. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the difference in partial pressure (the diffusion gradient) between alveolar air and deoxygenated blood for oxygen and for carbon dioxide. [2]
  3. (c) Carbon dioxide still diffuses out of the blood as quickly as oxygen diffuses in, even though its gradient is much smaller. Suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inspired air mixes with the air that remains in the lungs (residual air / air in airways) which has less oxygen; oxygen is continuously diffusing from alveolar air into the blood.
    1 mark: mixing with stale/residual air. 1 mark: O₂ constantly removed by diffusion into blood.
  2. (b) Oxygen: 8.0 kPa; carbon dioxide: 0.8 kPa
    1 mark each: 13.3 − 5.3 = 8.0 kPa; 6.1 − 5.3 = 0.8 kPa.
  3. (c) Carbon dioxide is much more soluble in water (in the moist alveolar lining and plasma) than oxygen, so it diffuses through the barrier faster for a given gradient.
    1 mark: CO₂ much more soluble.
