Q1Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the structure of an antibody molecule. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how antibodies cause bacteria to clump together (agglutination) and why this helps to protect the body. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Four polypeptide chains: two heavy and two light; held together by disulfide bonds; variable regions at the tips form two antigen-binding sites; constant region (same in all antibodies of that class); hinge region gives flexibility
    1 mark each for any four of: four polypeptides; two heavy and two light chains; disulfide bonds; variable region(s); antigen-binding sites (two); constant region; hinge region; Y-shape.
  2. (b) Each antibody has two antigen-binding sites so it can bind to antigens on two different bacteria, linking many together; clumps cannot spread easily and are easier for phagocytes to engulf
    1 mark: two binding sites link several bacteria. 1 mark: clumped bacteria easily engulfed by phagocytes / cannot spread/enter cells.
Q2Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which cells secrete antibodies? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A plasma cells
    Plasma cells are differentiated B-lymphocytes that make and secrete antibodies. T-helper cells (B) release cytokines that stimulate B-lymphocytes, but do not make antibodies themselves.
Q3Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Complete the comparison of B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes by stating, for each type: (i) where it matures, (ii) one way in which it helps to destroy pathogens. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) B-lymphocytes: mature in bone marrow; become plasma cells that secrete antibodies (humoral response). T-lymphocytes: mature in the thymus; T-helper cells release cytokines / T-killer (cytotoxic) cells destroy infected cells
    1 mark each: B – bone marrow; B – antibodies via plasma cells; T – thymus; T – cytokines from T-helper cells or killing infected cells by T-killer cells.
Q4Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define the term antigen. [1]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by clonal selection when a B-lymphocyte meets its specific antigen. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A molecule (usually a protein or glycoprotein, often on the surface of a cell or virus) that is recognised as foreign by the immune system and stimulates a specific immune response.
    1 mark: foreign (non-self) molecule that triggers an immune response, e.g. stimulates a lymphocyte to make a specific antibody.
  2. (b) Only the B-lymphocyte whose surface antibody (receptor) is complementary in shape to the antigen binds it; this cell is selected and divides by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells, some of which differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibody and some into memory cells.
    1 mark: only the lymphocyte with a complementary receptor binds the antigen and is selected; 1 mark: that cell divides by mitosis to make many identical cells (a clone); 1 mark: clone differentiates into plasma cells (antibody secretion) and memory cells.

  1. (a) Compare the antibody response to the first exposure with the response to the second exposure. Use the data in your answer. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate how many times greater the peak antibody concentration was after the second exposure. [1]
  3. (c) Explain the difference between the two responses. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Second response is faster (high levels within 7 days of the second exposure v about 14 days); much larger (peak 400 v 40 a.u.); lasts longer / antibody stays high
    1 mark each for any three, at least one with data: faster; greater; longer-lasting; quoted values (e.g. 400 v 40, peak at day 42 v day 14).
  2. (b) 10 times

  3. (c) Memory cells were made during the first response; on second exposure memory B-cells recognise the antigen quickly; they divide rapidly to produce many plasma cells, so more antibody is made sooner

  2. (b) HIV infects and destroys T-helper cells. Explain why people with untreated HIV infection become ill with other infections such as tuberculosis. [2]

  1. (a) T-helper cells: bind to antigen presented by macrophages; release cytokines; stimulate B-cells to divide/become plasma cells and stimulate macrophages/T-killer cells.   1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in antibody concentration between day 7 and day 14. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why there is no specific antibody at day 0, and why the concentration is still low at day 7. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 700 % (increase of 105 units on a base of 15: 105 ÷ 15 × 100)

Q12Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain why an antibody that binds to an antigen on the surface of the measles virus cannot bind to an antigen on the mumps virus. [3]
  2. (b) Some antibodies neutralise bacterial toxins. Suggest how binding of an antibody to a toxin molecule prevents it harming cells. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The antigen-binding site (variable region) has a specific shape determined by its amino acid sequence; it is complementary only to the shape of the measles antigen; the mumps antigen has a different shape so it cannot fit/bind
    1 mark each: variable region/antigen-binding site; specific amino acid sequence gives specific tertiary structure/shape; complementary to one antigen only (mumps antigen different shape). Do not credit 'active site' or 'receptor'.
  2. (b) The antibody covers the part of the toxin that binds to cell receptors / changes its shape, so it cannot attach to or enter body cells; antibody–toxin complexes are removed by phagocytes
    1 mark: blocks toxin's binding site/prevents entry into cells. 1 mark: complex engulfed by phagocytes.

Q14Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A bacterium has many different antigens and epitopes on its surface. Explain why this bacterium stimulates several different clones of B-lymphocytes, and why the mixture of antibodies it produces is more effective than a single antibody type. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each epitope has its own shape, so only the B-lymphocytes whose receptors are complementary to a given epitope bind and are selected; several clones therefore divide and produce different antibodies. Several different antibodies bound to the same bacterium can cause agglutination, opsonisation and neutralisation more effectively than one type, so the bacterium is removed more effectively.
    1 mark: different epitopes have different shapes; 1 mark: each is recognised by a different complementary receptor, so several clones are selected; 1 mark: the mixture of antibodies binds many sites on the bacterium; 1 mark: effective destruction (agglutination, opsonisation, phagocytosis or neutralisation).
Q15Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why T-killer (cytotoxic) cells destroy body cells infected with a virus but do not destroy free virus particles circulating in the blood. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) T-killer cells have receptors that bind to an antigen displayed on the infected cell surface together with an MHC class I protein. Free virus particles do not carry an MHC protein displaying the antigen in this way, so the T-killer cell cannot bind them; free viruses are instead bound and neutralised by antibodies and removed by phagocytes.
    1 mark: T-killer receptor binds viral antigen displayed with MHC class I on the infected cell; 1 mark: free virus does not present antigen with MHC so is not recognised by T-killer cells; 1 mark: free virus is dealt with by antibodies (neutralisation/opsonisation) and phagocytes.

    1 mark each for any two sensible reasons.
Q22Vaccination & types of immunity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

    1 mark each for any two of: different antigens at different life-cycle stages; intracellular (liver/red blood cells) so hidden from antibodies; antigenic variation; many strains.

Q25Vaccination & types of immunity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Tetanus vaccine contains a toxoid: tetanus toxin that has been chemically inactivated so that it no longer causes harm. Explain how the toxoid gives immunity to tetanus even though the vaccine contains no live Clostridium tetani bacteria. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The toxoid keeps the shape of the toxin's antigens, so it is recognised as foreign. The B-lymphocyte with a complementary receptor is selected and divides, and its plasma cells make antibody that neutralises the toxin.
