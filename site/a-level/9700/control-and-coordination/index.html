Q1Neurones & action potentials · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the function of the myelin sheath and name the cells that form it. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a resting potential of about −70 mV is maintained across the membrane of a neurone. [3]
  3. (c) State the direction in which an impulse travels along a motor neurone. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Insulates the axon so that depolarisation occurs only at the nodes of Ranvier, speeding up conduction; formed by Schwann cells.
    B1 insulation / speeds conduction (saltatory). B1 Schwann cells.
  2. (b) Na⁺/K⁺ pumps actively transport 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, using ATP; the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺ (K⁺ leak channels open), so K⁺ diffuses out; large negatively charged proteins/organic anions stay inside; so the inside is negative relative to the outside.
    1 mark each for any three of: Na⁺/K⁺ pump, 3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in; active transport / ATP; membrane more permeable to K⁺ / K⁺ diffuses out through open channels; few Na⁺ channels open so little Na⁺ diffuses back in; organic anions inside / inside more negative.
  3. (c) From the cell body (in the CNS) along the axon to the effector (muscle).
    B1
Q2Neurones & action potentials · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A recording electrode inside an axon gave these readings of membrane potential during one action potential:0 ms: −70 mV; 0.5 ms: −55 mV; 1.0 ms: +35 mV; 1.5 ms: −20 mV; 2.0 ms: −80 mV; 3.5 ms: −70 mV

  1. (a) State the resting potential of this axon. [1]
  2. (b) Identify the time interval during which the axon is depolarising most rapidly, and name the ion moving at this time and its direction. [2]
  3. (c) Explain the change in potential between 1.0 ms and 1.5 ms. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why the potential at 2.0 ms is lower than the resting potential. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) −70 mV
    B1 −70 mV (reading at 0 ms and again at 3.5 ms).
  2. (b) Between 0.5 ms and 1.0 ms; sodium ions (Na⁺) diffuse into the axon.
    B1 0.5–1.0 ms (−55 to +35 mV). B1 Na⁺ moving in, through voltage-gated sodium channels.
  3. (c) Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels close and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out, so the inside becomes less positive (repolarisation).
    1 mark each: Na⁺ channels close / K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out down its electrochemical gradient, removing positive charge.
  4. (d) Hyperpolarisation: K⁺ channels are slow to close, so too many K⁺ ions leave.
    B1 K⁺ channels stay open briefly after resting potential is reached, so extra K⁺ diffuses out (hyperpolarisation).
Q3Neurones & action potentials · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A dentist in Lahore injects a local anaesthetic, lidocaine, near a nerve in the lower jaw before filling a tooth. Lidocaine blocks voltage-gated sodium channels in neurone membranes.

  1. (a) State where voltage-gated sodium channels are concentrated in a myelinated neurone. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the patient feels no pain while the tooth is drilled. [3]
  3. (c) The patient's lip also feels numb and may droop slightly. Suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) At the nodes of Ranvier.
    B1 nodes of Ranvier (gaps between Schwann cells).
  2. (b) Na⁺ cannot enter the sensory neurone, so the membrane cannot depolarise to threshold; no action potentials are generated or conducted; no impulses reach the brain (sensory area), so pain is not perceived.
    1 mark each: no Na⁺ influx / no depolarisation; threshold not reached so no action potential; impulses do not reach the brain so no sensation.
  3. (c) Lidocaine also blocks sodium channels in other sensory and motor neurones in the area (e.g. to the lip skin and lip muscles).
    B1 nearby sensory neurones (numbness) and/or motor neurones (drooping) are also blocked, because all neurones use the same type of channel.

Q5Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the changes in the membrane that cause the depolarisation and repolarisation phases of an action potential. [4]
  2. (b) Explain the importance of the refractory period. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A stimulus opens some voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; when the threshold (about −50 mV) is reached, many more open and Na⁺ diffuses in rapidly, making the inside positive (to about +30 mV); the Na⁺ channels then close and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out, so the potential falls back (repolarisation) and overshoots (hyperpolarisation) before the resting potential is restored.
    1 mark each for any four of: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; threshold reached → more open (positive feedback); Na⁺ diffuses in → inside becomes positive; Na⁺ channels close; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out → repolarisation; hyperpolarisation, then K⁺ channels close.
  2. (b) During the refractory period a new action potential cannot be generated at that part of the membrane (Na⁺ channels inactive); this makes impulses travel in one direction only and keeps impulses separate (discrete), limiting the maximum frequency.
    B1 impulses travel in one direction only. B1 action potentials separate / discrete / limits frequency.
Q6Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Electrodes were placed 0.90 m apart along a myelinated motor neurone in a volunteer's arm. An action potential took 15 ms to travel between them.

  1. (a) Calculate the speed of conduction in m s⁻¹. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how myelination allows such a high speed of conduction. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why multiple sclerosis, in which the myelin sheath is damaged, causes muscle weakness. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 60 m s⁻¹
    0.90 ÷ 0.015 = 60
  2. (b) Myelin insulates the axon so ions cannot move across the membrane except at the nodes of Ranvier; depolarisation occurs only at the nodes; local circuits of current flow between nodes so the action potential 'jumps' from node to node (saltatory conduction), covering more distance per action potential.
    1 mark each for any three of: myelin insulates / prevents ion movement; depolarisation only at nodes of Ranvier; local circuits between nodes; saltatory conduction – impulse jumps; fewer depolarisations needed per length of axon.
  3. (c) Impulses are conducted more slowly or fail to reach the muscle, so muscle fibres are stimulated less.
    B1
Q7Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Concentrations of two ions inside and outside a resting giant axon (mmol dm⁻³):K⁺: inside 400, outside 20Na⁺: inside 50, outside 440

  1. (a) Calculate how many times more concentrated K⁺ is inside the axon than outside, and how many times more concentrated Na⁺ is outside than inside. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how these concentration differences are set up and how they lead to a resting potential with the inside negative. [3]
  3. (c) A respiratory inhibitor is added. Suggest what will happen to the resting potential over the next hour and explain why it does not disappear immediately. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) K⁺: 20 times; Na⁺: 8.8 times
    B1 400 ÷ 20 = 20. B1 440 ÷ 50 = 8.8.
  2. (b) Na⁺/K⁺ pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in; the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ (K⁺ leak channels) so K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ diffuses in; more positive charge leaves than enters, so the inside is negative.
    1 mark each for three of: active transport by Na⁺/K⁺ pump, ATP; 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in; more K⁺ channels open/membrane more permeable to K⁺; K⁺ diffuses out down its gradient; net loss of positive charge.
  3. (c) The resting potential gradually falls towards zero; no ATP so the pump stops, but the existing ion gradients take time to run down by diffusion.
    B1 resting potential gradually lost / becomes less negative. B1 pump stops (no ATP) but gradients already present only slowly dissipate by leakage.
Q8Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A basketball player in Karachi is 1.95 m tall. The sensory neurone from a touch receptor in the skin of his big toe to his spinal cord is 1.05 m long and conducts impulses at 60 m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the time taken for an impulse to travel the length of this neurone. Give your answer in ms. [2]
  2. (b) Describe how local currents cause an action potential to move along an unmyelinated axon. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why the action potential cannot move back towards the receptor. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 17.5 ms
    M1 t = 1.05 ÷ 60 = 0.0175 s. A1 17.5 ms.
  2. (b) Na⁺ entering at the depolarised region diffuses sideways inside the axon to the next region; this depolarises the next region to threshold; voltage-gated Na⁺ channels there open, creating a new action potential further along.
    1 mark each: Na⁺ ions diffuse along the inside (local circuit) to the adjacent resting region; this region is depolarised to threshold; voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open there, so the action potential is regenerated next to the previous one.
  3. (c) The region just behind is in its refractory period (Na⁺ channels cannot reopen yet).
    B1 the membrane just behind is refractory / Na⁺ channels inactivated.
Q9Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

  2. (b) Suggest two reasons why conduction speed increases with temperature. [2]

  1. (a) 125 %

  3. (c) Channel proteins and pump proteins are denatured.
    B1 proteins (channels/pumps) denature, so ion movement cannot be controlled.
Q10Neurones & action potentials · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain why conduction along a myelinated neurone uses less ATP per impulse than conduction along an unmyelinated neurone of the same length. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a high speed of conduction is an advantage in a motor neurone to the leg muscles of a cheetah. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ion movement occurs only at the nodes of Ranvier, so far fewer Na⁺ and K⁺ ions cross the membrane per impulse; fewer ions must then be pumped back by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump; less ATP is used.
    1 mark each: depolarisation only at nodes (saltatory conduction); fewer ions move in total; less active transport/pumping to restore gradients so less ATP.
  2. (b) Impulses reach the muscles quickly, so the muscles contract and respond rapidly; allows fast coordinated movement to catch prey.
    1 mark each: rapid response/short delay; fast coordinated muscle contraction for chasing prey.
Q11Neurones & action potentials · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Conduction speeds of axons from different animals:Unmyelinated, diameter 2 µm: 1.2 m s⁻¹Unmyelinated, diameter 20 µm: 4.0 m s⁻¹Myelinated, diameter 2 µm: 12 m s⁻¹Myelinated, diameter 20 µm: 100 m s⁻¹

  1. (a) Use the data to describe how myelination and axon diameter affect conduction speed. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why wider axons conduct impulses faster. [2]
  3. (c) The squid has some unmyelinated axons about 500 µm in diameter. Suggest an advantage of these giant axons to the squid, and why mammals do not need them. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Myelinated axons conduct much faster than unmyelinated axons of similar diameter (at 2 µm: 12 v 1.2 m s⁻¹; at 20 µm: 100 v 4.0 m s⁻¹); in both types, wider axons conduct faster.
    B1 myelinated faster, with data. B1 larger diameter → faster, in both.
  2. (b) A wider axon has a lower resistance to the flow of ions inside it (larger cross-sectional area of cytoplasm); so local currents spread further and faster along the axon, depolarising the next region sooner.
    B1 lower resistance / less resistance to ion flow. B1 local currents spread further / faster.
  3. (c) Giant axons conduct fast enough for a rapid escape response without myelin; mammals achieve high speed with narrow myelinated axons, so many more axons can fit in a small space (e.g. nerves, spinal cord).
    B1 fast escape response. B1 myelination gives speed with small diameter / saves space and energy.
Q12Neurones & action potentials · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

  2. (b) Explain how the nervous system distinguishes between a weak stimulus and a strong stimulus. [2]

Q13Neurones & action potentials · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Two toxins were applied separately to isolated axons.Tetrodotoxin (TTX), from pufferfish, blocks voltage-gated sodium channels.Tetraethylammonium (TEA) blocks voltage-gated potassium channels.

  1. (a) Predict and explain the effect of TTX on the response of the axon to a stimulus. [2]
  2. (b) Predict and explain the effect of TEA on the shape of an action potential. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why TEA would reduce the maximum frequency of impulses the axon can carry. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) No action potential; Na⁺ cannot enter, so no depolarisation beyond a tiny local change and threshold is never reached.
    B1 no action potential. B1 no Na⁺ influx so no depolarisation.
  2. (b) Depolarisation still occurs normally, but repolarisation is much slower so the action potential is prolonged/broader; K⁺ can only leave through leak channels; little or no hyperpolarisation.
    B1 depolarisation unaffected (Na⁺ channels work). B1 repolarisation slowed/action potential lasts longer. B1 K⁺ cannot leave through voltage-gated channels (only slow leakage); no hyperpolarisation.
  3. (c) Each action potential and refractory period lasts longer, so fewer can occur per second.
    B1 longer action potential/refractory period means a longer minimum gap between impulses.
Q14Neurones & action potentials · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Hyperkalaemia is a condition in which the concentration of K⁺ ions in the tissue fluid is higher than normal. It may occur in patients with kidney failure.

  1. (a) Explain why the resting potential of neurones becomes less negative in hyperkalaemia. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest how mild hyperkalaemia would affect the size of stimulus needed to produce an action potential. Explain your answer. [2]
  3. (c) Severe hyperkalaemia keeps membranes partly depolarised for a long time. Suggest why this can stop neurones firing altogether. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The K⁺ concentration gradient across the membrane is reduced; so fewer K⁺ ions diffuse out through K⁺ leak channels; less net loss of positive charge, so the inside is less negative.
    1 mark each: smaller K⁺ gradient (outside concentration higher); less K⁺ diffusion out; less positive charge lost, inside less negative.
  2. (b) A smaller stimulus is needed; the resting potential is already closer to the threshold (about −55 mV).
    B1 smaller stimulus. B1 less depolarisation needed to reach threshold.
  3. (c) Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels become inactivated when the membrane stays depolarised; they cannot reopen until the membrane repolarises, so no action potentials (like a permanent refractory period).
    1 mark each: sodium channels inactivated/stay closed; membrane cannot repolarise to reset channels so no new action potentials.
Q15Neurones & action potentials · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A nerve was stimulated at one point. Two recording electrodes were placed on the same nerve fibre, 45 mm and 105 mm from the stimulating electrode. The action potential reached the first electrode 0.9 ms after the stimulus and the second electrode 2.1 ms after the stimulus.

  1. (a) Calculate the speed of conduction between the two recording electrodes in m s⁻¹. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the speed is calculated from the two recording electrodes rather than from the stimulating electrode to the first recording electrode. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest, with a reason, whether this fibre is myelinated. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 50 m s⁻¹
    M1 distance 60 mm = 0.060 m; time 1.2 ms = 0.0012 s. A1 0.060 ÷ 0.0012 = 50 m s⁻¹.
  2. (b) There is a delay at the stimulating electrode before an action potential is set up; using the difference between two recordings removes this delay, so only conduction time is measured.
    1 mark each: delay/latency in starting the action potential at the stimulus site; difference cancels it out / measures only propagation time.
  3. (c) Myelinated; 50 m s⁻¹ is much faster than unmyelinated mammalian/amphibian fibres (about 0.5–10 m s⁻¹).
    B1 myelinated, because the speed is high (saltatory conduction).
