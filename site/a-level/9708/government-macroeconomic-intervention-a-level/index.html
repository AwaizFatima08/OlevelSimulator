Q1Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In an open economy with a government, the marginal propensity to save is 0.1, the marginal tax rate is 0.15 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.25. Government spending rises by $20bn. By how much will national income eventually rise? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B $40bn
    Multiplier = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. ΔY = 2 × 20 = $40bn. D uses only the MPS (1 ÷ 0.1 = 10), ignoring taxes and imports.

Q7Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two types of time lag that reduce the effectiveness of macroeconomic policy. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the implementation lag is usually shorter for monetary policy than for fiscal policy. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Recognition lag: data take time to collect and reveal the problem. Implementation (decision) lag: time to agree and introduce the policy. Effect lag: time before the policy changes spending and output.

  2. (b) An independent central bank can change interest rates at its next meeting, whereas tax and spending changes need a budget and approval by parliament.
    1 central bank decides quickly; 1 fiscal changes need legislative approval.

Q9Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In which situation is a cut in interest rates least likely to raise aggregate demand? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Interest rates are already close to zero and households and firms expect prices to fall.
    Near-zero rates with deflationary expectations is a liquidity trap: people hold extra money rather than spend it, and real interest rates stay high, so monetary policy has little effect. B, C and D are conditions in which a rate cut works strongly (investment, disposable income, depreciation).
Q10Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) According to the Laffer curve, which statement is correct? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C If the tax rate is above the revenue-maximising rate, a cut in the rate can increase tax revenue.
    The Laffer curve shows revenue rising and then falling as the rate rises, because high rates discourage work and encourage avoidance. Only above the revenue-maximising rate does a cut raise revenue. A is the common misreading: below that rate a cut reduces revenue.

Q13Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how progressive income taxes and unemployment benefits act as automatic stabilisers. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether automatic stabilisers are likely to be effective in an economy with a large informal sector. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a downturn incomes fall, so tax revenue falls more than proportionately and benefit payments rise automatically, cushioning disposable income and AD; in a boom tax revenue rises and benefits fall, restraining AD. No new decision is needed.
    1 recession: tax revenue falls; 1 benefit spending rises → supports AD; 1 boom: opposite effect dampens AD; 1 automatic, without discretionary changes.
  2. (b) Less effective: few workers pay income tax or receive unemployment benefits, so revenue and transfers change little with the cycle; the stabilising effect is weak, so discretionary policy is needed. But indirect taxes still vary with spending and cash-transfer programmes may help.
    Analysis (up to 3): narrow tax base, low benefit coverage, small size of government; Consideration (1+): sales taxes, targeted transfers, judgement.
Q14Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a cut in interest rates may affect the exchange rate and the current account of the balance of payments. [4]
  2. (b) Consider why the effect of the interest rate cut on the current account may be small. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Lower rates reduce returns on deposits in the currency → hot money outflows → lower demand for the currency → depreciation; exports become cheaper and imports dearer, which may improve the current account (if Marshall–Lerner holds), but higher domestic spending also raises imports.
    1 capital outflow/less demand for currency; 1 depreciation; 1 exports more price competitive, imports dearer; 1 net effect on current account depends on elasticities/higher income raising imports.
  2. (b) Demand for exports and imports may be price inelastic in the short run (J-curve); higher incomes from cheaper borrowing raise imports; other countries may also cut rates; exchange rates are affected by many other factors.
    1 reason; 1 explained; 1 judgement/second reason.

Q18Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a country with a fixed exchange rate and free movement of financial capital may be unable to use monetary policy to boost aggregate demand. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether fiscal policy is likely to be more effective in this situation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) If the central bank cuts interest rates, capital flows out, putting downward pressure on the currency; to keep the peg it must buy its own currency with reserves, which shrinks the money supply and pushes rates back up; so interest rates must stay in line with the anchor country.
    1 rate cut → outflows; 1 pressure on the peg; 1 intervention reverses the cut; 1 conclusion: monetary independence lost.
  2. (b) Yes: with interest rates fixed by the peg, extra government spending does not raise rates or cause appreciation, so crowding out is small; but fiscal policy is limited by debt levels and import leakages. Judgement.
    Up to 2 analysis; up to 2 evaluation.

    1 conflict named; 2 data used.
  3. (c) As expectations of inflation fell, the short-run Phillips curve shifted down, so lower inflation became consistent with lower unemployment; recovery in growth (2.3%) and possible supply-side improvements also helped.
    1 expectations; 1 SRPC shifts; 1 recovery/supply-side.
  4. (d) Benefits: inflation down 17 points, more stable environment, smaller budget deficit (7.5% → 4.1%). Costs: a recession, higher unemployment and lost output in Years 2–3. The costs seem temporary; long-run gains depend on whether low inflation persists. Judgement.

Q60Policy objectives and conflicts · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Invented data. A central bank's inflation target is 5% ± 1 percentage point. It forecasts inflation of 8% next year if its policy rate is unchanged, with unemployment at 6%. It estimates that each 1 percentage point rise in the policy rate lowers next year's inflation by 0.6 percentage points and raises unemployment by 0.3 percentage points.

  1. (a) Calculate the rise in the policy rate needed to bring inflation down to 5% next year, and the resulting unemployment rate. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rise in the policy rate needed to bring inflation down to 6%, the top of the target band, to two decimal places, and the resulting unemployment rate. [2]
  3. (c) Evaluate whether the central bank should aim for the middle or the top of its target band. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5 percentage points; unemployment 7.5%
    M1 (8 − 5) ÷ 0.6 = 5. A1 6 + 0.3 × 5 = 7.5%.
  2. (b) 3.33 percentage points; unemployment 7.0%
    M1 (8 − 6) ÷ 0.6 = 3.33. A1 6 + 0.3 × 3.33 = 7.0%.
  3. (c) Top of band: smaller rate rise, 0.5 point less unemployment, still within target; middle: stronger credibility and anchors expectations, reducing future costs. Depends on cause of inflation (supply shock may be temporary), reliability of estimates and lags. Judgement.
    Up to 2 analysis using results; up to 2 evaluation.
