Q1Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Printers and ink cartridges are complements. The price of printers falls sharply. What is most likely to happen in the market for ink cartridges?A price falls and quantity bought risesB price rises and quantity bought risesC price rises and quantity bought fallsD price and quantity bought are unchanged [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B price rises and quantity bought rises
    Cheaper printers → more printers bought → demand for cartridges shifts right → higher equilibrium price and quantity. A is wrong: it describes an increase in supply, not demand. D ignores the link between complements.
Q2Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The daily market for tomatoes in Karachi is described by:Qd = 240 − 4PQs = −30 + 5Pwhere P is the price in Rs per kg and Q is in thousand kg per day.

  1. (a) Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. [2]
  2. (b) The city government fixes the price at Rs 24 per kg. Calculate the excess demand at this price. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how, without price control, the price mechanism would remove a shortage in this market. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) P = Rs 30 per kg, Q = 120 thousand kg per day
    M1 240 − 4P = −30 + 5P → 9P = 270. A1 P = Rs 30, Q = 240 − 120 = 120 thousand kg.
  2. (b) Excess demand = 54 thousand kg per day
    M1 Qd = 240 − 96 = 144; Qs = −30 + 120 = 90. A1 excess demand = 54 thousand kg.
  3. (c) Buyers compete and bid the price up; higher price contracts quantity demanded and extends quantity supplied until the market clears at Rs 30.
    1 mark each: shortage leads buyers/sellers to raise price (signal); higher price gives incentive to sellers → extension of supply (movement along supply); higher price rations → contraction of demand; process continues until Qd = Qs at equilibrium.
Q3Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Beef and leather are in joint supply. Demand for beef from restaurants in Lahore rises. Other things equal, what happens in the leather market?A Supply of leather falls and its price risesB Demand for leather rises and its price risesC Supply of leather rises and its price fallsD Demand for leather falls and its price falls [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C Supply of leather rises and its price falls
    More cattle are slaughtered to meet beef demand, so more hides are produced as a by-product: the supply curve of leather shifts right, price falls and quantity rises. B confuses joint supply with joint demand; the demand for leather has not changed.
Q4Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data. In 2025 floods cut a South Asian country's cotton crop by 28%. The world price of raw cotton rose from 78 US cents per pound in 2024 to 96 US cents per pound in 2025.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in the world price of cotton between 2024 and 2025. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain, with the help of a demand and supply diagram, the likely effect of the rise in the cotton price on the market for polyester fibre. [3]
  3. (c) Cottonseed oil is produced from the seeds left after cotton is ginned. Consider the effect of the smaller cotton crop on the market for cottonseed oil. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) +23.1%
    M1 (96 − 78) ÷ 78 × 100. A1 23.1% (increase).
  2. (b) Polyester is a substitute: demand for polyester shifts right, raising its equilibrium price and quantity.
    1 polyester and cotton are substitutes (competitive demand); 1 demand curve for polyester shifts right (diagram with labelled axes P, Q and D1→D2); 1 equilibrium price and quantity both rise.
  3. (c) Joint supply: a smaller crop reduces supply of cottonseed, so supply of oil shifts left, price rises and quantity falls; the size depends on stocks, imports and PED.
    1 joint supply identified; 1 supply of cottonseed oil shifts left → price up, quantity down; 1 consideration, e.g. effect smaller if stocks held or imports of other edible oils available, or if demand for the oil is price elastic as other cooking oils substitute.
Q5Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the market for a good is linked to the markets for its complements and for goods produced in joint supply with it, and consider whether a fall in the price of a good will always change the equilibrium in a related market. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Complements: lower price of good A raises demand for complement B (P and Q of B rise). Joint supply: changes in output of A change supply of its by-product. A related market changes only if the price fall causes a change in quantity of A and the link is strong.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of complements (joint demand), joint supply, a related market. Analysis: price of A falls → extension of demand for A → demand for complement shifts right (diagram); if price of A falls because of lower demand, output of A falls → supply of joint product shifts left → its price rises (diagram). Evaluation: effect depends on how strong the link is (cross elasticity), on the PED of A (if inelastic, little extra quantity), on whether the fall in price is caused by demand or supply, and on time; a weakly related good will hardly change. Judgement: not always, and the size varies.
Q6Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

The Punjab government is considering a subsidy for each electric motorcycle sold in Lahore. Most motorcycles in the city currently run on petrol.

  1. (a) Identify the relationship between electric motorcycles and electricity, and between electric motorcycles and petrol motorcycles. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether a government subsidy for electric motorcycles in Lahore will reduce the price of petrol. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Electric motorcycles and electricity: complements (joint demand). Electric and petrol motorcycles: substitutes (competitive demand).
    1 mark for each correct relationship.
  2. (b) Possibly a small fall in petrol demand, but the effect on the petrol price is likely to be small: Pakistan's petrol price depends on world oil prices, the exchange rate, taxes and government pricing, and motorcycles are only part of petrol demand.
    Analysis (up to 4): subsidy lowers price of e-motorcycles → more bought → fewer petrol motorcycles bought (substitutes) → demand for petrol falls → leftward shift of petrol demand → lower equilibrium price, other things equal; also demand for electricity rises (complement). Evaluation (up to 2): petrol price is largely set by world crude price and government levy, not local demand; motorcycles are a small share of total fuel use; existing petrol bikes last years so effect is slow; supply of petrol may be elastic (imports) so price change is small; judgement needed.
Q7Market equilibrium and interrelated markets · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The market for fitness bands in Dubai is described by Qd = 500 − 10P and Qs = 50 + 5P, where P is the price in US$ and Q is in thousands per month.

  1. (a) Calculate the original equilibrium price and quantity. [2]
  2. (b) Rising incomes change demand to Qd = 560 − 10P and a fall in component costs changes supply to Qs = 80 + 5P. Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why, when demand and supply both increase, the direction of the change in equilibrium price cannot be predicted without knowing the size of each shift. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) P = $30, Q = 200 thousand per month
    M1 500 − 10P = 50 + 5P → 15P = 450. A1 P = $30, Q = 200 thousand.
  2. (b) P = $32, Q = 240 thousand per month
    M1 560 − 10P = 80 + 5P → 15P = 480. A1 P = $32, Q = 240 thousand.
  3. (c) Both shifts raise quantity, but an increase in demand raises price while an increase in supply lowers it, so the net price change depends on which shift is larger.
    1 increase in demand alone → higher price and quantity; 1 increase in supply alone → lower price, higher quantity; 1 so quantity definitely rises but price rises only if the demand shift is larger (here demand rose by 60 thousand at each price, supply by 30 thousand, so price rose).
Q8Income and cross elasticity of demand · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A household's monthly income rises from Rs 40,000 to Rs 46,000. The number of restaurant meals it buys in a year rises from 200 to 236. What is its income elasticity of demand for restaurant meals, and what kind of good are they?A 0.83, a normal good that is a necessityB 1.2, a normal good that is income elasticC −1.2, an inferior goodD 1.2, an inferior good [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 1.2, a normal good that is income elastic
    %ΔQ = 36/200 = 18%; %ΔY = 6,000/40,000 = 15%; YED = 18 ÷ 15 = +1.2. Positive and greater than 1 → normal, income elastic (luxury). A inverts the formula (15 ÷ 18).
Q9Income and cross elasticity of demand · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A supermarket in Islamabad raises the price of loose tea from Rs 1,200 to Rs 1,380 per kg. Over the next month its sales of instant coffee rise from 5,000 to 5,450 jars.

  1. (a) Calculate the cross elasticity of demand for instant coffee with respect to the price of tea. [2]
  2. (b) State what the sign of your answer shows about the two goods. [1]
  3. (c) Explain one way the supermarket could use this information. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) XED = +0.6
    M1 %ΔQ coffee = 450/5,000 = 9%; %ΔP tea = 180/1,200 = 15%. A1 9 ÷ 15 = +0.6.
  2. (b) Positive: they are substitutes.
    1 positive XED → substitutes.
  3. (c) e.g. when tea prices rise, stock more coffee / raise coffee price slightly, as demand for coffee will increase.
    1 use identified (stocking, pricing, promotion); 1 linked to the positive but low XED (coffee demand rises only modestly, by 0.6% per 1% rise in tea price).
Q10Income and cross elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Invented estimates of income elasticity of demand (YED) for goods bought by households in Pakistan:Wheat flour: 0.2Motorcycles: 1.8Second-hand clothing: −0.4Private tuition: 1.3

  1. (a) Identify the inferior good in the table and explain how you know. [2]
  2. (b) Real household incomes are forecast to fall by 5%. Calculate the percentage change in demand for motorcycles and for second-hand clothing. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how a motorcycle manufacturer could use the YED estimate when planning production. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Second-hand clothing: its YED is negative (−0.4).
    1 second-hand clothing; 1 negative YED means demand falls as income rises.
  2. (b) Motorcycles: -9%; second-hand clothing: +2%
    1 motorcycles 1.8 × (−5) = −9%; 1 second-hand clothing −0.4 × (−5) = +2%.
  3. (c) With YED 1.8, sales are very sensitive to income: cut planned output/stocks and staff hiring when incomes are expected to fall, expand when growth is expected; consider cheaper models.
    1 income elastic, so demand changes more than proportionately with income; 1 plan output, stock or capacity in line with forecast income (e.g. reduce output about 9% if incomes fall 5%); 1 further use, e.g. diversify into lower-priced models or export markets with rising incomes.
Q11Income and cross elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A games company estimates that the cross elasticity of demand between its consoles and its games is −2.5. It sells 40,000 games a month and plans to cut the price of its consoles by 8%.

  1. (a) Calculate the expected percentage change in sales of games and the new monthly sales. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether cutting the price of its consoles is likely to increase the firm's total profit. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) +20%; 48,000 games a month
    M1 −2.5 × (−8%) = +20%. A1 40,000 × 1.2 = 48,000.
  2. (b) Probably, if the extra profit on games (strong complements, XED −2.5) outweighs lower revenue per console; it depends on console PED, margins on games and competitors' reactions.
    Analysis (up to 3): strong complements → large rise in game sales; games often have higher profit margins than consoles; extra console buyers also buy games for years. Consideration (1+): lower revenue per console may cause losses on hardware; XED is an estimate and may change; rivals may also cut prices; judgement.
Q12Income and cross elasticity of demand · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether income elasticity of demand is more useful than cross elasticity of demand to a firm planning its production for the next five years. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) A balanced judgement: YED is usually more useful for long-term planning because incomes change predictably over five years, but XED matters more in markets with close rivals or complements.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides with a diagram where relevant and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. YED points: measures response to income; normal/inferior/luxury; over five years real income growth forecasts allow capacity, product range and market choice (e.g. a firm with YED > 1 expands as the economy grows; inferior-good producers plan for decline). XED points: substitutes and complements; helps predict effect of rivals' price changes and pricing of complementary products; useful for bundling and competition strategy. Evaluation: estimates are based on past data and may change; income forecasts are uncertain; XED changes as new substitutes appear; usefulness depends on market structure and type of product; PED and PES may also matter; both are needed. Judgement must answer 'more useful'.
Q13Price elasticity of demand · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A cinema in Islamabad cuts its ticket price from Rs 1,000 to Rs 800. Weekly ticket sales rise from 2,500 to 3,250.

  1. (a) Calculate the price elasticity of demand for the tickets. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the change in the cinema's weekly total revenue. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how your answer to (b) is linked to your answer to (a). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) PED = -1.5
    M1 %ΔQ = +30%, %ΔP = −20%. A1 30 ÷ (−20) = −1.5.
  2. (b) Increase of Rs 100,000
    M1 Rs 2,500,000 before; Rs 2,600,000 after. A1 + Rs 100,000.
  3. (c) Demand is price elastic (|PED| > 1), so a price cut raises quantity by a larger percentage and total revenue rises.
    1 elastic demand; 1 percentage rise in quantity outweighs percentage fall in price → TR rises.
Q14Price elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A bus company raises its fares by 20%. Its total revenue from fares rises by 8%. What is the price elasticity of demand for its bus journeys?A −0.4B −0.5C −0.6D −2.5 [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B −0.5
    New TR = 1.2 × new Q = 1.08 × old TR, so new Q = 1.08 ÷ 1.2 = 0.9 of old Q: a 10% fall. PED = −10 ÷ 20 = −0.5. A wrongly divides the revenue change by the price change (8 ÷ 20).
Q15Price elasticity of demand · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the price elasticity of demand for petrol is likely to be lower in the short run than in the long run. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether a government should use price elasticity of demand when deciding which goods to tax. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Short run: few substitutes, vehicles already owned, habits and commuting fixed → inelastic. Long run: people switch to fuel-efficient or electric vehicles, public transport, move home/work → more elastic.
    Up to 2 for short-run reasons (lack of close substitutes, necessity for work travel, existing petrol vehicles); up to 2 for long-run reasons (time to find substitutes such as CNG/electric vehicles, car-sharing, relocation) with link to responsiveness.
  2. (b) Yes for revenue (tax inelastic goods raises revenue with small fall in sales) and to judge the effect on consumption; but inelastic goods are often necessities, so tax may be regressive, and PED estimates are uncertain.
    Analysis (up to 3): inelastic demand → tax revenue high and stable, burden mostly on consumers; elastic demand → large fall in consumption (useful for demerit goods only if elastic). Consideration (1+): equity/regressive effect on poor; aim of tax matters (revenue vs reducing consumption); PED changes over time; judgement.
Q16Price elasticity of demand · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The demand for a brand of bottled water is Qd = 120 − 2P, where P is the price in Rs and Q is in thousand bottles per week.

  1. (a) Calculate the price elasticity of demand when the price rises from Rs 10 to Rs 12. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the price elasticity of demand when the price rises from Rs 50 to Rs 55. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the price elasticity of demand changes along this demand curve even though its slope is constant. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) PED = -0.2
    M1 Q falls from 100 to 96: −4%; P rises 20%. A1 −4 ÷ 20 = −0.2.
  2. (b) PED = -5
    M1 Q falls from 20 to 10: −50%; P rises 10%. A1 −50 ÷ 10 = −5.
  3. (c) PED depends on percentage changes, not absolute ones. The same absolute change in Q is a small percentage at high quantities and a large percentage at low quantities, while the opposite holds for price.
    1 slope measures absolute change (ΔP/ΔQ) but PED uses percentage changes; 1 at low prices/high quantities, %ΔQ is small and %ΔP large → inelastic; 1 at high prices/low quantities, %ΔQ large and %ΔP small → elastic (PED moves from 0 towards −∞ up the curve).
Q17Price elasticity of demand · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the factors that determine the price elasticity of demand for a product and consider whether a firm selling a branded product, such as a soft drink, can make the demand for its product more price inelastic. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Determinants: number and closeness of substitutes, proportion of income, necessity/habit, time period, definition of the market. A firm can reduce substitutability through branding, advertising and loyalty, but success is limited by rivals' actions.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definition and formula of PED; determinants listed. Analysis: explain how each determinant changes responsiveness (e.g. close substitutes → consumers switch when price rises → elastic). Application: brand advertising, product differentiation, loyalty schemes, exclusive distribution reduce perceived substitutes → steeper demand curve, allowing price rises that raise revenue. Evaluation: rivals also advertise; advertising is costly; consumers of soft drinks are price-sensitive and many substitutes exist; effect may be temporary; judgement.

Q19Demand and supply curves · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a change in quantity supplied and a change in supply. [2]
  2. (b) State two events that would shift the supply curve of mangoes in Multan to the left. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Change in quantity supplied: movement along the supply curve caused by a change in the good's own price. Change in supply: shift of the whole curve caused by a non-price factor.
    1 each correct distinction.
  2. (b) Any two: a heatwave or pest damaging the crop; higher fertiliser, diesel or labour costs; a new indirect tax; growers switching land to more profitable crops.
    1 each, up to 2.
Q20Demand and supply curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Weekly demand for milk (litres) by three households in a street:Price Rs 200: household X 7, Y 5, Z 4Price Rs 220: X 6, Y 5, Z 3Price Rs 240: X 5, Y 4, Z 2

  1. (a) Calculate the market demand at each price. [2]
  2. (b) A fourth household moves into the street. It buys 3 litres a week at each of the three prices. Calculate the new market demand at Rs 220 and state what happens to the market demand curve. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why the market demand curve slopes downwards. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 200: 16 litres; Rs 220: 14 litres; Rs 240: 11 litres
    Horizontal summation. 2 marks all three correct; 1 mark two correct.
  2. (b) 17 litres; the market demand curve shifts to the right.
    1 14 + 3 = 17; 1 rightward shift (more demanded at every price).
  3. (c) e.g. as price falls, the good becomes cheaper relative to substitutes (substitution effect) and consumers' real income rises (income effect), so more is demanded; new buyers enter the market at lower prices.
    1 reason identified; 1 explained.
Q21Demand and supply curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The supply of plastic chairs from factories near Gujranwala is Qs = −40 + 8P, where P is the price in hundreds of rupees (so P = 20 means Rs 2,000) and Q is in thousand chairs per month. A rise in electricity tariffs raises the cost of producing each chair by Rs 500 (5 in these units), shifting the supply curve upwards by this amount.

  1. (a) Calculate the quantity supplied at a price of Rs 2,000 per chair (P = 20) before and after the increase in costs. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two factors other than costs of production that could shift the supply curve for a manufactured good. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before: 120 thousand chairs per month; after: 80 thousand chairs per month
    1 Qs = −40 + 8 × 20 = 120; 1 new supply Qs = −40 + 8(P − 5), so at P = 20, Qs = −40 + 120 = 80 thousand chairs.
  2. (b) Any two explained, e.g. improved technology (more output from same inputs → shift right); indirect taxes or subsidies; number of firms in the market; weather/natural events; prices of other goods the firms could produce.
    Up to 2 marks for each factor: 1 identified, 1 explained with direction of shift.
Q22Demand and supply curves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data. Over one year the average price of cooking oil in Pakistan rose by 35% while the quantity bought fell by 5%.

  1. (a) Explain whether these data are consistent with a downward-sloping demand curve. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether the rise in price was more likely to result from a change in demand or a change in supply. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Yes: a higher price with a lower quantity bought fits a movement up along a downward-sloping demand curve, e.g. caused by a fall in supply.
    1 price up, quantity down = movement along D; 1 caused by a leftward shift of supply.
  2. (b) More likely a fall in supply (higher import costs, depreciation, taxes) because quantity fell; an increase in demand would raise both price and quantity. Demand may also have risen, but supply effect dominated.
    Analysis (up to 4): increase in demand alone → P and Q both rise, so not consistent with Q falling; decrease in supply → P up, Q down, consistent; likely causes: higher world palm-oil prices, rupee depreciation, import duties; the small fall in Q (5%) relative to the 35% price rise suggests inelastic demand. Evaluation (up to 2): both curves may have shifted (population growth raising demand while supply fell more); data cover a year and other factors change; judgement.
Q23Consumer and producer surplus · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Five fans, A to E, want one ticket each for a qawwali concert. The most each is willing to pay is: A Rs 900, B Rs 800, C Rs 700, D Rs 600, E Rs 500.

  1. (a) Define consumer surplus. [2]
  2. (b) The tickets are priced at Rs 650. Calculate the total consumer surplus. [2]
  3. (c) The organiser cuts the price to Rs 550. Calculate the new total consumer surplus. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The difference between the maximum amount consumers are willing to pay for a good and the price they actually pay.
    1 willingness to pay; 1 minus the market price (area below demand curve and above price).
  2. (b) Rs 450
    M1 buyers A, B, C buy: (900 − 650) + (800 − 650) + (700 − 650). A1 Rs 450.
  3. (c) Rs 800
    M1 A, B, C, D buy: 350 + 250 + 150 + 50. A1 Rs 800.
Q24Consumer and producer surplus · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a market, demand is P = 50 − 0.5Q and supply is P = 14 + 0.3Q, where P is in $ and Q in units. What is consumer surplus at equilibrium?A $506.25B $303.75C $1,012.50D $810.00 [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A $506.25
    50 − 0.5Q = 14 + 0.3Q → Q = 45, P = $27.50. CS = ½ × 45 × (50 − 27.50) = $506.25. B is producer surplus, ½ × 45 × (27.50 − 14).
Q25Consumer and producer surplus · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

In the market for rooftop solar panels in Pakistan, demand is P = 120 − Q and supply is P = 20 + Q, where P is in Rs thousand per panel and Q is in thousand panels per month. Cheaper imported panels then change supply to P = Q.

  1. (a) Calculate the consumer surplus and producer surplus before the change in supply. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the new consumer surplus and the change in consumer surplus. [2]
  3. (c) In this example producer surplus also rises. Explain why an increase in supply does not always raise producer surplus. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) CS = Rs 1,250 million; PS = Rs 1,250 million
    M1 equilibrium 120 − Q = 20 + Q → Q = 50, P = 70. A1 CS = ½ × 50 × 50 = 1,250; PS = ½ × 50 × 50 = 1,250 (Rs million).
  2. (b) New CS = Rs 1,800 million; increase of Rs 550 million
    M1 120 − Q = Q → Q = 60, P = 60; CS = ½ × 60 × 60 = 1,800. A1 change +550.
  3. (c) Price falls, which cuts surplus on units already sold; extra units add surplus. If demand is price inelastic, the price fall is large and PS can fall.
    1 lower price reduces PS on existing output; 1 extra output and lower costs add PS; 1 net effect depends on PED / size of price fall (inelastic demand → PS may fall).
Q26Consumer and producer surplus · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether an increase in demand for a product always benefits both consumers and producers. Use the concepts of consumer surplus and producer surplus in your answer. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Producers gain (higher price and output → PS rises). Consumers: total CS may rise or fall — existing buyers pay more, but more buyers enter; outcome depends on PES and the cause of the demand increase. Not always both.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides with a diagram where relevant and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Analysis: diagram with D shifting right; PS clearly rises (higher P and Q); CS: original buyers lose from higher price, new demand curve gives new CS triangle — with a parallel shift and elastic supply CS rises; with perfectly inelastic supply price rises and CS of original buyers falls. Consider cause: tastes (welfare of new buyers counts), population growth. Evaluation: depends on PES (time period, spare capacity), size of shift, whether a maximum price or monopoly exists; distribution between consumer groups; short run vs long run as supply responds. Judgement: producers almost always gain; consumers only sometimes.
Q27Price elasticity of supply · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A supply curve is a straight line that passes through the origin. What is its price elasticity of supply?A 0 at every priceB equal to 1 at every priceC greater than 1 at every priceD different at every point on the curve [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B equal to 1 at every price
    Along a straight line from the origin, Q is proportional to P, so any percentage change in price gives the same percentage change in quantity: PES = 1. D is the common trap: the slope can differ between such lines but PES is still 1.
Q28Price elasticity of supply · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The wholesale price of chicken in Lahore rises from Rs 600 to Rs 690 per kg. Over the next fortnight the quantity supplied rises from 20,000 kg to 21,500 kg per day.

  1. (a) Calculate the price elasticity of supply of chicken. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why the supply of chicken is price inelastic in the short run. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) PES = 0.5
    M1 %ΔQs = 1,500/20,000 = 7.5%; %ΔP = 90/600 = 15%. A1 0.5.
  2. (b) Any two explained: time needed to raise birds (several weeks); limited shed capacity; chicken is perishable, so stocks cannot be held; feed and chicks must be ordered in advance.
    Up to 2 each: reason identified + explained link to slow response of quantity supplied.
Q29Price elasticity of supply · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Define price elasticity of supply. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether the price elasticity of supply of manufactured goods is always greater than that of agricultural goods. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the price of the good: %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP.
    1 responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price; 1 formula.
  2. (b) Usually yes in the short run (factories can add shifts, use stocks; crops have long growing periods and are perishable), but not always: factories at full capacity, complex goods like aircraft or chips have very inelastic supply, while some farm products (poultry, vegetables in greenhouses, stored grain) can respond quickly.
    Analysis (up to 4): factors – time to produce, spare capacity, stocks, factor mobility; manufacturing usually has stocks and spare capacity → elastic; agriculture has growing seasons, perishability, land constraints → inelastic. Evaluation (up to 2): counter-examples (full capacity, specialised inputs, semiconductors; stored wheat, fast-growing crops); depends on time period; judgement 'not always'.
Q30Functions of the price mechanism · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the signalling, incentive and rationing functions of the price mechanism and consider how well these functions work in the market for rented housing in a fast-growing city such as Karachi. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Signalling: rising rents show scarcity. Incentive: higher rents encourage building and letting. Rationing: rents allocate flats to those willing and able to pay. In housing the functions work slowly because supply is inelastic, information is poor and the poor are priced out.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of the three functions. Analysis: increase in demand from migration → rents rise (signal) → landlords and developers have an incentive to build/convert property (extension of supply) → higher rents ration the existing stock. Evaluation: supply of housing is inelastic in the short run (land, planning permission, construction time); informal settlements and incomplete information; rationing by ability to pay leaves low-income families without decent housing, which may be inequitable; rent controls distort signals; judgement on 'how well'.
