Q1Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A consumer price index (2024 = 100) was 112.0 in 2025 and 120.4 in 2026. What was the rate of inflation in 2026?A 7.0%B 7.5%C 8.4%D 20.4% [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 7.5%
    (120.4 − 112.0) ÷ 112.0 × 100 = 7.5%. C (8.4%) is the change in index points, not a percentage of the 2025 level; D measures the change since the base year.
Q2Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Invented data for a consumer price index:Food: weight 40, price change +10%Housing and utilities: weight 30, price change +5%Transport: weight 20, price change +15%Other items: weight 10, price change −2%

  1. (a) Calculate the value of the price index at the end of the year (start of year = 100). [3]
  2. (b) Explain why weights are used when calculating a consumer price index. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 108.3
    M1 multiply each price change by its weight: 40 × 10, 30 × 5, 20 × 15, 10 × (−2). M1 sum = 830, ÷ 100 weights = 8.3. A1 index = 108.3.
  2. (b) Households spend different shares of income on different items, so a price change in a large item (food) affects the cost of living more than one in a small item.
    1 weights reflect share of household spending; 1 so the index measures change in cost of a typical basket accurately.
Q3Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define deflation. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two possible consequences of deflation for an economy. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A sustained fall in the general price level, so the rate of inflation is negative.
    1 sustained/persistent fall; 1 in the general (average) price level.
  2. (b) Any two explained, e.g. consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices → lower AD and output; real value of debts rises → borrowers cut spending, more defaults; firms' revenues fall → wage cuts or job losses; exports become more price competitive.
    Up to 2 each: consequence identified + explained.
Q4Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a country the annual rate of inflation falls from 9% to 4%. Which statement must be correct?A The general price level has fallen.B The general price level is still rising, but more slowly.C The purchasing power of money has risen.D The economy is experiencing deflation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B The general price level is still rising, but more slowly.
    Inflation of 4% is still a rise in the average price level, just a slower one (disinflation). A and D confuse falling inflation with falling prices; C is wrong because money still loses value, only more slowly.
Q5Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Invented data. A factory worker in Faisalabad earned Rs 50,000 a month in 2025 and Rs 56,000 a month in 2026. The consumer price index rose from 100 in 2025 to 118 in 2026.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in the worker's money wage. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the worker's real wage in 2026 at 2025 prices, to the nearest rupee, and the percentage change in the real wage. [2]
  3. (c) Explain what has happened to the worker's standard of living. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) +12%
    1 (56,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 12%.
  2. (b) Rs 47,458; a fall of 5.1%
    M1 56,000 ÷ 1.18 = 47,458. A1 (47,458 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 = -5.1%.
  3. (c) It has fallen: prices rose by more than the money wage, so the wage buys about 5% fewer goods and services.
    1 real wage/purchasing power fell; 1 because inflation (18%) exceeded the money wage rise (12%).
Q6Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. [4]
  2. (b) Consider which type of inflation is more likely in an oil-importing economy when the world price of crude oil rises by 40%. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than the economy's capacity grows → price level rises with higher real output. Cost-push: SRAS shifts left due to higher costs → price level rises while real output falls.
    1 demand-pull: AD increases (e.g. consumer or government spending) as output nears full capacity; 1 diagram AD1→AD2, higher P and Y; 1 cost-push: higher costs (wages, imported raw materials) shift AS left; 1 diagram AS1→AS2, higher P and lower Y.
  2. (b) Mainly cost-push: higher fuel and transport costs shift AS left. But demand-pull elements are possible if wages rise in response or the government expands spending; effect depends on oil's share of costs and the exchange rate.
    Analysis (up to 3): oil is an input for transport, electricity and manufacturing → costs rise → SRAS left → cost-push; import bill rises, which may reduce AD (more spending on imports), dampening demand-pull. Consideration (1+): second-round wage-price spiral; subsidies to fuel; currency movements; judgement.
Q7Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a high rate of inflation is always more harmful to an economy than deflation. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always. High inflation erodes purchasing power, harms savers and fixed-income groups, reduces competitiveness and creates uncertainty; but persistent deflation can delay spending, raise real debt burdens and cause unemployment. Harm depends on the size, cause and whether it is anticipated.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides with a diagram where relevant and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Inflation costs: menu and shoe-leather costs, redistribution from savers/lenders to borrowers, fiscal drag, loss of international competitiveness, uncertainty discouraging investment, hyperinflation risk. Deflation costs: postponed consumption, rising real value of debt, falling profits → unemployment, real interest rates rise when nominal rates can't fall below zero, deflationary spiral. Evaluation: 'good' deflation from productivity gains (AS shift right) vs 'bad' deflation from falling AD; mild, anticipated inflation is less harmful than unanticipated high inflation; effects differ for different groups; the context (debt levels, indexation). Judgement must address 'always'.
Q8Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data for Country K:Year: 2024, 2025, 2026Inflation rate (%): 25, 12, 6Central bank policy interest rate (%): 22, 18, 11

  1. (a) Calculate the price level at the end of 2026, taking the end of 2023 as 100. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Using real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate, calculate the real interest rate in 2024 and in 2026. [2]
  3. (c) Consider whether the fall in inflation from 2024 to 2026 shows that the economy is performing well. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 148.4
    M1 100 × 1.25 × 1.12 × 1.06. A1 148.4 (not 100 + 25 + 12 + 6 = 143).
  2. (b) 2024: -3%; 2026: 5%
    1 2024: 22 − 25 = −3%; 1 2026: 11 − 6 = +5%.
  3. (c) It helps (more stable prices, real interest rates now positive, which protects savers and confidence), but prices are still rising, high real interest rates may cut investment, output and employment, so other indicators are needed.
    Analysis (up to 3): lower inflation reduces uncertainty, improves competitiveness, protects real incomes; prices still rose ~48% over the three years, so living costs remain much higher; positive real rate of 5% discourages borrowing → lower AD, growth and jobs. Consideration (1+): need data on growth, unemployment, current account; judgement.
Q9Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In an economy, consumption is $600bn, investment $150bn, government spending $200bn, exports $250bn and imports $300bn. What is aggregate demand?A $600bnB $900bnC $1,200bnD $1,500bn [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B $900bn
    AD = C + I + G + (X − M) = 600 + 150 + 200 + (250 − 300) = $900bn. D adds imports instead of subtracting them.
Q10Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the aggregate demand curve slopes downwards from left to right. [3]
  2. (b) State two causes of a rightward shift of the long-run aggregate supply curve. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A lower price level raises real wealth (real balance effect), lowers interest rates (more borrowing and spending) and makes exports cheaper relative to imports, so real expenditure rises.
    1 mark each for any three explained effects: wealth/real balance effect; interest rate effect; international trade (competitiveness) effect.
  2. (b) Any two: more or better-quality labour (migration, education, training); more capital (investment); new technology; discovery of natural resources; supply-side reforms raising productivity.
    1 each.
Q11Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain the effect of a fall in consumer confidence on the price level and real output. [4]
  2. (b) State two components of aggregate demand other than consumption. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Households spend less and save more → consumption falls → AD shifts left → lower price level and lower real output.
    1 consumption falls; 1 AD shifts left (diagram with labelled axes); 1 price level falls (or rises more slowly); 1 real output falls.
  2. (b) Any two: investment; government spending; net exports (exports minus imports).
    1 each.
Q12Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which change would shift the short-run aggregate supply curve to the left?A a rise in consumer confidenceB a fall in the world price of oilC a rise in money wage rates with no change in productivityD a cut in the rate of income tax [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C a rise in money wage rates with no change in productivity
    Higher wages without higher productivity raise unit labour costs, so firms supply less at each price level: SRAS shifts left. A and D shift AD, not SRAS; B lowers costs and shifts SRAS to the right.
Q13Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data for Country Q, $bn at constant prices:2025: C 420, I 95, G 130, X 160, M 1752026: C 438, I 88, G 150, X 172, M 186

  1. (a) Calculate aggregate demand in 2025 and 2026 and the percentage change. Give the percentage to one decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Identify the component whose change made the largest contribution to the change in AD. [1]
  3. (c) With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain the likely effect of this change in AD on the price level and real output. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2025: $630bn; 2026: $662bn; +5.1%
    1 2025: 420 + 95 + 130 + 160 − 175 = 630; 1 2026: 662; 1 (662 − 630) ÷ 630 = 5.1%.
  2. (b) Government spending (+$20bn).
    1 G rose by 20 (C +18; I −8; net exports 160−175 = −15 to 172−186 = −14, +1).
  3. (c) AD shifts right: real output rises and the price level rises; the split depends on spare capacity (flat section → mainly output, near full capacity → mainly prices).
    1 AD shifts right (diagram labelled price level and real output/real GDP); 1 real output rises; 1 price level rises; 1 relative size depends on how close the economy is to full capacity / shape of AS.
Q14Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a large rise in world oil prices shifts the short-run aggregate supply curve of an oil-importing economy. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether a large rise in world oil prices is likely to reduce real output more than it raises the price level in an oil-importing economy. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Oil is a key input in transport, power and manufacturing, so firms' costs of production rise and they supply less at each price level: SRAS shifts left.
    1 higher costs of production; 1 SRAS shifts left (upwards).
  2. (b) Both effects occur (stagflation). Which is larger depends on the slope of AD and AS, whether AD also falls as the import bill rises, policy responses and how long the shock lasts.
    Analysis (up to 4): SRAS left → higher price level and lower real output; AD may also fall because spending leaks to imports and real incomes fall → output falls further while dampening the price rise; if AD is steep/inelastic, effect is mainly on price; second-round wage rises push prices further. Evaluation (up to 2): depends on oil intensity, fuel subsidies, monetary policy response (rate rises cut output more), exchange rate, whether the shock is temporary; judgement.
Q15Aggregate demand and aggregate supply · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how an increase in investment affects both aggregate demand and aggregate supply, and consider whether its effect on the price level is likely to be inflationary. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Investment is a component of AD (short run: AD right) and adds to productive capacity (long run: LRAS right). Price level rises in the short run but may be stable or fall in the long run if capacity rises enough.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: investment as spending on capital goods; AD = C + I + G + (X − M); LRAS/capacity. Analysis: higher I → AD shifts right → higher price level and output (with multiplier); new capital raises productivity → LRAS shifts right → lower price level, higher output. Evaluation: timing (AD effect immediate, AS effect delayed); type of investment (replacement vs new capacity); spare capacity; size of the shifts; judgement on whether net effect is inflationary.
Q16Economic growth · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A country's nominal GDP rises from $800bn to $880bn in a year. Over the same year the GDP deflator rises from 100 to 106. What is the rate of real economic growth, correct to one decimal place?A 3.8%B 4.0%C 10.0%D 16.6% [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 3.8%
    Real GDP = 880 ÷ 1.06 = $830.2bn (at last year's prices); (830.2 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 3.8%. B uses the shortcut 10% − 6%, which is only an approximation and is not correct to one decimal place; C is nominal growth.
Q17Economic growth · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between actual economic growth and potential economic growth. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how more spending on education can lead to potential economic growth. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Actual growth: an increase in real GDP (output actually produced). Potential growth: an increase in the economy's productive capacity (LRAS/PPC shifts out).
    1 each.
  2. (b) Education raises skills and the productivity of labour, increasing the quality of the factor labour; the productive capacity of the economy rises, shifting LRAS (or the PPC) outward.
    1 better skills/human capital; 1 higher labour productivity; 1 LRAS or PPC shifts right/outward (over time).
Q18Economic growth · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data for Country R:Year: 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026Real GDP ($bn): 300, 309, 315, 330Population (million): 200, 204, 208, 212

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of economic growth in 2026. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head between 2023 and 2026. Give your answer to one decimal place. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why a rise in real GDP per head may overstate the improvement in living standards. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.8%
    M1 (330 − 315) ÷ 315 × 100. A1 4.8%.
  2. (b) +3.8%
    M1 2023: 300bn ÷ 200m = $1,500; M1 2026: 330bn ÷ 212m = $1,557. A1 +3.8%.
  3. (c) Average figure hides inequality; ignores pollution, longer working hours and loss of leisure; output may be military or exports rather than consumer goods; informal economy changes; quality of life measures like health are excluded.
    1 mark each for any three explained points.
Q19Economic growth · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain one cause of actual economic growth that comes from the demand side of the economy. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether an increase in government spending on roads and electricity grids will lead to sustained economic growth. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. a rise in consumer spending or exports increases AD; if there is spare capacity, firms raise output so real GDP grows.
    1 demand-side cause; 1 explained link to higher real output when spare capacity exists.
  2. (b) Short run: AD rises (actual growth); long run: lower transport and energy costs raise productivity, shifting LRAS → potential growth. Sustained only if projects are well chosen, completed and maintained, and financed without crowding out or debt problems.

Q20Economic growth · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether the benefits of economic growth always outweigh its costs for a developing economy such as Pakistan. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Growth brings higher incomes, jobs, tax revenue for health and education and less poverty; costs include pollution, depletion of resources, inequality, inflation and current-account pressures. Benefits usually outweigh costs for a low-income country, but not always: it depends on the type, sustainability and distribution of growth.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides with a diagram where relevant and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Benefits: higher real incomes and living standards, lower unemployment, more tax revenue without higher tax rates, reduced poverty. Costs: environmental damage (smog, water stress), resource depletion, rising inequality, inflation if AD outruns capacity, import surge and current-account deficits (boom–bust cycles), urban congestion. Evaluation: growth led by consumption/imports vs by investment/exports; whether gains reach the poor; opportunity cost of investment (less consumption now); sustainability; policy can reduce costs. Judgement must address 'always'.

Q23Unemployment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Invented data for Country S: working-age population 120 million; labour force 78 million; unemployed according to the labour force survey 5.46 million; people claiming unemployment benefits 3.12 million.

  1. (a) Calculate the unemployment rate shown by each measure. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why the claimant count measure is lower than the labour force survey measure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Labour force survey: 7%; claimant count: 4%
    1 5.46 ÷ 78 × 100 = 7%; 1 3.12 ÷ 78 × 100 = 4%. (Use the labour force, not the working-age population.)
  2. (b) Some unemployed people are not eligible for benefits (e.g. partner's income, recently left education, informal workers); some choose not to claim (stigma, small benefit, don't know how); the survey uses the international definition (looking for work and available).
    Up to 2 each: reason identified + explained.
Q24Unemployment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a recession causes cyclical unemployment. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether structural unemployment is more harmful than cyclical unemployment to an economy. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Falling AD means firms sell less, so they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand) and cut jobs.
    1 fall in AD/output; 1 lower derived demand for labour → job losses.
  2. (b) Structural unemployment is often longer lasting and concentrated in regions/industries; skills become obsolete and it does not disappear when the economy recovers. Cyclical unemployment can be widespread and severe but usually falls as AD recovers. Judgement depends on scale, duration and policy response.
    Analysis (up to 4): structural – mismatch of skills/location, long duration, hysteresis, regional decline, needs costly retraining; cyclical – affects many sectors, lost output across the economy, falling AD can deepen recession, but responds to fiscal/monetary policy. Evaluation (up to 2): a deep recession can turn cyclical into long-term unemployment; structural change also brings efficiency gains; depends on labour mobility and retraining; judgement.
Q25Circular flow of income · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Invented data for an open economy, $bn per year:Investment 120, Government spending 200, Exports 180Saving 100, Taxation 210, Imports 220

  1. (a) Calculate the total injections and the total withdrawals. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what will happen to the level of national income. [2]
  3. (c) State the two main flows between households and firms in the circular flow. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Injections $500bn; withdrawals $530bn
    1 I + G + X = 120 + 200 + 180 = 500; 1 S + T + M = 100 + 210 + 220 = 530.
  2. (b) Withdrawals exceed injections by $30bn, so less is spent on domestic output than is earned; national income will fall until withdrawals equal injections.
    1 W > I so spending in the flow falls; 1 national income falls to a new equilibrium where injections = withdrawals.
  3. (c) Households supply factor services and receive factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit); households spend on goods and services, which is revenue for firms.
    1 factor incomes from firms to households; 1 consumer spending from households to firms.
Q26Circular flow of income · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In the circular flow of income, which is an injection?A income tax paid by householdsB spending on imported goodsC spending by foreigners on a country's exportsD money saved by households in banks [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C spending by foreigners on a country's exports
    Injections are investment, government spending and exports – spending that enters the flow from outside the household–firm loop. A, B and D are withdrawals (taxation, imports and saving).
Q27Circular flow of income · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the circular flow of income in an open economy with a government sector, and consider whether an increase in spending on imports will always reduce national income. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Households and firms exchange factor services and spending; saving, taxes and imports leak out; investment, government spending and exports are injected. More imports are a withdrawal and reduce national income unless offset by higher injections (e.g. imported capital raising investment and exports, or rising exports).
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: households, firms, government, foreign sector; injections (I, G, X) and withdrawals (S, T, M); equilibrium where I + G + X = S + T + M. Analysis: higher M → greater withdrawal → spending on domestic output falls → national income falls (multiplier). Evaluation: imports of capital goods and raw materials may raise investment and export capacity; foreigners may spend their earnings on the country's exports; if imports replace saving rather than domestic consumption, effect differs; judgement on 'always'.
Q28National income statistics · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Invented data for Country T, $bn:GDP at market prices: 350Primary income (profits, interest, dividends) received from abroad: 12Primary income paid to foreign owners: 20Capital consumption (depreciation): 30

  1. (a) Calculate gross national income (GNI). [2]
  2. (b) Calculate net national income (NNI). [2]
  3. (c) Explain why GNI may be lower than GDP for a country where many firms are foreign-owned. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $342bn
    M1 GNI = GDP + primary income from abroad − primary income paid abroad = 350 + 12 − 20. A1 $342bn.
  2. (b) $312bn
    M1 NNI = GNI − capital consumption (depreciation) = 342 − 30. A1 $312bn.
  3. (c) Profits and interest earned by foreign owners from production in the country are counted in GDP but paid abroad, so they are subtracted to give GNI.
    1 foreign-owned firms send profits/dividends abroad; 1 these are primary income outflows deducted from GDP.
Q29National income statistics · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Invented data for Country U: GDP at market prices $500bn; indirect taxes on spending $60bn; subsidies on products $15bn.

  1. (a) Calculate GDP at basic prices. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the output, income and expenditure methods of measuring GDP should give the same total. [3]
  3. (c) State one reason why the three totals differ in practice. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) $455bn
    M1 GDP at basic prices = GDP at market prices − indirect taxes + subsidies = 500 − 60 + 15. A1 $455bn.
  2. (b) The value of output produced is either spent (expenditure) and the revenue from it is paid out as factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) to those who produced it, so all three measure the same flow.
    1 output is sold/spent (output = expenditure, counting changes in inventories); 1 the money received is paid as incomes to factors; 1 so the three measure the same circular flow.
  3. (c) Statistical discrepancy: errors and omissions, hidden/informal economy, different data sources and timing.
    1 any valid reason.
Q30National income statistics · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Invented data for Country V:2020: nominal GDP Rs 50 trillion, GDP deflator 100, population 220 million2026: nominal GDP Rs 105 trillion, GDP deflator 190, population 245 million

  1. (a) Calculate real GDP in 2026 at 2020 prices, in Rs trillion, to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head between 2020 and 2026, to one decimal place. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why economists use real GDP per head rather than nominal GDP when comparing living standards over time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 55.3 trillion
    M1 real GDP = nominal ÷ (deflator ÷ 100) = 105 ÷ 1.9. A1 Rs 55.3 trillion.
  2. (b) -0.8%
    M1 2020: 50 trillion ÷ 220 million = Rs 227,273; M1 2026: 55.26 trillion ÷ 245 million = Rs 225,564. A1 -0.8% (a small fall, although real GDP rose by 10.5%).
  3. (c) Nominal GDP rises with inflation even if output does not; real GDP removes price changes. Dividing by population shows the average output per person, as a growing population shares the output.
    1 real removes the effect of inflation; 1 per head allows for population growth.
