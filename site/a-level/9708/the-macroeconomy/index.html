Q5Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a consumer price index (CPI). [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why a consumer price index may overstate the true rise in the cost of living. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A weighted average of the prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by typical households, measured against a base year that has the value 100.
    1 weighted average of prices of a basket of consumer goods and services; 1 compared with a base year = 100.
  2. (b) Substitution: households switch away from items whose prices rise fastest, but the fixed basket assumes they do not. Q8Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. [4]
  2. (b) Consider which type of inflation is more likely in an oil-importing economy when the world price of crude oil rises by 40%. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than the economy's capacity grows → price level rises with higher real output. Cost-push: SRAS shifts left due to higher costs → price level rises while real output falls.
    1 demand-pull: AD increases (e.g. consumer or government spending) as output nears full capacity; 1 diagram AD1→AD2, higher P and Y; 1 cost-push: higher costs (wages, imported raw materials) shift AS left; 1 diagram AS1→AS2, higher P and lower Y.
  2. (b) Mainly cost-push: higher fuel and transport costs shift AS left. But demand-pull elements are possible if wages rise in response or the government expands spending; effect depends on oil's share of costs and the exchange rate.
    Analysis (up to 3): oil is an input for transport, electricity and manufacturing → costs rise → SRAS left → cost-push; import bill rises, which may reduce AD (more spending on imports), dampening demand-pull. Consideration (1+): second-round wage-price spiral; subsidies to fuel; currency movements; judgement.

  1. (a) Using the data, identify one piece of evidence that the fall in the price level in Country Z was not caused by a fall in aggregate demand. [2]
  2. (b) With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain how productivity growth can cause the price level to fall while real output rises. [4]
  3. (c) Consider whether the deflation in Country Z is a cause for concern. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Real GDP grew by 3.8 % and unemployment fell (5.2 % to 4.6 %), which would not happen if AD had fallen.

  2. (b) Productivity gains lower unit costs and raise productive capacity, shifting SRAS (and LRAS) to the right. With AD unchanged or growing slowly, the new equilibrium has a lower price level and higher real output.

Q11Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a high rate of inflation is always more harmful to an economy than deflation. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always. High inflation erodes purchasing power, harms savers and fixed-income groups, reduces competitiveness and creates uncertainty; but persistent deflation can delay spending, raise real debt burdens and cause unemployment. Harm depends on the size, cause and whether it is anticipated.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides with a diagram where relevant and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Inflation costs: menu and shoe-leather costs, redistribution from savers/lenders to borrowers, fiscal drag, loss of international competitiveness, uncertainty discouraging investment, hyperinflation risk. Deflation costs: postponed consumption, rising real value of debt, falling profits → unemployment, real interest rates rise when nominal rates can't fall below zero, deflationary spiral. Evaluation: 'good' deflation from productivity gains (AS shift right) vs 'bad' deflation from falling AD; mild, anticipated inflation is less harmful than unanticipated high inflation; effects differ for different groups; the context (debt levels, indexation). Judgement must address 'always'.

Q14Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between anticipated and unanticipated inflation, and consider which is more damaging to firms. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Anticipated inflation is expected and built into wage deals, prices and interest rates; unanticipated inflation is a surprise. Unanticipated inflation is usually more damaging to firms because it distorts price signals, creates uncertainty that discourages investment and can raise costs unexpectedly; but even anticipated inflation brings menu costs and loss of competitiveness if it is higher than abroad.
    Mark split (8): knowledge 3, analysis 3, evaluation 2. K: definitions of both; costs such as menu costs, shoe-leather costs, wage bargaining. E: depends on the rate (high anticipated inflation is still harmful), whether firms can index contracts, the exchange rate, and whether firms are borrowers (gain from surprise inflation).
Q15Price stability (inflation and deflation) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that inflation is always caused by excessive growth in the money supply. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) The quantity theory (MV = PT) suggests that if V and T are stable, faster money growth raises P, so monetarists argue that sustained inflation is a monetary phenomenon. However, V is not always stable, T can grow, and inflation can start from cost-push shocks (oil, depreciation) or demand-pull from fiscal expansion; money growth may follow rather than cause inflation. A justified conclusion is that persistent high inflation usually needs monetary growth, but individual episodes have other causes.
    Mark bands (20): AO1/AO2 knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 — accurate, developed chains of reasoning with an accurate, correctly labelled diagram where relevant; AO3 evaluation up to 6 — weighs both sides, considers context and assumptions, and reaches a justified conclusion that answers the question. One-sided answers are capped at mid Level 2. Content: equation of exchange; monetarist assumptions (V and T fixed in the short run, T set by real factors); Fisher/quantity theory chain M↑ → P↑; examples of hyperinflation with rapid money creation. Counter-arguments: velocity changes (e.g. with digital payments or a fall in confidence); cost-push inflation from oil prices, wages or depreciation shown on AD/AS; demand-pull from consumer or government spending; money supply may be endogenous (banks lend as demand rises); spare capacity means extra money raises output not prices. Evaluation: distinction between triggers and sustained inflation; time period; Keynesian vs monetarist views; judgement on 'always'.

(c) Better than market rates for comparing what incomes can buy, but it is still an average that ignores distribution, informal and non-market output, leisure, environment, health and education; baskets differ between countries and PPP estimates are uncertain. Composite measures such as the HDI or MPI add other dimensions. Conclusion: a useful starting point but not sufficient on its own.

Q89National income statistics · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether gross national income (GNI) per head is a reliable indicator of differences in living standards between countries. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) GNI per head measures average income available to residents, so it is a common, easily compared indicator linked to spending power, health and education. But it ignores distribution, non-market and informal output, quality of goods, leisure, working conditions, environmental damage and life expectancy; conversion at market exchange rates distorts comparisons unless PPP is used. Composite indicators (HDI, MEW, MPI) address some weaknesses. Conclusion: a necessary but incomplete indicator.
    Mark bands (20): AO1/AO2 knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 — accurate, developed chains of reasoning with an accurate, correctly labelled diagram where relevant; AO3 evaluation up to 6 — weighs both sides, considers context and assumptions, and reaches a justified conclusion that answers the question. One-sided answers are capped at mid Level 2. Content: definition of GNI and why per head; why it is used (data availability, correlation with HDI components); limitations listed above; PPP vs market exchange rates; alternatives HDI (income, health, education), MEW, MPI and their own weaknesses. Evaluation: purpose of comparison, levels of development, data quality, judgement.
Q90National income statistics · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether real GDP statistics are useful for comparing living standards in a country over time. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Real GDP per head shows changes in average output and income after removing inflation, so it is a useful headline indicator. But it ignores how income is distributed, changes in quality and new products, the informal economy, leisure, environmental costs and what output is produced (e.g. defence vs health); population growth must be allowed for. Other indicators should be used too.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides, diagram where relevant, and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is only asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Content: nominal vs real; need for per head figures; usefulness (consistent, regular, comparable across years). Limitations above, plus changes in the size of the informal sector over time, accuracy of the price index. Evaluation: still the best single available measure; usefulness depends on the period and the country; combine with HDI, poverty and inequality data; judgement.
