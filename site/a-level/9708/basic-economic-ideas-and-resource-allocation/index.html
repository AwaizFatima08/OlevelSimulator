Q2Production possibility curves · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A flood destroys roads, bridges and factories in a country. Using a production possibility curve described in words, explain the effect on the country's productive capacity. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how this differs from the effect of a rise in unemployment caused by a fall in demand. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The PPC shifts inwards because the quantity of capital (a factor of production) has fallen.
    1 mark: axes are two goods/services (e.g. agricultural goods and manufactured goods), not price and quantity. 1 mark: capital stock is destroyed, so the maximum output possible falls. 1 mark: the whole PPC shifts inwards (towards the origin); if mainly factories are lost the shift is larger on the manufactured goods axis.
  2. (b) Unemployment moves the economy to a point inside an unchanged PPC.
    1 mark: productive capacity is unchanged, so the PPC does not shift. 1 mark: actual output falls, shown by a movement from a point on the curve to a point inside it.
Q3Production possibility curves · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which event would shift a country's production possibility curve outwards? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B the discovery and development of new natural gas reserves
    New natural resources raise the economy's productive capacity, so the whole curve moves outwards. A moves the economy from a point inside the curve towards it (no shift); C and D are movements along the existing curve.
Q4Production possibility curves · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a movement along a production possibility curve and a shift of the curve. [2]
  2. (b) State two reasons why an economy may be producing at a point inside its production possibility curve. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A movement along the curve is a reallocation of existing, fully employed resources from one good to another; a shift is a change in the economy's total productive capacity (quantity or quality of resources, technology).
    1 mark: movement = reallocation of given resources between goods/change in combination produced; 1 mark: shift = change in productive capacity/potential output caused by a change in quantity or quality of resources or technology.
  2. (b) Any two of: unemployed labour; idle capital/factories working below capacity; inefficient use of resources (productive inefficiency), e.g. poor management or power cuts.
    1 mark each for any two valid reasons.
Q5Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) An economy moves along its production possibility curve from 60 units of capital goods and 300 units of consumer goods to 80 units of capital goods and 260 units of consumer goods. What is the opportunity cost of each extra unit of capital goods? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 2 units of consumer goods
    Consumer goods given up = 300 − 260 = 40; extra capital goods = 80 − 60 = 20; 40 ÷ 20 = 2 units of consumer goods per unit. D is the total sacrifice, not the cost per unit; A inverts the ratio.
Q6Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

The table shows the production possibilities of a farming region in Punjab that grows only wheat and cotton (invented data).Combination: wheat (thousand tonnes), cotton (thousand bales)A: 0, 500B: 100, 470C: 200, 420D: 300, 340E: 400, 220F: 500, 0

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of one extra tonne of wheat when production moves from combination C to combination D. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the opportunity cost of one extra tonne of wheat when production moves from combination E to combination F. [1]
  3. (c) Using your answers to (a) and (b), explain why the production possibility curve for this region is concave (bowed outwards) from the origin. [3]
  4. (d) State whether the combination 200 thousand tonnes of wheat and 450 thousand bales of cotton can be produced. Give a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.8 bales of cotton per tonne of wheat
    Cotton given up = 420 − 340 = 80 thousand bales (M1); ÷ 100 thousand tonnes of wheat = 0.8 bales per tonne (A1).
  2. (b) 2.2 bales of cotton per tonne of wheat
    220 thousand bales ÷ 100 thousand tonnes = 2.2 bales per tonne (B1).
  3. (c) Opportunity cost rises as more wheat is produced because resources are not equally suited to both crops.
    1 mark: opportunity cost of wheat increases (0.8 → 2.2). 1 mark: land and labour best suited to cotton are moved into wheat last / factors are not perfectly mobile or equally productive in both uses. 1 mark: so each extra unit of wheat needs more cotton to be given up, making the slope steeper and the curve concave.
  4. (d) No, it is unattainable.
    B1 not attainable; B1 at 200 thousand tonnes of wheat the maximum cotton output is 420 thousand bales, so 450 lies outside the PPC with current resources and technology.
Q7Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) With the help of a production possibility curve diagram, explain the effect of a new technology that raises productivity in the textile industry only, and consider whether such a change will always increase the economic welfare of the country. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) The PPC pivots outwards along the textile axis only; welfare usually rises but not always.
    Knowledge (3): PPC shows maximum combinations of two goods (textiles and other goods) with given resources and technology; opportunity cost; correct axis labels. Analysis (3): the maximum output of textiles rises while the maximum of other goods is unchanged, so the curve pivots outwards from the other-goods intercept; at every point except that intercept the country can now produce more; the opportunity cost of textiles falls. Evaluation (2): welfare may not rise if the economy is inside its PPC (unemployment), if workers displaced by machines stay unemployed, if extra output creates pollution, or if textiles are mostly exported and the gains go to a few owners; a justified conclusion.
Q8Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A small island economy can produce either 400 tablet computers or 1000 tonnes of rice per month, or any combination on a straight-line production possibility curve between these two points (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of producing one tablet computer. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the maximum quantity of rice the country can produce if it makes 150 tablet computers. [2]
  3. (c) Explain what the straight-line shape of this production possibility curve assumes about the country's resources. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.5 tonnes of rice
    1000 ÷ 400 = 2.5 tonnes of rice (B1).
  2. (b) 625 tonnes
    Rice given up = 150 × 2.5 = 375 (M1); 1000 − 375 = 625 tonnes (A1).
  3. (c) Resources are equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost is constant.
    1 mark: constant opportunity cost (same slope throughout). 1 mark: this assumes factors are equally productive in making tablets and rice / perfectly transferable, which is unlikely in reality.
Q9Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A provincial government can use its resources to treat hospital patients or to build roads. Its production possibilities for one year are (invented data):Combination A: 0 million patients, 500 km of roadCombination B: 10 million patients, 480 km of roadCombination C: 20 million patients, 440 km of roadCombination D: 30 million patients, 360 km of roadCombination E: 40 million patients, 200 km of road

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost, in km of road, of treating the extra 10 million patients when the economy moves from combination C to combination D. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the average opportunity cost per million patients of moving from combination A to combination E. [1]
  3. (c) Using the data, explain why the opportunity cost of treating patients increases as more patients are treated. [2]
  4. (d) The province actually treats 25 million patients and builds 380 km of road. State whether this combination is on, inside or outside the production possibility curve, and justify your answer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 80 km of road
    440 − 360 = 80 km (1).
  2. (b) 12.5 km of road per million patients
    500 km given up ÷ 40 million patients = 12.5 km (1).
  3. (c) Each extra 10 million patients costs more road (20, 40, 80, 160 km) because resources are not equally suited to both uses; the resources moved last (e.g. road engineers, heavy machinery) are better at road building than health care.
    1 mark: uses the data to show rising cost (20 → 40 → 80 → 160 km); 1 mark: explanation – resources are specialised/not perfectly substitutable between uses.
  4. (d) Inside the curve: with 25 million patients the province could build at least 400 km of road (midway between 440 and 360), so 380 km means resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
    1 mark: inside; 1 mark: justification using the data – maximum road at 25 million patients is about 400 km (higher still on a curve bowed outwards), so 380 km is below it.
Q10Production possibility curves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how unemployment and an outdated stock of capital equipment each affect an economy's position in relation to its production possibility curve, and consider which of the two problems would be easier for a government to correct. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Unemployment: the economy produces at a point inside its PPC; reducing it moves output towards the curve without any shift. An outdated capital stock: the PPC itself is closer to the origin than it could be; modernising it (investment, new technology) shifts the curve outwards but takes time and has an opportunity cost in consumer goods now. Judgement: cyclical unemployment can often be reduced quickly by raising demand, while renewing capital is slow; but structural unemployment may be as hard to fix as old capital.
    Marks: 3 knowledge and understanding, 3 analysis, 2 evaluation. Knowledge: point inside the PPC = unused resources; position of the PPC = productive capacity; correctly labelled axes (two goods, not P and Q). Analysis: unemployment → movement to the curve; new capital → outward shift; investment requires giving up consumer goods now (movement along the curve first). Evaluation: depends on the type of unemployment (cyclical vs structural); time lags; funding constraints; a reasoned conclusion.
Q11Production possibility curves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks

Invented data for two countries, average for 2015–2024:Country X: investment as a share of GDP 32 %; average annual real GDP growth 6.1 %Country Y: investment as a share of GDP 14 %; average annual real GDP growth 2.8 %

  1. (a) Compare the average growth rates of the two countries over the period. [2]
  2. (b) With the help of a production possibility curve described in words, explain how a decision to produce more capital goods now can affect a country's future output. [4]
  3. (c) Assess whether the data prove that Country Y should increase the share of output it devotes to investment. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Country X's average growth (6.1 %) was about 2.2 times that of Country Y (2.8 %), i.e. 3.3 percentage points higher.
    1 mark: X grew faster. 1 mark: data manipulated, e.g. 3.3 percentage points higher or about 2.2 times as fast.
  2. (b) Choosing a point with more capital goods and fewer consumer goods now raises the capital stock, shifting the PPC outward in future.
    Axes labelled capital goods and consumer goods (1). Moving along the PPC towards capital goods means an opportunity cost of current consumer goods (1). Extra capital raises productive capacity (1). Future PPC shifts outwards further than for a country choosing more consumer goods, allowing more of both goods later (1).
  3. (c) Not proven: the data show an association, but growth also depends on other factors; a higher investment share has a current consumption cost.
    Analysis (up to 4): higher investment links to faster capacity growth (X); sacrifice of current consumption may harm living standards of poor households; return depends on type/quality of investment; growth also depends on labour skills, technology, demand, political stability; the data cover only two countries and averages, so correlation not causation. Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement, e.g. investment is likely to help but the data alone cannot prove it; it depends on whether Y has spare capacity or weak demand.
Q12Production possibility curves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether the production possibility curve is a useful way to explain the economic problem facing a low-income country. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Useful for showing scarcity, choice, opportunity cost and growth, but limited because it is a simplified two-good model.
    Up to 8 marks for analysis: PPC illustrates scarcity (finite resources), choice between combinations, opportunity cost (slope), unemployment (inside), growth (outward shift) and the trade-off between capital and consumer goods, which is key for a low-income country with low savings. Limitations: only two goods; assumes fixed resources and technology; does not show distribution of output, quality of life, or which point is best (a normative choice); points outside may be reached through trade or aid. Up to 4 marks for evaluation: a supported judgement, e.g. useful as a starting framework but policy decisions need further evidence on income distribution and markets. Level 3 (9–12): balanced, applied to low-income country, justified conclusion; Level 2 (5–8): some analysis, limited evaluation; Level 1 (1–4): descriptive.
Q13Production possibility curves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Invented data for a country (US$ billion, at constant prices): potential output in 2024 was 500 and actual output was 470. In 2025 potential output grew by 5 % and actual output grew by 3 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the output gap (potential minus actual output) as a percentage of potential output in 2024 and in 2025. Give your answers to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Using a production possibility curve described in words, explain what happened to actual and potential growth between 2024 and 2025. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2024: 6.0 %; 2025: 7.8 %
    2025 potential = 500 × 1.05 = 525.00; actual = 470 × 1.03 = 484.1 (M1). 2024 gap = 30 ÷ 500 = 6.0 % (A1). 2025 gap = 40.9 ÷ 525.00 = 7.8 % (A1).
  2. (b) The PPC shifted outwards (potential growth 5 %) and the production point moved outwards (actual growth 3 %), but the point ended up further inside the new curve.
    1 mark: outward shift of PPC shows potential growth. 1 mark: movement of the production point away from the origin shows actual growth. 1 mark: because actual grew less than potential, the gap between the point and the curve widened (more unused resources).

Q15Production possibility curves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 9 marks

Two regions of a country each produce only wheat and machines, and each has a straight-line production possibility curve (invented data, per month):North: either 60 tonnes of wheat or 30 machines, or any combination on the straight line between themSouth: either 40 tonnes of wheat or 40 machines, or any combination on the straight line between them

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of producing one machine in each region. [2]
  2. (b) The two regions combine their production. State the maximum quantity of wheat and the maximum number of machines the combined economy can produce. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the maximum quantity of wheat the combined economy can produce if it makes 55 machines. Explain which region should make the machines. [3]
  4. (d) Explain why the combined production possibility curve is bowed outwards (concave to the origin) even though each region's own curve is a straight line. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) North: 2 tonnes of wheat per machine; South: 1 tonne of wheat per machine.
    1 mark each: North 60 ÷ 30 = 2; South 40 ÷ 40 = 1.
  2. (b) 100 tonnes of wheat or 70 machines.
    1 mark: 60 + 40 = 100 tonnes of wheat; 1 mark: 30 + 40 = 70 machines.
  3. (c) 30 tonnes of wheat; South should make its 40 machines first because its opportunity cost is lower, and North makes the other 15.
    M1: South makes 40 machines (lower opportunity cost, 1 tonne each); M1: North makes 15 machines, giving up 15 × 2 = 30 tonnes; A1: North produces 60 − 30 = 30 tonnes of wheat, South none, total 30 tonnes.
  4. (d) As more machines are made, production moves from the low-cost region (South, 1 tonne) to the high-cost region (North, 2 tonnes), so the opportunity cost of machines rises and the combined curve has a kink that bows outwards.
    1 mark: the two regions have different opportunity costs; 1 mark: extra machines must eventually come from the higher-cost region, so opportunity cost increases.

Q17Classification of goods and services · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define a public good. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a private firm is unlikely to provide street lighting in a residential area of Lahore. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A good that is non-rival and non-excludable.
    1 mark non-rival: one person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others. 1 mark non-excludable: people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting.
  2. (b) Because of the free-rider problem, the firm could not charge users and would make no revenue.
    1 mark: street lighting is non-excludable, so non-payers cannot be prevented from using it. 1 mark: consumers have an incentive to be free riders and not reveal their willingness to pay. 1 mark: the firm cannot earn revenue to cover costs, so the market fails to provide it (missing market).

Q19Classification of goods and services · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain the two characteristics that distinguish a private good from a public good. [2]
  2. (b) Classify each of the following as a private good, a public good or a free good, giving a reason in each case: (i) a cricket bat; (ii) a tsunami early-warning siren on a coastline; (iii) sea air at the beach. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A private good is rival (one person's consumption reduces what is available to others) and excludable (non-payers can be prevented from consuming it); a public good is non-rival and non-excludable.
    1 mark: rivalry explained; 1 mark: excludability explained.
  2. (b) (i) private good – rival and excludable; (ii) public good – everyone in range hears it (non-excludable) and one person hearing it does not reduce it for others (non-rival); (iii) free good – not scarce, so it has zero opportunity cost.
    1 mark each for correct classification with a reason.
Q20Classification of goods and services · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

    1 mark: a valid method; 1 mark: explanation of why it avoids the free-rider problem.

Q22Classification of goods and services · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain why motorcycle helmets are classified as a merit good. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why the government might make the wearing of helmets compulsory rather than subsidise their price. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Riders underestimate the benefit of a helmet (protection from serious head injury), so in a free market fewer helmets are bought and worn than is in the riders' own interest.
    1 mark: a merit good is one that would be under-consumed if left to the free market / benefits are greater than consumers realise. 1 mark: information failure – riders underestimate the risk of an accident or the severity of head injuries (or are over-confident about short journeys). 1 mark: so demand and consumption are below the level that best serves the rider (and society, e.g. lower hospital costs).
  2. (b) A subsidy lowers the price, but riders who underestimate the benefit may still not buy or wear a helmet; a law changes behaviour directly.
    1 mark reason (the cause of under-consumption is information failure, not mainly price / regulation has a low budget cost). 1 mark development, e.g. even a cheaper helmet may be left at home, while a law backed by fines makes every rider wear one; it also costs the government little compared with a subsidy.
Q23Classification of goods and services · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a merit good and a public good, and consider whether secondary education in Pakistan should be regarded as a merit good. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Public goods are defined by non-rivalry and non-excludability; merit goods by under-consumption due to information failure; education is usually a merit good, though this involves a value judgement.
    Knowledge (3): public good definition (non-rival, non-excludable, free-rider, missing market); merit good definition (under-consumed in free market, information failure, judged by government). Analysis (3): education is rival and excludable, so it is not a public good; households may underestimate future earnings and wider benefits, poor families face cost barriers or need children's labour; so consumption is below the level society desires. Evaluation (2): classification as merit good is partly a value judgement; for well-informed households demand may already be high; private schools show it can be supplied by the market; a justified conclusion.

Q25Classification of goods and services · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Using alcohol as an example, explain the difference between a demerit good and a good that has negative externalities in consumption. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why deciding that a good is a demerit good involves a value judgement. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A demerit good is one whose harm to the consumer is underestimated (imperfect information), so it is over-consumed; e.g. drinkers underestimate damage to their own liver. A negative consumption externality is a cost to third parties, e.g. drunk-driving accidents or violence affecting others. Show answer
  1. (a) 30 percentage points
    45 − 15 = 30 (1).
  2. (b) Local-currency deposits lose purchasing power quickly (poor store of value), while dollar deposits keep their value and may gain as the local currency depreciates.

  2. (b) Explain how increasing water scarcity forces choices between agricultural and urban uses of water. [3]
  3. (c) Consider whether charging higher prices for irrigation water would be the best way to deal with this scarcity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 82 %
    (5000 − 900) ÷ 5000 × 100 = 82 % (1).
  2. (b) With less water per person, extra water for growing cities (drinking, industry) means less for irrigation and lower crop output, and vice versa; the opportunity cost of using water in one place rises; the government must decide on allocation (canal rotations, pricing).
