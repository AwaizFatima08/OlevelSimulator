Practice questions

Original practice questions written by HomiLabs in the exam style. They are not Cambridge past paper questions. Try each one before opening the answer.

Q1Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following acts as an automatic stabiliser in an economy?A a progressive income tax systemB a one-off decision to build a new motorwayC a cut in the central bank's policy interest rateD a new tariff on imported cars [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a progressive income tax system
    As incomes fall in a recession, people move into lower tax bands, so tax paid falls faster than income and disposable income is cushioned, with no new government decision needed. B is discretionary fiscal policy (a deliberate decision), C is monetary policy. D is a protectionist measure, not a stabiliser.
Q2Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 1: Tax revenue of Country P, 2025 (PKR billion; data invented for practice)Income tax: 2900Corporate (company) profits tax: 1100General sales tax: 3400Customs duties: 1200Excise duties: 400

  1. (a) Distinguish between a direct tax and an indirect tax, and identify the direct taxes in Table 1. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate indirect taxes as a percentage of total tax revenue. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a general sales tax is likely to be regressive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A direct tax is paid straight to the government by the person or firm on whom it falls, out of income or wealth; an indirect tax is levied on spending and can be passed on in the price. Direct taxes: income tax and corporate profits tax.
    1 mark for a correct distinction (on income/wealth vs on spending, incidence can be shifted); 1 mark for identifying income tax and corporate profits tax.
  2. (b) 55.6 %
    M1: indirect taxes = 3400 + 1200 + 400 = 5000; total = 9000. A1: 5000 ÷ 9000 × 100 = 55.6 %.
  3. (c) Poorer households spend a larger share of their income on taxed goods, so the tax paid takes a larger percentage of a low income than of a high income.
    1 mark: regressive means the share of income paid in tax falls as income rises; 1 mark: low-income households spend (rather than save) most of their income, so sales tax paid is a higher % of their income.
Q3Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A government in South-East Asia plans to cut the basic rate of income tax from 20 % to 17 % to support spending during a slowdown.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a progressive income tax. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether a cut in income tax rates will increase aggregate demand. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A tax where the proportion of income paid in tax (the average rate) rises as income rises, because higher slices of income are taxed at higher marginal rates.
    1 mark: average rate rises as income rises; 1 mark: higher marginal rates on higher bands of income (or an example).
  2. (b) Usually yes, because disposable income and consumption rise, but the effect may be small if households save the extra income, buy imports, or lack confidence.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: lower tax → higher disposable income (1) → higher consumption (1) → AD (C + I + G + X − M) shifts right (1); possible extra effect on incentives. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: the rise may be small if the extra income is saved (low confidence, debt repayment), spent on imports, or goes mainly to high earners with a low propensity to consume; or offset if spending is cut to pay for it.
Q4Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between automatic stabilisers and discretionary fiscal policy, and consider which is more useful in reducing the effects of a fall in aggregate demand. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Automatic stabilisers change tax revenue and spending without a decision as income changes; discretionary policy is a deliberate change. Stabilisers act quickly but only cushion; discretionary policy can be larger but suffers time lags.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of fiscal policy, automatic stabilisers (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) and discretionary policy (deliberate changes in tax rates or spending); examples. Analysis: in a downturn, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically, supporting disposable income and AD; discretionary policy, e.g. extra infrastructure spending, shifts AD right by a chosen amount; AD/AS diagram. Evaluation: stabilisers work immediately and without political delay but only reduce, not reverse, a fall in AD; discretionary policy can be targeted and larger but has recognition, decision and implementation lags and may arrive too late; usefulness also depends on the size of the welfare system and the government's ability to borrow; judgement.
Q5Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a country, the first PKR 600 000 of annual income is tax-free, the next PKR 600 000 is taxed at 5 % and all income above PKR 1 200 000 is taxed at 15 %. A worker earns PKR 1 800 000 a year. What are the worker's marginal and average rates of income tax?A marginal rate 15 %, average rate 6.7 %B marginal rate 6.7 %, average rate 15 %C marginal rate 15 %, average rate 15 %D marginal rate 5 %, average rate 6.7 % [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A marginal rate 15 %, average rate 6.7 %
    Tax = 0 + 5 % × 600 000 + 15 % × 600 000 = 30 000 + 90 000 = PKR 120,000. Average rate = 120,000 ÷ 1 800 000 = 6.7 %. The marginal rate is the rate on the last rupee earned, 15 %. B reverses the two definitions.
Q6Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 2: Government finances of Country R (US$ million; data invented for practice)Year 1: nominal GDP 52 000; government spending 10 400; tax revenue 8320Year 2: nominal GDP 50 400; government spending 10 900; tax revenue 7860

  1. (a) Calculate the budget deficit as a percentage of GDP in Year 1 and in Year 2. Give your answers to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Using the data, explain how automatic stabilisers might account for the change in the budget deficit between Year 1 and Year 2. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1: 4.0 %; Year 2: 6.0 %
    Year 1: (10 400 − 8320) = 2080; 2080 ÷ 52 000 × 100 = 4.0 % (1). Year 2: (10 900 − 7860) = 3040; 3040 ÷ 50 400 × 100 = 6.0 % (1).
  2. (b) GDP fell, so incomes, spending and profits fell; tax revenue fell automatically while spending on unemployment and welfare benefits rose automatically, so the deficit increased with no new policy decision.
    1 mark: identifies the fall in nominal GDP (52 000 to 50 400) as a sign of recession; 1 mark: lower incomes/spending/profits → lower revenue from income, sales and profits taxes (revenue fell 8320 → 7860); 1 mark: rising unemployment → more benefit payments, so spending rose (10 400 → 10 900); 1 mark: these changes happen without a government decision and cushion the fall in AD, so the rise in the deficit is partly cyclical.
Q7Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether expansionary fiscal policy is the best way to reduce unemployment during a recession. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expansionary fiscal policy can raise AD and reduce cyclical unemployment, but its success depends on spare capacity, the multiplier, debt levels and time lags; it does little for structural unemployment, and monetary or supply-side policy may be better in some cases.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: higher government spending or lower taxes → higher AD → firms raise output and demand more labour; with a negative output gap there is little inflationary pressure (AD/AS diagram with AD shifting right on the flatter part of AS); spending can be targeted at regions or labour-intensive projects; comparison with monetary policy (interest rate cuts) and supply-side policies (training) is required by 'best'. Evaluation: works for cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment but not structural or frictional; time lags; leakages to imports; a large existing debt may limit borrowing or raise interest rates; confidence may stay low; monetary policy may be faster but weak if interest rates are already near zero; a combination may be best; justified conclusion.
Q8Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a government could use fiscal policy to reduce demand-pull inflation, and consider whether doing so will harm economic growth. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Contractionary fiscal policy (higher taxes, lower spending) reduces AD and so the pressure on prices; it may slow growth in the short run, but stable prices can support long-run growth.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: demand-pull inflation is caused by AD rising faster than the economy's capacity; contractionary (deflationary) fiscal policy. Analysis: higher income tax or sales tax reduces disposable income and consumption; cuts in government spending reduce G directly; AD shifts left, reducing the price level rise (AD/AS diagram near full capacity). Evaluation: real output may fall and unemployment rise in the short run; cutting capital spending may reduce future LRAS; but if the economy is at full capacity the fall in output may be small, and lower inflation improves confidence, investment and competitiveness, helping long-run growth; judgement.
Q9Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A government's budget deficit fell from $40 billion last year to $25 billion this year. Assuming the government finances its deficit only by borrowing, which statement must be correct?A The national debt fell this year.B The national debt still rose this year, but by less than last year.C Government spending fell this year.D Tax revenue rose this year. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B The national debt still rose this year, but by less than last year.
    The national debt is the stock of past borrowing; any deficit adds to it, so a $25 billion deficit raises the debt by $25 billion, which is less than last year's $40 billion rise. A confuses a smaller deficit (flow) with a falling debt (stock); C and D are possible, but the deficit could fall through either, so neither must be true.
Q10Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): A lower-middle-income country collects tax equal to only 9.5 % of its GDP of PKR 110 000 billion. Fewer than 4 % of adults file income-tax returns, and a large informal sector pays little tax. A lender has asked the government to raise the ratio to 12 % within three years.

  1. (a) Calculate the extra tax revenue the government would collect if the tax-to-GDP ratio rose from 9.5 % to 12 % with GDP unchanged. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether widening the tax base is a better way to raise tax revenue than raising existing tax rates. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) PKR 2,750 billion
    M1: (12 % − 9.5 %) = 2.5 % of GDP. A1: 0.025 × 110 000 = PKR 2,750 billion.
  2. (b) Widening the base is usually better for efficiency and fairness, but it is slower and harder to enforce; raising rates gives quicker revenue but may reduce incentives and encourage evasion. A judgement depends on administrative capacity and the size of the informal sector.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: widening the base (bringing traders, retailers, agricultural incomes and property into the net) raises revenue without higher rates, spreads the burden more fairly, and reduces distortions; raising rates on the few who already pay may reduce work and investment incentives, encourage evasion and avoidance, and can be regressive if it relies on sales tax; higher rates may raise revenue quickly. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: a broader base needs records, digital payments and political will, so it is slow; in the short run higher rates may be needed; overall judgement (e.g. a broader base is better for sustained revenue).
Q11Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a government should always aim to balance its budget. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always: deficits can be justified in recessions or for productive investment, and surpluses during booms; but persistent deficits raise debt and interest costs, so a balanced budget over the economic cycle is a more sensible aim than a balanced budget every year.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: meaning of a balanced budget, deficit and surplus; benefits of balance: no rise in national debt, lower interest payments, confidence of lenders, avoids possible crowding out and future tax rises; costs: in a recession, cutting spending or raising taxes to balance the budget reduces AD further and deepens unemployment (AD/AS); deficits can finance infrastructure and education that raise future LRAS and tax revenue; a surplus in a boom can reduce demand-pull inflation. Evaluation: depends on the stage of the cycle, the size of existing debt, whether borrowing is in foreign currency, and what the borrowing pays for (investment vs current spending); conclusion that 'always' is too strong, balance over the cycle is more realistic.
Q12Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): To reduce its budget deficit, a government has raised its general sales tax rate from 17 % to 18 %. An opposition party argues that it should have cut government spending instead.

  1. (a) A good has a price of PKR 1000 before tax. Calculate the percentage increase in its price when the sales tax rate rises from 17 % to 18 %, assuming the tax is fully passed on to consumers. Give your answer to two decimal places. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether raising the sales tax rate is a better way to reduce the budget deficit than cutting government spending. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.85 %
    M1: price with tax rises from PKR 1170 to PKR 1180. A1: 10 ÷ 1170 × 100 = 0.85 %. (Not 1 %: the base is the tax-inclusive price.)
  2. (b) A sales tax rise raises revenue quickly from a broad base but is regressive and adds to inflation; spending cuts avoid higher prices but can damage public services and growth. The better choice depends on what spending is cut and on the distribution of the burden.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: sales tax raises revenue from a wide base and is hard to avoid on formal-sector sales; it causes a one-off rise in the price level (cost-push) and falls more heavily on the poor (regressive); it reduces consumption and AD. Spending cuts reduce the deficit directly and do not raise prices, but cuts to health, education or infrastructure reduce future growth and welfare; both reduce AD in the short run. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: depends on whether essentials are exempt, on how efficient current spending is, on the state of the economy, and on PED of taxed goods; a justified judgement.
Q13Supply-side policy · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which policy is a supply-side policy?A a reduction in the central bank's policy interest rateB government subsidies for vocational training coursesC an increase in unemployment benefit paymentsD a temporary cut in sales tax to boost spending [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B government subsidies for vocational training courses
    Training raises the skills and productivity of the labour force, increasing the economy's productive capacity (LRAS). A and D mainly change aggregate demand (monetary and demand-side fiscal policy); C raises spending on benefits and may even weaken incentives to work.
Q14Supply-side policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A government in East Africa plans to double its spending on technical and vocational colleges over five years.

  1. (a) Define supply-side policy. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how increased government spending on technical education could affect the long-run aggregate supply of an economy. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Government policy aimed at increasing the productive capacity (potential output) of the economy by improving the quantity and/or quality of factors of production or the efficiency of markets.
    1 mark: increases productive capacity/potential output/LRAS; 1 mark: via quantity or quality of resources, or efficiency of markets.
  2. (b) Workers gain skills, so labour productivity rises; firms can produce more with the same inputs and costs fall; the economy's productive capacity rises, shifting LRAS to the right (and the PPC outwards), though only after a time lag.
    1 mark: improves quality of labour/human capital; 1 mark: higher labour productivity/lower unit costs; 1 mark: increases potential output, LRAS shifts right (diagram description); 1 mark: time lag of several years before effects appear / also adds to AD in the short run.
Q15Supply-side policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between market-based and interventionist supply-side policies, and consider which type is likely to increase output more quickly. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Market-based policies rely on incentives and competition (tax cuts, deregulation, privatisation); interventionist policies use government spending and direct action (education, infrastructure, R&D grants). Speed depends on the specific policy; neither type is always faster.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definition of supply-side policy; market-based examples and interventionist examples. Analysis: both aim to shift LRAS right; market-based policies change incentives to work, invest and compete; interventionist policies improve the quality and quantity of factors directly. Evaluation: deregulation or tax incentives can act within months, while education and large infrastructure projects take years; however, market-based policies depend on how firms and workers respond, which may be weak, and may increase inequality; interventionist spending also raises AD quickly; a judgement on which is faster in a given context.
Q16Supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following is an interventionist supply-side policy rather than a market-based one?A privatisation of a state-owned airlineB removing regulations that restrict entry into the telecommunications marketC a reduction in the top rate of income taxD government investment in a new national rail network [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) D government investment in a new national rail network
    Interventionist supply-side policy uses direct government action or spending to raise productive capacity, such as infrastructure. A, B and C are market-based: they reduce the role of the state and rely on incentives and competition.
Q17Supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 3: Output per worker in Country V (US$ per year, constant 2020 prices; data invented for practice)2020: 56002025: 6100The government plans to privatise its state-owned steel and electricity companies.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in output per worker between 2020 and 2025. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether privatising state-owned enterprises is likely to raise productivity further. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) +8.9 %
    M1: (6100 − 5600) ÷ 5600; A1: × 100 = 8.9 % increase.
  2. (b) Privatisation may raise productivity through the profit motive, competition and access to private capital, but gains are uncertain if a public monopoly simply becomes a private monopoly or if regulation is weak.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: profit motive and shareholder pressure → cost cutting and better management; access to private finance → investment in new technology; competition (if markets are opened) → efficiency; removes political interference. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: a private monopoly may lack incentive to improve; job losses may cut output in the short run; depends on regulation and on whether the market is contestable.
Q18Supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether reducing labour-market regulations, such as limits on hiring and firing, is the most effective supply-side policy to reduce unemployment. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Labour-market deregulation can make firms more willing to hire and reduce frictional and some structural unemployment, but it may reduce job security and wages and does little where unemployment is caused by low skills or weak demand; training and education may be more effective for structural unemployment.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: flexible labour markets reduce the cost and risk of hiring, so firms take on more workers; quicker matching reduces frictional unemployment; lower non-wage costs increase competitiveness; compare with other supply-side policies: training and education (structural unemployment), better job-matching services, reducing benefit traps, geographic mobility (housing). Evaluation: deregulation may increase insecure work and inequality, reduce worker morale and productivity; does not help cyclical unemployment; effect depends on the type of unemployment, on how strict regulations were to begin with, and on the size of the informal sector (where rules are often not enforced anyway); justified conclusion on 'most effective'.
Q19Supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): Inflation in a South Asian economy has risen to 14 %, partly because of power shortages and congestion at its main port. The finance minister says supply-side reforms will 'bring prices down within a year'.

  1. (a) Explain why supply-side policies often take a long time to have an effect. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether supply-side policies can reduce inflation in the short run. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Building infrastructure, educating workers or changing behaviour in response to incentives takes years, so productive capacity rises only slowly.
    1 mark: identifies a slow process (training/education, construction, new firms entering); 1 mark: so the shift in LRAS happens after a long time lag.
  2. (b) Mostly not: their main effect on capacity comes in the long run, and extra government spending on infrastructure may even add to AD in the short run. Some measures, such as cutting import duties on inputs or removing supply bottlenecks, can reduce costs fairly quickly, so the effect depends on the policy and the cause of inflation.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: supply-side policy shifts LRAS/AS right, lowering the price level for a given AD (AD/AS diagram); this needs time (training, infrastructure); in the short run interventionist spending raises AD and may increase demand-pull inflation; tax cuts raise disposable income and AD; but some measures act quickly on costs, e.g. removing tariffs on imported inputs, clearing port bottlenecks, deregulating energy supply, which reduce cost-push pressure. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: depends on cause of inflation (cost-push vs demand-pull), type of policy, credibility; usually monetary/fiscal policy is needed in the short run; justified judgement.
Q20Supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether supply-side policy is more effective than fiscal policy in achieving economic growth in a low-income country. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Supply-side policy is essential for sustained growth because it raises potential output, but in a low-income country it is slow and needs funds; fiscal policy can raise actual growth quickly where there is spare capacity, and much supply-side policy is itself funded through the budget. A combination is likely to be most effective.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: distinguish actual and potential growth; fiscal policy (higher G, lower taxes) raises AD and actual output where there is a negative output gap; supply-side policies (education, health, infrastructure, property rights, deregulation) shift LRAS right, raising potential growth without inflation; diagrams. Evaluation: in low-income countries, capacity constraints (power, skills, transport) limit the effect of extra demand, which may become inflation or imports; but supply-side policy is slow, costly and may depend on aid or borrowing; weak institutions and corruption may reduce the effect of both; overlap — infrastructure spending is both; judgement on 'more effective' with conditions.
Q21Monetary policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A central bank in South America is concerned that inflation is well above its 3 % target.

  1. (a) State two instruments of monetary policy. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how an increase in interest rates could reduce the rate of inflation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: the central bank's policy interest rate; the money supply (e.g. open market operations, reserve requirements); quantitative easing; the exchange rate.
    1 mark each for any two correct instruments.
  2. (b) Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, reduce disposable income of borrowers and may raise the exchange rate; consumption, investment and net exports fall, AD grows more slowly and demand-pull pressure on prices eases.
    1 mark: higher cost of borrowing → less credit-financed consumption/investment; 1 mark: higher reward for saving / higher loan repayments reduce disposable income; 1 mark: possible appreciation → cheaper imports, fewer exports; 1 mark: AD falls/grows more slowly → less demand-pull inflation.

Table 5: Car loans in Country M (data invented for practice)Quarter 1: bank lending rate 12 %; car loans approved 48 000Quarter 4: bank lending rate 16 %; car loans approved 39 600

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage fall in the number of car loans approved between Quarter 1 and Quarter 4. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why higher interest rates reduce the number of car loans. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 17.5 %
    M1: (48.0 − 39.6) ÷ 48.0; A1: × 100 = 17.5 %.
  2. (b) Higher interest rates raise monthly repayments, so a car bought on credit becomes more expensive and fewer households can afford or are willing to borrow.
    1 mark: higher cost of borrowing/higher repayments; 1 mark: reduces affordability or willingness to buy on credit (or banks lend less to riskier borrowers).
Q23Monetary policy · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A central bank raises its policy interest rate. Which effect is most likely?A The country's currency depreciates.B Household borrowing to buy cars and other durable goods falls.C The interest cost of the government's debt falls.D Households save a smaller proportion of their income. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B Household borrowing to buy cars and other durable goods falls.
    Higher interest rates raise the cost of loans, so credit-financed spending on durables falls, reducing consumption and AD. A is wrong: higher rates usually attract financial inflows and cause appreciation; D is wrong because saving becomes more rewarding.
Q24Monetary policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 4: Country K (data invented for practice)January 2024: central bank policy rate 15 %; inflation rate 12 %December 2024: central bank policy rate 22 %; inflation rate 29 %

  1. (a) Using the approximation real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate, calculate the real interest rate in January 2024 and in December 2024. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the increase in the policy rate may not have reduced inflation quickly. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) January 2024: +3 %; December 2024: −7 %
    15 − 12 = +3 % (1); 22 − 29 = −7 % (1).
  2. (b) Because inflation rose faster than the policy rate, the real interest rate actually fell (became negative), so borrowing became cheaper in real terms; in addition, monetary policy works with long time lags, and inflation caused by rising import and energy prices (cost-push) is not reduced much by higher interest rates.
    1 mark: real rate fell from +3 % to −7 %, so policy was looser in real terms; 1 mark: time lags (12–24 months) before AD responds; 1 mark: if inflation is cost-push (energy, imports, currency depreciation) higher rates do little to the cause; 1 mark: other relevant reason, e.g. much spending is not financed by bank credit / large informal sector, or government borrowing continued to rise.
Q25Monetary policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a reduction in interest rates might increase aggregate demand, and consider why it might fail to do so during a severe recession. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Lower rates reduce the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, raising consumption and investment, and may depreciate the currency, raising net exports; in a severe recession low confidence, high debt and banks' unwillingness to lend may weaken these effects.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: monetary policy; the components of AD; meaning of a cut in the policy rate. Analysis: cheaper credit → more spending on durables and housing; lower loan repayments raise disposable income; investment projects become profitable; lower rates may cause capital outflows → depreciation → exports rise, imports fall; AD shifts right (diagram). Evaluation: if consumers and firms are pessimistic they may not borrow; banks may ration credit; rates may already be close to zero; time lags; depends on how much spending is financed by credit; judgement.
Q26Monetary policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): A family in Lahore has a variable-rate loan of PKR 2 000 000 on which it pays interest only. The bank's lending rate rises from 18 % to 21 % after the central bank raises its policy rate to fight inflation caused mainly by higher prices of imported fuel and edible oil.

  1. (a) Calculate the increase in the family's annual interest payment and express it as a percentage of the original payment. Give the percentage to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether raising interest rates is an effective way to control inflation caused by rising import prices. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Increase of PKR 60,000 a year, a 16.7 % rise
    M1: 18 % × 2 000 000 = 360,000; 21 % × 2 000 000 = 420,000. A1: rise = 60,000, which is 60,000 ÷ 360,000 × 100 = 16.7 %.
  2. (b) Only partly: higher rates may strengthen the currency and reduce second-round wage and price rises, but they do not lower world prices, and they reduce output and employment, squeezing households already hit by higher prices.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: inflation from import prices is cost-push (AS shifts left); higher interest rates reduce AD (consumption via higher repayments as in the data, investment), which limits second-round effects and inflation expectations; higher rates may attract capital inflows and appreciate the currency, reducing import prices; but they reduce output further, adding to the fall in real GDP (stagflation). Up to 2 marks for evaluation: depends on whether the import price rise is temporary, on how sensitive the exchange rate is to interest rates, on the share of households with variable-rate loans, and on the credibility of the central bank; justified judgement.
Q27Monetary policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether monetary policy is more effective than fiscal policy in reducing inflation. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Monetary policy is often more flexible and independent of politics, and works through several channels, but it has time lags and is weak against cost-push inflation; fiscal policy acts directly on AD but is slower to change and politically difficult. Effectiveness depends on the cause of inflation and the economy's circumstances.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: monetary policy: higher interest rates/reduced money supply → lower C, I and possibly net exports via appreciation → AD falls; independent central banks can act quickly and build credibility on inflation expectations. Fiscal policy: higher taxes, lower spending reduce AD directly and can be targeted; AD/AS diagram for demand-pull inflation. Evaluation: monetary policy affects all regions and sectors equally, may harm investment and raise government debt interest; works poorly where few people borrow from banks; fiscal tightening is politically unpopular, slow (budget timetable) and may cut useful public investment; neither works well against cost-push inflation; coordination matters; justified conclusion.
Q28Macroeconomic policy objectives · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following is NOT one of a government's main macroeconomic policy objectives?A low and stable inflationB a low level of unemploymentC a fixed price for petrolD sustainable economic growth [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C a fixed price for petrol
    The main macroeconomic objectives are economic growth, low unemployment, stable prices (low inflation) and balance of payments stability (plus, often, income redistribution). A fixed petrol price is a microeconomic intervention in one market, not a macroeconomic objective.

Q30Macroeconomic policy objectives · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether keeping inflation low should be a government's most important macroeconomic objective. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Low inflation supports confidence, investment and competitiveness and protects the poor, so it is a strong candidate; but in a recession or with high unemployment, growth and employment may matter more, and the priority depends on the country's current problems.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: the four main objectives; benefits of low and stable inflation: protects real incomes and savings (especially of those on fixed incomes), reduces uncertainty, encourages investment, keeps exports competitive, avoids menu and shoe-leather costs; conflicts between objectives (e.g. low inflation vs low unemployment in the short run); costs of unemployment and low growth. Evaluation: the priority depends on circumstances: with inflation at 25 %, controlling it comes first; with deflation risk or high unemployment, growth and jobs matter more; low inflation can be seen as a means to sustainable growth rather than a rival; central bank inflation targets show many governments give it priority; justified conclusion.
