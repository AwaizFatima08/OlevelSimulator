Q1Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which of the following acts as an automatic stabiliser in an economy? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a progressive income tax system
    As incomes fall in a recession, people move into lower tax bands, so tax paid falls faster than income and disposable income is cushioned, with no new government decision needed. B is discretionary fiscal policy (a deliberate decision), C is monetary policy. D is a protectionist measure, not a stabiliser.
Q2Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 1: Tax revenue of Country P, 2025 (PKR billion; data invented for practice)Income tax: 2900Corporate (company) profits tax: 1100General sales tax: 3400Customs duties: 1200Excise duties: 400

  1. (a) Distinguish between a direct tax and an indirect tax, and identify the direct taxes in Table 1. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate indirect taxes as a percentage of total tax revenue. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a general sales tax is likely to be regressive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A direct tax is paid straight to the government by the person or firm on whom it falls, out of income or wealth; an indirect tax is levied on spending and can be passed on in the price. Direct taxes: income tax and corporate profits tax.
    1 mark for a correct distinction (on income/wealth vs on spending, incidence can be shifted); 1 mark for identifying income tax and corporate profits tax.
  2. (b) 55.6 %
    M1: indirect taxes = 3400 + 1200 + 400 = 5000; total = 9000. A1: 5000 ÷ 9000 × 100 = 55.6 %.
  3. (c) Poorer households spend a larger share of their income on taxed goods, so the tax paid takes a larger percentage of a low income than of a high income.
    1 mark: regressive means the share of income paid in tax falls as income rises; 1 mark: low-income households spend (rather than save) most of their income, so sales tax paid is a higher % of their income.
Q3Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A government in South-East Asia plans to cut the basic rate of income tax from 20 % to 17 % to support spending during a slowdown.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a progressive income tax. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether a cut in income tax rates will increase aggregate demand. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A tax where the proportion of income paid in tax (the average rate) rises as income rises, because higher slices of income are taxed at higher marginal rates.
    1 mark: average rate rises as income rises; 1 mark: higher marginal rates on higher bands of income (or an example).
  2. (b) Usually yes, because disposable income and consumption rise, but the effect may be small if households save the extra income, buy imports, or lack confidence.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: lower tax → higher disposable income (1) → higher consumption (1) → AD (C + I + G + X − M) shifts right (1); possible extra effect on incentives. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: the rise may be small if the extra income is saved (low confidence, debt repayment), spent on imports, or goes mainly to high earners with a low propensity to consume; or offset if spending is cut to pay for it.
Q4Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between automatic stabilisers and discretionary fiscal policy, and consider which is more useful in reducing the effects of a fall in aggregate demand. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Automatic stabilisers change tax revenue and spending without a decision as income changes; discretionary policy is a deliberate change. Stabilisers act quickly but only cushion; discretionary policy can be larger but suffers time lags.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of fiscal policy, automatic stabilisers (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) and discretionary policy (deliberate changes in tax rates or spending); examples. Analysis: in a downturn, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically, supporting disposable income and AD; discretionary policy, e.g. extra infrastructure spending, shifts AD right by a chosen amount; AD/AS diagram. Evaluation: stabilisers work immediately and without political delay but only reduce, not reverse, a fall in AD; discretionary policy can be targeted and larger but has recognition, decision and implementation lags and may arrive too late; usefulness also depends on the size of the welfare system and the government's ability to borrow; judgement.

Q7Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the budget deficit as a percentage of GDP in Year 1 and in Year 2. Give your answers to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Using the data, explain how automatic stabilisers might account for the change in the budget deficit between Year 1 and Year 2. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1: 4.0 %; Year 2: 6.0 %
    Year 1: (10 400 − 8320) = 2080; 2080 ÷ 52 000 × 100 = 4.0 % (1). Year 2: (10 900 − 7860) = 3040; 3040 ÷ 50 400 × 100 = 6.0 % (1).
  2. (b) GDP fell, so incomes, spending and profits fell; tax revenue fell automatically while spending on unemployment and welfare benefits rose automatically, so the deficit increased with no new policy decision.
    1 mark: identifies the fall in nominal GDP (52 000 to 50 400) as a sign of recession; 1 mark: lower incomes/spending/profits → lower revenue from income, sales and profits taxes (revenue fell 8320 → 7860); 1 mark: rising unemployment → more benefit payments, so spending rose (10 400 → 10 900); 1 mark: these changes happen without a government decision and cushion the fall in AD, so the rise in the deficit is partly cyclical.
Q8Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether expansionary fiscal policy is the best way to reduce unemployment during a recession. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expansionary fiscal policy can raise AD and reduce cyclical unemployment, but its success depends on spare capacity, the multiplier, debt levels and time lags; it does little for structural unemployment, and monetary or supply-side policy may be better in some cases.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: higher government spending or lower taxes → higher AD → firms raise output and demand more labour; with a negative output gap there is little inflationary pressure (AD/AS diagram with AD shifting right on the flatter part of AS); spending can be targeted at regions or labour-intensive projects; comparison with monetary policy (interest rate cuts) and supply-side policies (training) is required by 'best'. Evaluation: works for cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment but not structural or frictional; time lags; leakages to imports; a large existing debt may limit borrowing or raise interest rates; confidence may stay low; monetary policy may be faster but weak if interest rates are already near zero; a combination may be best; justified conclusion.
Q9Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a government could use fiscal policy to reduce demand-pull inflation, and consider whether doing so will harm economic growth. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Contractionary fiscal policy (higher taxes, lower spending) reduces AD and so the pressure on prices; it may slow growth in the short run, but stable prices can support long-run growth.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: demand-pull inflation is caused by AD rising faster than the economy's capacity; contractionary (deflationary) fiscal policy. Analysis: higher income tax or sales tax reduces disposable income and consumption; cuts in government spending reduce G directly; AD shifts left, reducing the price level rise (AD/AS diagram near full capacity). Evaluation: real output may fall and unemployment rise in the short run; cutting capital spending may reduce future LRAS; but if the economy is at full capacity the fall in output may be small, and lower inflation improves confidence, investment and competitiveness, helping long-run growth; judgement.
Q10Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the national debt at the end of Year 2 and at the end of Year 3. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the national debt as a percentage of nominal GDP at the end of Year 1 and at the end of Year 3. Give your answers to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Consider whether the fall in the budget deficit from Year 1 to Year 3 shows that the government has made its fiscal policy more contractionary. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 2: $254 billion; Year 3: $266 billion.
    Debt rises by each year's deficit: 240 + 14 = 254 (1); 254 + 12 = 266 (1).
  2. (b) Year 1: 60.0 %; Year 3: 54.3 %.
    240/400 × 100 = 60.0 % (1); 266/490 × 100 = 54.3 % (1).

Up to 2 for judgement: need data on tax rates and planned spending (the structural or cyclically adjusted position); inflation may also raise nominal revenue; the government is still running a deficit, so it is still adding to AD; conclusion that the data alone cannot show the fiscal stance.

Q12Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): A lower-middle-income country collects tax equal to only 9.5 % of its GDP of PKR 110 000 billion. Fewer than 4 % of adults file income-tax returns, and a large informal sector pays little tax. A lender has asked the government to raise the ratio to 12 % within three years.

  1. (a) Calculate the extra tax revenue the government would collect if the tax-to-GDP ratio rose from 9.5 % to 12 % with GDP unchanged. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether widening the tax base is a better way to raise tax revenue than raising existing tax rates. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) PKR 2,750 billion
    M1: (12 % − 9.5 %) = 2.5 % of GDP. A1: 0.025 × 110 000 = PKR 2,750 billion.
  2. (b) Widening the base is usually better for efficiency and fairness, but it is slower and harder to enforce; raising rates gives quicker revenue but may reduce incentives and encourage evasion. A judgement depends on administrative capacity and the size of the informal sector.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: widening the base (bringing traders, retailers, agricultural incomes and property into the net) raises revenue without higher rates, spreads the burden more fairly, and reduces distortions; raising rates on the few who already pay may reduce work and investment incentives, encourage evasion and avoidance, and can be regressive if it relies on sales tax; higher rates may raise revenue quickly. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: a broader base needs records, digital payments and political will, so it is slow; in the short run higher rates may be needed; overall judgement (e.g. a broader base is better for sustained revenue).
Q13Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a government should always aim to balance its budget. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always: deficits can be justified in recessions or for productive investment, and surpluses during booms; but persistent deficits raise debt and interest costs, so a balanced budget over the economic cycle is a more sensible aim than a balanced budget every year.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: meaning of a balanced budget, deficit and surplus; benefits of balance: no rise in national debt, lower interest payments, confidence of lenders, avoids possible crowding out and future tax rises; costs: in a recession, cutting spending or raising taxes to balance the budget reduces AD further and deepens unemployment (AD/AS); deficits can finance infrastructure and education that raise future LRAS and tax revenue; a surplus in a boom can reduce demand-pull inflation. Evaluation: depends on the stage of the cycle, the size of existing debt, whether borrowing is in foreign currency, and what the borrowing pays for (investment vs current spending); conclusion that 'always' is too strong, balance over the cycle is more realistic.
Q14Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Extract (invented for practice): To reduce its budget deficit, a government has raised its general sales tax rate from 17 % to 18 %. An opposition party argues that it should have cut government spending instead.

  1. (a) A good has a price of PKR 1000 before tax. Calculate the percentage increase in its price when the sales tax rate rises from 17 % to 18 %, assuming the tax is fully passed on to consumers. Give your answer to two decimal places. [2]
  2. (b) Assess whether raising the sales tax rate is a better way to reduce the budget deficit than cutting government spending. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.85 %
    M1: price with tax rises from PKR 1170 to PKR 1180. A1: 10 ÷ 1170 × 100 = 0.85 %. (Not 1 %: the base is the tax-inclusive price.)
  2. (b) A sales tax rise raises revenue quickly from a broad base but is regressive and adds to inflation; spending cuts avoid higher prices but can damage public services and growth. The better choice depends on what spending is cut and on the distribution of the burden.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: sales tax raises revenue from a wide base and is hard to avoid on formal-sector sales; it causes a one-off rise in the price level (cost-push) and falls more heavily on the poor (regressive); it reduces consumption and AD. Spending cuts reduce the deficit directly and do not raise prices, but cuts to health, education or infrastructure reduce future growth and welfare; both reduce AD in the short run. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: depends on whether essentials are exempt, on how efficient current spending is, on the state of the economy, and on PED of taxed goods; a justified judgement.

Q18Supply-side policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between market-based and interventionist supply-side policies, and consider which type is likely to increase output more quickly. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Market-based policies rely on incentives and competition (tax cuts, deregulation, privatisation); interventionist policies use government spending and direct action (education, infrastructure, R&D grants). Speed depends on the specific policy; neither type is always faster.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definition of supply-side policy; market-based examples and interventionist examples. Analysis: both aim to shift LRAS right; market-based policies change incentives to work, invest and compete; interventionist policies improve the quality and quantity of factors directly. Evaluation: deregulation or tax incentives can act within months, while education and large infrastructure projects take years; however, market-based policies depend on how firms and workers respond, which may be weak, and may increase inequality; interventionist spending also raises AD quickly; a judgement on which is faster in a given context.

Q25Supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how reducing the power of trade unions could be used as a supply-side policy to increase long-run aggregate supply, and consider whether it is likely to benefit workers. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Weaker unions can make labour markets more flexible: wages adjust more easily, firms can change working practices and hire more, and costs fall, shifting LRAS right. Workers may gain from more jobs, but may lose bargaining power, pay and protection.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: supply-side policy (raising productive capacity/LRAS); market-based policy; role of trade unions (collective bargaining, wage above equilibrium, restrictive practices).   1. (a) Explain one reason why a central bank may set its inflation target as a range, such as 5–7 %, rather than as a single figure. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a fall in unemployment might lead to a worsening of the current account of the balance of payments. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inflation cannot be controlled exactly because of shocks and time lags, so a range allows flexibility without the bank being seen to fail at every small deviation, which keeps the target credible.
    1: inflation is affected by shocks/lags and cannot be hit exactly; 1: a range allows flexibility and protects credibility (avoids over-reacting to small changes).
  2. (b) More people in work means higher incomes and spending, some of which goes on imports; firms may divert output from exports to the home market; a tighter labour market raises wages and costs, so exports become less price-competitive.
    1 mark each for four linked points: higher employment → higher disposable income; higher consumption → more imports (marginal propensity to import); goods diverted to the home market → fewer exports; rising wages/costs → higher prices → exports less competitive; current account deficit widens.

(a) Unemployment has direct human and output costs, so it often deserves priority, but a large deficit financed by short-term borrowing can trigger a currency crisis that raises unemployment later; the priority depends on the size of each problem, how the deficit is financed and the type of unemployment.

Evaluation: deficit may be sustainable if financed by FDI or offset by remittances; deficits from importing capital goods may raise future output; long-term unemployment is hard to reverse; supply-side policy may help both; priority depends on circumstances; justified conclusion.

  1. (a) Assess whether economic growth should be the main macroeconomic objective of a government in a low-income country. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Growth is vital in a low-income country because it raises incomes, jobs and tax revenue for health and education, but rapid growth may bring inflation, a current account deficit, inequality and environmental damage; stable prices and sustainability support growth itself, so growth should be a main but not the only objective.

Evaluation: quality of growth matters (inclusive, sustainable); high inflation and balance of payments crises can halt growth, so macro stability is a precondition; distribution and environmental costs; judgement on priority in context.
