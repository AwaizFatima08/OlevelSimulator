Q1Market structures and price discrimination · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which condition holds for a firm in long-run equilibrium in perfect competition? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A price = marginal cost = minimum average cost
    Free entry and exit remove supernormal profit, so P = AR = MR = MC = minimum AC and the firm earns normal profit. C describes monopolistic competition in the long run (excess capacity).
Q2Market structures and price discrimination · Core · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Describe the kinked demand curve model of oligopoly, explaining the assumptions about how rival firms react. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the model predicts that prices in oligopoly will be stable even when costs change. [3]
  3. (c) State one limitation of the kinked demand curve model. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rivals match price cuts but do not follow price rises, so demand is elastic above the current price and inelastic below it, producing a kink.
    1 mark: assumption that rivals do not follow a price rise – demand above the kink is price elastic (firm loses many customers). 1 mark: rivals follow a price cut – demand below the kink is price inelastic (little gain in sales). 1 mark: kink at the current price. 1 mark: MR curve has a vertical discontinuity (gap) below the kink.
  2. (b) Because MC can shift up or down within the gap in the MR curve without changing the profit-maximising output and price.
    1 mark: profit maximisation where MR = MC. 1 mark: the discontinuity in MR means MC can rise or fall within the gap. 1 mark: output and price stay the same – price rigidity.
  3. (c) It does not explain how the initial price was set (or: evidence shows oligopoly prices do change; ignores non-price competition and collusion).
    B1.

Q4Market structures and price discrimination · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a perfectly contestable market. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how the threat of 'hit-and-run' entry affects the behaviour of a firm in such a market. [3]
  3. (c) Give one example of a sunk cost that might make the market for domestic air travel less contestable. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A market where entry and exit are free and costless, with no sunk costs, so incumbents face the threat of entry whatever the number of firms.
    1 mark: no barriers to entry or exit. 1 mark: no sunk costs, so firms can leave without loss. 1 mark: entrants have access to the same technology / incumbents cannot react instantly; number of firms may be small.
  2. (b) Supernormal profits would attract entrants who take profits and leave, so incumbents keep prices near average cost.
    1 mark: hit-and-run entry described. 1 mark: incumbents therefore limit price to earn only normal profit. 1 mark: they also have an incentive to minimise costs – outcome may be close to productive/allocative efficiency even with few firms.
  3. (c) Advertising to build a brand / training of crew / landing slot fees that cannot be recovered.
    B1 (aircraft themselves can be leased or resold, so are not mainly sunk).
Q5Market structures and price discrimination · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always: monopoly can bring higher prices and lower output, but economies of scale, dynamic efficiency and regulation may benefit consumers.
    Knowledge and understanding / analysis (up to 14): diagram comparing monopoly with perfect competition – monopoly sets MR = MC, higher price, lower output, P > MC (allocative inefficiency), possibly not at minimum AC (productive inefficiency), deadweight loss, transfer of consumer surplus to producer; X-inefficiency without competition; less choice. Counter-arguments: natural monopoly and economies of scale may give lower costs and prices than many small firms; supernormal profit funds research and development (dynamic efficiency, new products); contestable markets restrain prices; price discrimination can allow some consumers to be served who otherwise would not; regulation (price caps) and competition policy. Evaluation (up to 6): depends on barriers to entry, the industry (utilities vs consumer goods), whether profits are reinvested, government regulation; reasoned conclusion on 'always'. Level 4 (15–20) requires a well-supported judgement.
Q6Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 10 marks

A rail company sells peak-time and off-peak tickets on one route (invented data). Peak demand is P = 100 − 2Q and off-peak demand is P = 60 − Q, where P is the fare in dollars and Q is thousands of tickets per week. Marginal cost is constant at $20 per ticket in both markets.

  1. (a) Calculate the profit-maximising quantity and price in each market. [4]
  2. (b) Calculate the price elasticity of demand (as a positive number) in each market at these prices, and explain how this relates to the prices charged. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the firm's total profit from the two markets, ignoring fixed costs. [1]
  4. (d) State two conditions that make it possible for the company to practise this price discrimination. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Peak: Q = 20 thousand, P = $60; off-peak: Q = 20 thousand, P = $40
    Peak: MR = 100 − 4Q = 20 so Q = 20 (M1), P = 100 − 40 = 60 (A1). Off-peak: MR = 60 − 2Q = 20 so Q = 20 (M1), P = 60 − 20 = 40 (A1).
  2. (b) Peak 1.5; off-peak 2.0; the higher price is charged where demand is less price elastic.
    For P = a − bQ, PED = P ÷ (a − P). Peak: 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5 (B1). Off-peak: 40 ÷ 20 = 2.0 (B1). The firm charges more in the market with lower PED (B1).
  3. (c) $1200 thousand
    (60 − 20) × 20 + (40 − 20) × 20 = 800 + 400 = 1200 (B1).
  4. (d) Some monopoly power; markets can be separated (ticket valid only at that time) with no resale; different PEDs in the two markets.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q7Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 6 marks

Two airlines, Alpha and Beta, are the only carriers on a route. Each must choose a high fare or a low fare. The table shows annual profits in $ million (Alpha's profit first, Beta's second) (invented data).Alpha high, Beta high: 60, 60Alpha high, Beta low: 20, 80Alpha low, Beta high: 80, 20Alpha low, Beta low: 35, 35

  1. (a) Identify each airline's dominant strategy, if it has one. Explain your answer. [3]
  2. (b) State the Nash equilibrium and explain why the airlines may be tempted to collude. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both airlines have a dominant strategy of a low fare.
    If Beta sets High, Alpha earns 80 with Low > 60 with High (1). If Beta sets Low, Alpha earns 35 with Low > 20 with High (1). So Low is dominant for Alpha; by symmetry also for Beta (1).
  2. (b) Nash equilibrium: both set low fares ($35 million each). If both colluded on high fares they would each earn $60 million.
    1 mark: Nash equilibrium (Low, Low). 1 mark: joint profit at (High, High) is 120 > 70. 1 mark: this is a prisoners' dilemma – collusion raises profit but each has an incentive to cheat (80 > 60), so agreements are unstable without trust or enforcement.
Q8Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether price discrimination by firms such as airlines and electricity suppliers benefits consumers. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Some consumers gain (lower prices, access to services), others lose (higher prices, loss of consumer surplus); overall benefit depends on output effects and use of profit.
    Analysis (up to 14): definition and conditions (market power, separation of markets, different PED, no resale); third-degree price discrimination diagram with two markets, higher price in the inelastic market; first-degree discrimination converts all consumer surplus to producer surplus. Benefits: lower prices for students, off-peak users or low-income groups; total output may rise; a service may only be viable with discrimination (covering high fixed costs); off-peak pricing spreads demand and uses capacity better; profits may fund investment. Costs: consumers in the inelastic market pay more; loss of consumer surplus; perceived unfairness; may be used to drive out rivals (predatory). Evaluation (up to 6): judge which consumers, whether output increases, whether profits are reinvested and whether regulation protects captive users; reasoned conclusion.
Q9Externalities and cost-benefit analysis · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Households that install solar panels reduce air pollution for their neighbours. Assuming no government intervention, what is true of the market for solar panels? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B marginal social benefit exceeds marginal private benefit, so too few are installed
    There is a positive externality (external benefit), so MSB > MPB and the market output, where MPB = MPC, is below the socially optimal output where MSB = MSC. C describes a negative production externality.
Q10Externalities and cost-benefit analysis · Standard · Paper 4 style · 10 marks

A city government is considering a new metro line. Estimated values over its life, in present-value terms ($ million, invented data):private costs (construction and operation): 900private benefits (fare revenue): 600external benefits (time saved by road users, less pollution, fewer accidents): 520external costs (noise and disruption during construction): 80

  1. (a) Calculate the net private benefit and the net social benefit of the project. [3]
  2. (b) Use your answers to explain why the project would not be built by a private firm but might be undertaken by the government. [3]
  3. (c) Explain two difficulties in valuing the external benefits of the project. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Net private benefit −$300 million (a loss of $300 million); net social benefit +$140 million
    Net private = 600 − 900 = −300 (B1). Social benefit = 600 + 520 = 1120; social cost = 900 + 80 = 980 (M1); NSB = 140 (A1).
  2. (b) A private firm only counts private costs and benefits, which give a loss; the government includes externalities, which make social benefits exceed social costs.
    1 mark: private firm makes a loss of $300 million. 1 mark: external benefits (time saved, cleaner air, fewer accidents) are not paid for by users, so they are ignored by the market. 1 mark: social benefits exceed social costs, so CBA supports public provision or a subsidy.
  3. (c) Placing money values on time saved and lives saved; predicting future usage; choosing a discount rate; deciding which third parties to include.
    2 marks each for two explained difficulties (identify 1, explain 1).
Q11Externalities and cost-benefit analysis · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

In a cement market, the marginal private benefit is MPB = 100 − Q and the marginal private cost is MPC = 20 + Q, where Q is output in thousand tonnes and values are in dollars per tonne. Production causes air pollution with a constant external cost of $20 per tonne. There are no external benefits (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the free-market output and the socially optimal output. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the deadweight welfare loss caused by the externality. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the free market overproduces cement in this case. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Free market 40 thousand tonnes; social optimum 30 thousand tonnes
    Market: MPB = MPC: 100 − Q = 20 + Q so Q = 40 (B1). MSC = MPC + 20 = 40 + Q (M1); with no external benefit MSB = MPB, so 100 − Q = 40 + Q gives Q = 30 (A1).
  2. (b) $100 thousand
    Triangle between MSC and MSB from Q = 30 to 40: at Q = 40, MSC − MSB = 80 − 60 = 20 (M1); ½ × 10 × 20 = 100 (A1).
  3. (c) Producers ignore the external cost of pollution, so they produce where MPB = MPC, beyond the output where MSB = MSC.
    1 mark: external cost not included in private costs. 1 mark: so MPC < MSC and the market output (40) exceeds the social optimum (30), where the extra units cost society more than they are worth.
Q12Externalities and cost-benefit analysis · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether cost-benefit analysis is a reliable method for deciding whether a government should build a large dam for irrigation and electricity. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) CBA provides a systematic framework including externalities, but valuations, forecasts, discount rates and distributional issues make results uncertain.
    Analysis (up to 14): stages of CBA – identify private and external costs and benefits (irrigation, electricity, flood control vs construction, displacement of communities, loss of farmland, ecological damage, downstream effects); place money values (shadow pricing, willingness to pay); discount future values to present value; compare social benefits with social costs. Strengths: considers third parties, makes assumptions transparent, allows comparison of projects. Weaknesses: valuing intangibles (lives, cultural sites, biodiversity); forecasting over 50+ years; choice of discount rate can reverse the decision; who gains and who loses (distribution) is not shown by net figures; political bias in estimates; risks such as earthquakes. Evaluation (up to 6): CBA is better than ignoring external effects but should be combined with sensitivity analysis and consultation; reasoned conclusion.
Q13Indifference curves and budget lines · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A consumer has an income of $1200 to spend on two goods, X and Y. The price of X is $40 and the price of Y is $30 (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the maximum quantity of each good the consumer could buy, and the slope of the budget line (Y on the vertical axis). [3]
  2. (b) The price of X falls to $30. Describe how the budget line changes. [2]
  3. (c) State the condition for the consumer's optimum choice in terms of indifference curves and the budget line. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) X intercept 30; Y intercept 40; slope −4/3 (−1.33)
    1200 ÷ 40 = 30 units of X (B1); 1200 ÷ 30 = 40 units of Y (B1); slope = −Px/Py = −40/30 = −4/3 (B1).
  2. (b) It pivots outwards from the Y intercept (40): the X intercept rises to 40 and the slope becomes −1.
    1 mark pivot about the Y intercept. 1 mark new X intercept 40 / slope −1.
  3. (c) Where the budget line is tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve (MRS = Px/Py).
    B1.
Q14Indifference curves and budget lines · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) For a Giffen good, which describes the effects of a rise in its price? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B the substitution effect reduces quantity demanded and the income effect increases it by a larger amount
    A Giffen good is a strongly inferior good: a price rise lowers real income, which raises quantity demanded by more than the substitution effect reduces it, so quantity rises. D describes an ordinary inferior good.
Q15Indifference curves and budget lines · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the usefulness of indifference curve analysis in explaining why demand curves usually slope downwards. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) It separates income and substitution effects, explains normal, inferior and Giffen goods without measuring utility, but rests on unrealistic assumptions.
    Analysis (up to 14): indifference curves (convex, MRS diminishing), budget lines, optimum at tangency; price fall pivots budget line; derive demand curve from successive tangencies (price-consumption curve); separation into substitution effect (always negative) and income effect (positive for normal, negative for inferior goods); explains why most demand curves slope down and the Giffen exception; uses ordinal not cardinal utility, an advance on marginal utility theory. Limitations: assumes rational, consistent, fully informed consumers; only two goods; preferences fixed; behavioural economics (habits, bounded rationality, framing) challenges it; hard to test. Evaluation (up to 6): a reasoned judgement on usefulness – strong theoretical insight, weaker predictive power; conclusion.
Q16Indifference curves and budget lines · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Using indifference curves and budget lines described in words, explain the income and substitution effects of a fall in the price of an inferior good that is not a Giffen good. [6]
  2. (b) Explain how the result would differ for a Giffen good. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Substitution effect increases quantity; income effect reduces it; the substitution effect is larger, so quantity demanded rises overall.
    1 mark: budget line pivots outwards; new tangency on a higher indifference curve. 1 mark: hypothetical (compensated) budget line parallel to the new one, tangent to the original indifference curve, separates the effects. 1 mark: substitution effect – movement along original curve; always increases quantity of the cheaper good. 1 mark: income effect – parallel shift; for an inferior good, higher real income reduces quantity. 1 mark: substitution effect outweighs income effect. 1 mark: net result – quantity demanded rises, so the demand curve slopes downwards.
  2. (b) The negative income effect is larger than the substitution effect, so quantity demanded falls when price falls (upward-sloping demand).
    1 mark income effect outweighs substitution effect. 1 mark quantity falls when price falls.
Q17Costs, revenue and production · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A bakery has a fixed number of ovens. The table shows total product (loaves per hour) for different numbers of workers (invented data).Workers: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6Total product: 10, 26, 45, 60, 70, 75

  1. (a) Calculate the marginal product and the average product of labour for each number of workers. [3]
  2. (b) State after which worker diminishing marginal returns set in, and explain why they occur. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) MP: 10, 16, 19, 15, 10, 5; AP: 10.0, 13.0, 15.0, 15.0, 14.0, 12.5
    MP = change in TP for each extra worker (B1 all correct); AP = TP ÷ workers (B1 all correct); accuracy (B1).
  2. (b) After the 3rd worker (MP falls from 19 to 15 with the 4th worker).
    B1 identification. Explanation (2): in the short run at least one factor (the bakery's ovens/space) is fixed; adding more variable labour to the fixed factor eventually gives each worker less capital to work with, so the extra output falls.
Q18Costs, revenue and production · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is meant by the minimum efficient scale of production? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B the lowest output at which long-run average cost is minimised
    Minimum efficient scale is the smallest output at which the long-run average cost curve reaches its minimum, i.e. where economies of scale are exhausted. A refers to marginal cost, which reaches its minimum before average cost does.
Q19Costs, revenue and production · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A perfectly competitive firm has the following total costs (invented data).Output (units): 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6Total cost ($): 120, 180, 220, 252, 300, 370, 462

  1. (a) Calculate the average variable cost, average total cost and marginal cost for outputs of 1 to 6 units. [3]
  2. (b) State the minimum price the firm needs in order to continue producing in the short run, and in the long run. Explain your answers. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) AVC: 60, 50, 44, 45, 50, 57; ATC: 180, 110, 84, 75, 74, 77; MC: 60, 40, 32, 48, 70, 92
    FC = 120 (TC at zero output). AVC = (TC − 120) ÷ Q (B1); ATC = TC ÷ Q (B1); MC = change in TC (B1).
  2. (b) Short run: $44 (minimum AVC); long run: $74 (minimum ATC)
    B1 $44: in the short run the firm stays open if price covers AVC, because fixed costs must be paid anyway (B1). B1 $74: in the long run all costs are variable, so price must cover ATC or the firm leaves the industry (B1).
Q20Costs, revenue and production · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A firm faces the demand curve P = 80 − 2Q, where P is the price in dollars and Q is the quantity per day (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the output and price at which total revenue is maximised, and the value of total revenue. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the price elasticity of demand (as a positive number) when the price is $60, and explain whether the firm should raise or cut its price from $60 to increase revenue. [3]
  3. (c) State the price elasticity of demand at the revenue-maximising output. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q = 20, P = $40, TR = $800
    TR = 80Q − 2Q²; MR = 80 − 4Q (M1); MR = 0 at Q = 20 (A1); P = 40, TR = 800 (A1).
  2. (b) PED = 3; demand is price elastic, so cut the price.
    At P = 60, Q = 10; dQ/dP = −½ (M1); PED = ½ × 60 ÷ 10 = 3 (A1). Elastic, so a price cut raises TR (MR > 0) (B1).
  3. (c) Unit elasticity (PED = 1)
    B1.

Q22Utility and marginal utility theory · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A consumer has Rs 900 to spend on goods X and Y. The price of X is Rs 100 and the price of Y is Rs 200. The table shows the marginal utility (utils) of each unit (invented data).Unit: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5MU of X: 400, 300, 200, 150, 100MU of Y: 1000, 800, 400, 300, 200

  1. (a) Calculate the combination of X and Y that maximises the consumer's total utility, and the total utility at that combination. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the consumer would not choose 5 units of X and 2 units of Y, even though this also costs Rs 900. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3 units of X and 3 units of Y; total utility 3100 utils
    MU/P for X: 4, 3, 2, 1.5, 1; for Y: 5, 4, 2, 1.5, 1 (M1). Equi-marginal principle: MUx/Px = MUy/Py = 2 at 3X, 3Y (M1); spending 3 × 100 + 3 × 200 = 900 = income (A1). TU = 400 + 300 + 200 + 1000 + 800 + 400 = 3100 (A1).
  2. (b) At that combination MUx/Px (1) < MUy/Py (4), so switching spending from X to Y raises total utility (TU = 2950 < 3100).
    1 mark: ratios unequal. 1 mark: transferring Rs 200 from 2 units of X to 1 unit of Y adds more utility than it loses; TU would be 1150 + 1800 = 2950.
Q23Utility and marginal utility theory · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that consumers rarely behave in the way that traditional utility theory predicts. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Traditional theory explains many broad patterns (downward-sloping demand), but behavioural evidence shows systematic departures; the importance depends on the decision.
    Analysis (up to 14): traditional theory – rational utility maximisers, diminishing MU, equi-marginal principle, derivation of demand. Behavioural evidence – bounded rationality and heuristics, anchoring (initial prices), framing ('90 % fat-free'), loss aversion, default choices (pension opt-out), herd behaviour, present bias (saving, unhealthy food), altruism. Governments use nudges based on these ideas. Counter-view: in aggregate, demand still falls when price rises; for repeated purchases consumers learn; some 'irrational' behaviour is rational given information costs. Evaluation (up to 6): weigh the extent – theory useful for predicting market-level responses, weaker for one-off, complex or emotional decisions (health, pensions); reasoned conclusion on 'rarely'.
Q24Utility and marginal utility theory · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the principle of diminishing marginal utility can be used to derive a downward-sloping demand curve for an individual consumer. [4]
  2. (b) Explain two limitations of the assumption that consumers behave rationally when making choices. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A consumer buys until MU/P is equal across goods; if price falls, MU/P rises, so the consumer buys more until MU falls enough to restore equality.
    1 mark: diminishing MU – each extra unit adds less satisfaction. 1 mark: equi-marginal condition MUa/Pa = MUb/Pb. 1 mark: a fall in Pa makes MUa/Pa > MUb/Pb. 1 mark: consumer buys more A, MUa falls until equality is restored – lower price, higher quantity demanded.
  2. (b) e.g. bounded rationality (limited information and time), habits, anchoring/framing, herd behaviour, inconsistent preferences over time.
    2 marks each for two explained limitations drawn from behavioural economics: bounded rationality, rules of thumb (heuristics), anchoring, social norms and herd behaviour, present bias (choosing short-term pleasure), and difficulty measuring utility in cardinal units.
Q25Efficiency and market failure · Core · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Define allocative efficiency and productive efficiency, stating the condition for each. [4]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by dynamic efficiency. [2]
  3. (c) Explain what is meant by Pareto optimality. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Allocative: resources produce the goods consumers value most, P = MC. Productive: output is produced at the lowest possible average cost, at minimum AC (MC = AC).
    1 mark each definition, 1 mark each condition.
  2. (b) Efficiency over time through innovation and investment that lower costs or create better products.
    1 mark: improvements over time. 1 mark: via research and development, new technology and new products, often financed from profits.
  3. (c) A position where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
    1 mark definition; 1 mark link: it requires both allocative and productive efficiency in all markets.

Q27Efficiency and market failure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 12 marks

Invented data: a pharmaceutical company spent $1.2 billion over 12 years developing a new medicine. Only about 1 in 10 medicines that reach clinical trials are eventually approved. The new medicine is protected by a 20-year patent. Its marginal cost of production is about $30 per course of treatment, but it is sold for $900 per course.

  1. (a) Explain why a 20-year patent may reduce allocative efficiency. [4]
  2. (b) With reference to the data, evaluate whether patents increase economic efficiency overall. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) It gives the firm monopoly power, so it sets price above marginal cost and restricts output.
    1 mark: patent = legal barrier to entry. 1 mark: firm sets MR = MC. 1 mark: price ($900) far above marginal cost ($30). 1 mark: output below the level where P = MC; some patients who value the drug above its cost cannot afford it – deadweight loss.
  2. (b) Patents trade static allocative efficiency for dynamic efficiency; the overall effect depends on how much innovation they cause and how high prices are.
    Analysis (up to 6): high R&D costs ($1.2 billion) and a low chance of success (1 in 10) mean firms would not invest without the chance of supernormal profit; without patents, rivals could copy at marginal cost; so patents promote dynamic efficiency (new medicines). Costs: price 30 times MC, large deadweight loss, low-income patients excluded; firms may make small changes to extend patents. After expiry, generic producers lower prices. Evaluation (up to 2): judgement – efficiency gains likely where innovation is costly and risky, but shorter patents, price regulation or tiered pricing for low-income countries could reduce the static losses.
Q28Growth, survival and objectives of firms · Standard · Paper 4 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain the principal-agent problem that can arise from the divorce of ownership and control in large public limited companies. [4]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which shareholders might reduce this problem. [4]
  3. (c) Explain what is meant by 'satisficing'. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Shareholders (principals) want maximum profit, but managers (agents) may pursue their own goals such as high salaries, sales growth or status.
    1 mark: divorce of ownership (shareholders) and control (managers). 1 mark: managers have more information than shareholders (asymmetric information). 1 mark: managers may pursue sales/revenue maximisation, growth or perks. 1 mark: result – profits lower than shareholders want.
  2. (b) Link managers' pay to profits or share price (share options, bonuses); vote out directors / threat of takeover; independent non-executive directors and audits.
    2 marks each for two explained methods.
  3. (c) Aiming for a satisfactory level of profit that keeps shareholders content, while also meeting the goals of other stakeholders.
    1 mark: satisfactory rather than maximum profit. 1 mark: balancing competing stakeholder objectives.
Q29Growth, survival and objectives of firms · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A firm faces the demand curve P = 120 − 2Q and has total cost TC = 400 + 20Q, where P is in dollars and Q in units per week. Total cost includes normal profit (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the output, price and profit if the firm maximises profit. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the output, price and profit if the firm maximises total revenue. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the largest output the firm can sell while still earning at least normal profit (sales maximisation). Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q = 25, P = $70, profit = $850
    MR = 120 − 4Q; MC = 20; Q = 25 (M1); P = 70 (A1); profit = 70 × 25 − (400 + 500) = 850 (A1).
  2. (b) Q = 30, P = $60, profit = $800
    MR = 0 at Q = 30 (M1); P = 60 (A1); profit = 1800 − (400 + 600) = 800 (A1).
  3. (c) Q = 45.6 (price ≈ $28.77)
    Profit = 0: 120Q − 2Q² − 400 − 20Q = 0, so Q² − 50Q + 200 = 0 (M1); Q = (50 + √1700) ÷ 2 = 45.6 (A1).
Q30Growth, survival and objectives of firms · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether the vertical integration of firms is likely to benefit consumers. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) It may lower costs and secure supply, benefiting consumers through lower prices and reliability, but can increase market power and foreclose rivals.
    Analysis (up to 14): distinguish backward and forward vertical integration (with horizontal and conglomerate as contrast); motives – control of supplies and quality, removing intermediaries' profit margins, reducing transaction costs, securing retail outlets, economies of scale; benefits to consumers – lower costs passed on as lower prices, more reliable supply, better coordination and quality. Risks – market power increases; rivals may be denied access to inputs or outlets (foreclosure), raising barriers to entry; reduced competition leads to higher prices; diseconomies of scale and X-inefficiency in a large firm; managers may lack expertise in the new stage. Evaluation (up to 6): depends on market structure at each stage, competition policy, whether cost savings are passed on; reasoned conclusion.
