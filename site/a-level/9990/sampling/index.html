Q1Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by an opportunity sample. [2]
  2. (b) Give one strength and one weakness of opportunity sampling. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants are selected because they are available and willing at the time the researcher is collecting data, e.g. asking people who are in a college canteen at lunchtime.
    1 mark: people who are available/convenient at the time. 1 mark: elaboration or example (researcher approaches them).
  2. (b) Strength: quick, cheap and easy to obtain participants. Weakness: likely to be unrepresentative because only people in one place at one time are sampled (and the researcher may choose people who look approachable), so findings may not generalise.
    1 mark: strength. 1 mark: weakness with reason.
Q2Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a researcher could obtain a volunteer (self-selected) sample for a study of online gaming habits among young adults in Lahore. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of using a volunteer sample in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Place an advertisement (e.g. on a university notice board or a gaming forum) describing the study and asking young adults aged 18 to 25 who play online games to contact the researcher if they would like to take part.
    1 mark: advertisement/request for participants. 1 mark: participants choose to respond/sign up, linked to context.
  2. (b) People who reply may be especially keen gamers, or more confident and helpful, so the sample may over-represent heavy gaming and not reflect typical young adults (volunteer bias).
    1 mark: volunteer bias. 1 mark: linked to the gaming study.
Q3Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a random sample of 25 pupils could be selected from a school of 250 pupils in Hyderabad. [2]
  2. (b) State the probability that any one pupil is selected. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Obtain a list of all 250 pupils, give each a number, then use a random number generator (or put all names in a container and draw out 25) to choose 25 pupils.
    1 mark: list of the whole target population. 1 mark: random selection method (lottery/random number generator) giving each pupil an equal chance.
  2. (b) 0.1 (1 in 10).
    B1: 25 ÷ 250 = 0.1.
Q4Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by a snowball sample. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why snowball sampling would be useful for a study of people recovering from gambling problems. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researcher finds a few participants from the target population, then asks them to recommend or recruit other people they know who fit the criteria; those people recruit others, so the sample grows.
    1 mark: initial small group found. 1 mark: they recruit/refer further participants.
  2. (b) People with gambling problems are hard to identify and may hide it; existing participants know others in a similar situation (e.g. from support groups) and can approach them with trust, so the researcher can reach a hidden population.
    1 mark: hard-to-reach / hidden population. 1 mark: participants know and trust others like them.
Q5Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a truly random sample is often difficult to obtain in psychology. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researcher needs a complete list of everyone in the target population, which is often unavailable. Even if names are drawn randomly, some selected people may refuse or not be contactable, so the final sample becomes partly self-selected. It can also be time-consuming and costly to reach people who are spread over a wide area.
    1 mark each for any three of: need full list; refusals/non-response make it partly volunteer; time/cost; selected people may be hard to reach.
Q6Sampling techniques · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain one ethical advantage of using a volunteer sample compared with an opportunity sample. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Volunteers have actively chosen to take part after reading about the study, so they are more likely to have given genuine informed consent and feel free to refuse, whereas people approached face-to-face may feel pressured to agree.
    1 mark: volunteers choose freely / informed. 1 mark: comparison – less pressure than being approached directly.

Q8Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical snowball sample for a study of home-based tailors in Faisalabad, the researcher starts with 3 tailors. In each round of recruitment, every tailor recruited in the previous round (starting with the first 3) recruits exactly 2 new tailors. There are three rounds of recruitment. Calculate the total number of participants. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why this sample may be biased. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 45 participants.
    M1: 3 + 6 + 12 + 24. A1: 45.
  2. (b) Tailors recruit people they know, so the sample will consist of connected friendship or family networks, possibly from the same area and with similar working conditions, and tailors outside these networks will be missed.
    1 mark: participants recruit similar people / from own network. 1 mark: so sample unrepresentative of all home-based tailors.
Q9Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher placed an advertisement for a study about sleep in a newspaper read by about 4500 people in Rawalpindi. 300 people replied. Calculate the percentage of readers who replied. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [1]
  2. (b) Name the sampling technique and explain why the researcher should be cautious about generalising the results. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6.7 %
    B1: 300 ÷ 4500 × 100 = 6.666… = 6.7 %.
  2. (b) Volunteer (self-selected) sampling. Only a small proportion (6.7 %) replied, and those who reply may be different from other readers – e.g. people with sleep problems who are more interested in the topic, or people with more free time – and newspaper readers themselves may not represent the general population.
    1 mark: volunteer sampling. 1 mark: respondents are a small, self-selected group with particular characteristics. 1 mark: readers of one newspaper not typical of whole population.
Q10Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe 'random sampling' and 'opportunity sampling', using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Random sampling: every member of the target population has an equal chance of being selected; the researcher needs a complete list of the population and uses a lottery or random number generator, e.g. picking 30 staff numbers at random from a hospital's list of 600 nurses. It avoids researcher bias in selection but may still produce an unrepresentative sample by chance and can be time-consuming. Opportunity sampling: the researcher uses whoever is available and willing at the time, e.g. asking passengers waiting at a bus terminal; it is quick and convenient but likely to be biased towards certain types of people (e.g. those who use that terminal at that time).
    3 marks each: 1 mark definition, 1 mark elaboration (how done / strength or weakness), 1 mark example.
Q11Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to study the experiences of refugees from one region who have settled in a city. Explain which sampling technique would be most appropriate and why. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Snowball sampling: there is no complete list of refugees, so random sampling is not possible; refugees may be wary of strangers, but if a few trusted participants are found (e.g. through a community centre) they can introduce others, allowing the researcher to reach enough participants.
    1 mark: snowball. 1 mark: no list / hard to reach. 1 mark: trust and referral explanation.
Q12Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical study, a researcher stood outside a gym in Dubai at 7 a.m. and asked people leaving whether they would complete a questionnaire about healthy eating. Identify the sampling technique and explain two reasons why the results may be biased. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Opportunity sampling. 1. People leaving a gym early in the morning are probably more health-conscious than average, so they may report healthier eating than the general population. 2. Only people present at that time and place could be chosen (e.g. not shift workers or people who cannot afford gym fees), and the researcher may approach people who seem friendly, so the sample is not representative.
    1 mark: opportunity sample. For each reason: 1 mark identification, 1 mark explanation linked to the gym context.
Q13Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a researcher might choose an opportunity sample even though it is likely to be unrepresentative. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is the quickest and cheapest way of obtaining participants, and in some studies (e.g. a laboratory experiment on a basic process such as visual perception) the researcher assumes the process works the same in most people, so representativeness matters less.
    1 mark: practicality (time, cost, ease). 1 mark: elaboration, e.g. universal processes or pilot studies.
Q14Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe 'volunteer sampling' and 'snowball sampling', using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Volunteer sampling: participants select themselves, usually by responding to an advertisement, e.g. a poster in a library asking students to take part in a memory study; it gives willing and often committed participants but may be biased towards people who are more motivated or have more time. Snowball sampling: the researcher begins with a few participants who meet the criteria and asks them to recruit others they know, e.g. finding a few people who have recovered from a phobia, who then introduce others; it allows access to hard-to-reach groups, but the sample may contain people with similar characteristics because they come from the same networks.
    3 marks each: 1 mark definition, 1 mark elaboration (strength/weakness or procedure), 1 mark example.
Q15Sampling techniques · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A school has 480 pupils: 180 in Year 10, 150 in Year 11, 90 in Year 12 and 60 in Year 13. A researcher wants a stratified sample of 48 pupils, with each year group represented in proportion to its size.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of pupils to be sampled from each year group. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a stratified sample is more suitable than a simple random sample in this study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 10: 180 ÷ 480 × 48 = 18. Year 11: 150 ÷ 480 × 48 = 15. Year 12: 90 ÷ 480 × 48 = 9. Year 13: 60 ÷ 480 × 48 = 6. (Total 48.)
    1 mark for the method (each group's share of 480 multiplied by 48); 1 mark for 18 and 15; 1 mark for 9 and 6.
  2. (b) A stratified sample guarantees that every year group is represented in proportion to its size, so the sample reflects the school's structure. A simple random sample could by chance include too few pupils from one year group, which would bias the results.
    1 mark for ensuring each year group is represented in proportion; 1 mark for explaining that a random sample could by chance under-represent a group; 1 mark for linking this to reducing bias or improving representativeness.
Q16Sampling techniques · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A college in Karachi has 800 students. A researcher randomly selects 40 students for a study of exam stress, but 14 of the selected students refuse to take part. Calculate the percentage of selected students who refused. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how this refusal rate affects the representativeness of the sample. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 35 %
    B1: 14 ÷ 40 × 100 = 35 %.
  2. (b) The final sample of 26 is no longer truly random because the people who agreed are, in effect, volunteers. Students who refused may be different – e.g. the most stressed students might avoid a study about stress – so the sample may underestimate exam stress, and the smaller sample is also more affected by individual differences.
    1 mark: sample becomes partly self-selected. 1 mark: refusers may differ systematically (linked to stress). 1 mark: consequence – biased results / smaller sample.
Q17Sampling techniques · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Compare random sampling and snowball sampling in terms of representativeness and practicality. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Representativeness: random sampling gives every member of the population an equal chance and avoids researcher bias, so it is more likely to be representative; snowball samples consist of linked social networks, so they are likely to be biased towards similar people. Practicality: random sampling needs a full list of the population and may be impossible for hidden groups (e.g. people with an addiction), whereas snowball sampling is practical for hard-to-reach populations because participants recruit others they know.
    2 marks representativeness comparison (1 each side), 2 marks practicality comparison (1 each side).
Q18Sampling techniques · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a researcher could conduct a study using a questionnaire to investigate attitudes towards mental-health help-seeking among university students in Islamabad. Your plan must include the sampling technique, and you must explain how it would be carried out. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sampling: random sample – obtain the list of all registered undergraduates from the university, number them, use a random number generator to select 100; contact by university email with information sheet and consent form; replace non-responders by drawing more names and record the response rate. Questionnaire: closed questions with a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree), e.g. 'I would see a counsellor if I felt low for two weeks'; some open questions, e.g. 'What would stop you seeking help?'; demographic questions (age, gender, course). Procedure: online or paper in a quiet room, standardised instructions, about 15 minutes, anonymous with ID codes, right to withdraw, debrief with details of the counselling service. Analysis: total attitude score (quantitative) compared between groups; themes from open answers. Pilot the questionnaire with a few students.
    Level 4 (9–10): sampling technique named and fully described (how selected, from what population, size, recruitment); questionnaire details with example items; procedure replicable; ethics. Level 3 (6–8): most features with some detail. Level 2 (3–5): basic plan, sampling vague. Level 1 (1–2): very limited.
Q19Sampling techniques · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study needs a sample of 60 shoppers at a large market in Peshawar. Ali suggests asking the first 60 people who walk past a stall. Fatima suggests putting up a poster asking shoppers to sign up. Evaluate each suggestion and recommend which is better for reducing bias. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ali (opportunity): quick and gives a sample of people actually shopping, but only those passing one stall at one time are included, and passers-by in a hurry may refuse. Fatima (volunteer): participants choose freely, but only shoppers who notice the poster and have time or interest will sign up, creating volunteer bias and possibly a slow, small sample. Recommendation: e.g. Ali's method adapted – approach every 5th shopper at different stalls and times of day – reduces both selection by the researcher and self-selection bias; credit any justified recommendation.
    1 mark: evaluation of Ali's suggestion. 1 mark: evaluation of Fatima's suggestion. 1 mark: clear recommendation. 1 mark: justification about bias.
Q20Sampling techniques · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants a random sample from all 1500 adults registered at a health clinic in Lahore. He chooses 90 adults using random numbers, and 54 of them complete his questionnaire. Calculate (i) the percentage of registered adults who were selected, and (ii) the percentage of selected adults who completed the questionnaire. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way the researcher could increase the response rate without making the sample less random. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 6 % (ii) 60 %
    B1: 90 ÷ 1500 × 100 = 6 %. B1: 54 ÷ 90 × 100 = 60 %.
  2. (b) Send reminders to the selected adults who have not responded (or make the questionnaire shorter/available by phone or online), rather than replacing them with whoever is available, so that the original randomly chosen people are more likely to take part.
    1 mark: suitable method (reminders, convenience of format, short questionnaire, explanation of importance). 1 mark: keeps the originally selected random sample.

Q22Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a representative sample and why it is important. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A representative sample has the same important characteristics as the target population (e.g. similar proportions of ages, genders and backgrounds), so findings from it can be generalised to the population.
    1 mark: sample reflects the characteristics of the population. 1 mark: allows generalisation.
Q23Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Define 'sampling frame', and give one example of a sampling frame for a study of nurses in Pakistan. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A sampling frame is the list of people in the target population from which the sample is actually drawn. Example: a staff list or register of nurses held by a hospital or a nursing council.
    1 mark for the definition (list from which the sample is drawn); 1 mark for a suitable example.
Q24Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'generalisation', and identify one reason why generalising from a sample of 20 students is limited. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Generalisation means applying the findings from the sample to the whole target population. A sample of 20 students is small and may not represent people of other ages or backgrounds, so the findings may not apply to them.
    1 mark for the definition (applying findings to the target population); 1 mark for a limit (small and unrepresentative sample, e.g. only students).
Q25Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a researcher should define the target population more narrowly than "people" in a study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A narrow definition, such as "adults aged 18 to 30 who use social media every day", makes it clear who the findings apply to and allows a sample to be drawn from that group. A vague definition makes it impossible to judge whether the sample represents the population.
    1 mark for a precise definition is needed to identify the group; 1 mark for it allowing a sample to be drawn from the group; 1 mark for it allowing the representativeness of the sample to be judged.
Q26Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why researchers do not usually study the whole target population. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is usually too time-consuming, expensive or impractical to study everyone in the target population, so a sample is drawn that is intended to represent it.
    1 mark for time, cost or practicality; 1 mark for the idea that a sample stands in for the population.
Q27Populations and samples · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks

  1. (a) A representative sample reflects the important characteristics of the population, so the results can be generalised to that population rather than applying only to the people studied.
    1 mark for a representative sample reflecting the population; 1 mark for allowing generalisation of the findings.
Q28Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study wanted to investigate the reading habits of teenagers in Pakistan. The researcher's sample was 120 students from private English-medium schools in Islamabad, of whom 36 were girls. Calculate the percentage of the sample who were girls. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which this sample may not represent the target population. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 30 %
    B1: 36 ÷ 120 × 100 = 30 %.
  2. (b) 1. Only 30 % were girls, whereas girls make up about half of teenagers, so girls' reading habits are under-represented. 2. All students came from private English-medium schools in one city; teenagers in government or Urdu-medium schools, in rural areas or not in school may have very different access to books and reading habits.

Q29Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical experiment on the effect of caffeine on concentration used 20 psychology undergraduates from one university in the UK. Explain why the target population may be narrower than the researchers claim. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researchers may claim the results apply to 'adults', but psychology undergraduates are mostly young, educated and from one country, and many may know about research aims. People who are older, from other cultures, or who rarely drink caffeine may react differently, so the findings really only generalise to similar students.
    1 mark: identifies the narrow sample characteristics. 1 mark: explains how other groups may differ in caffeine response or concentration. 1 mark: conclusion that the population the results apply to is limited.
Q30Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A survey of 400 students at a Peshawar university found that 208 of the respondents were women. The university has 5,000 students, of whom 2,900 are women. The data are hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of women in (i) the sample and (ii) the university population. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why this sample may not be representative of the university's students, and suggest one way to improve it. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 208 ÷ 400 × 100 = 52.0%. (ii) 2900 ÷ 5000 × 100 = 58.0%.
    1 mark for 52.0%; 1 mark for 58.0%.
  2. (b) Women make up a smaller share of the sample (52.0%) than of the population (58.0%), so women are under-represented and the sample is biased in favour of men. Improvement: use a stratified sample by gender, drawing the number of women and men in proportion to the population.
    1 mark for identifying the under-representation of women; 1 mark for explaining the bias; 1 mark for a suitable improvement (stratified sampling by gender, or weighting).
Q31Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

  1. (a) The sample covers only one age group and one type of school, so it does not represent all teenagers, such as those in rural areas or in state schools. It also covers only one city, so cultural and regional differences are not included. Thirty pupils is also a small number.
    1 mark each for two reasons (one age group; one school type or social background; one city or region; small size); 1 mark for explaining each reason in terms of the target population.
Q32Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'bias' in a sample, and give two examples of how a sample can be biased. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bias means that some members of the target population are more or less likely to be included than others, so the sample does not reflect the population. Examples: a sample drawn only from people who visit a particular clinic; a sample where most volunteers are highly motivated people.
    1 mark for the definition (unequal chance of selection); 1 mark for each of two examples (e.g. one location, volunteers only, a gender imbalance).
Q33Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A radio phone-in show asked listeners whether they supported a new law. The show received 812 calls, and 530 callers said they supported the law. The data are hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of callers who supported the law. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why this result cannot be taken as the views of Lahore residents. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 530 ÷ 812 × 100 = 65.3%.
    M1 divide 530 by 812 and multiply by 100; A1 65.3%.
  2. (b) Callers are self-selected, so people with strong opinions, or those who listen to the programme, are more likely to call; the sample is not random and does not represent all residents. The poll also covered only callers to one programme, and many residents may not have heard of the law or been able to call.
    1 mark for self-selection or volunteer bias; 1 mark for the sample not representing all residents (only programme listeners); 1 mark for the effect on the conclusion (cannot generalise to all Lahore residents).
Q34Populations and samples · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A study of sleep used a sample of 240 adults from a town of 12,000 adults. Of the 240 sample members, 12 were aged 65 or over. The town has 1,800 adults aged 65 or over. The data are hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of people aged 65 or over in (i) the sample and (ii) the town. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why this sample may not represent older adults in the town, and how this affects the findings on sleep. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 12 ÷ 240 × 100 = 5.0%. (ii) 1,800 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 15.0%.
    1 mark for 5.0%; 1 mark for 15.0%.
  2. (b) Older adults make up only 5.0% of the sample, against 15.0% of the town, so their views and sleep patterns are under-represented. The findings on sleep may therefore not apply to older residents.
    1 mark for under-representation of older adults with figures; 1 mark for the effect on the generalisability of the findings.
Q35Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a target population and a sampling frame (the list from which a sample is actually drawn), using an example. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The target population is everyone the researcher wants to generalise to, e.g. all adults with diabetes in Karachi. The sampling frame is the actual list available, e.g. patients registered at three diabetes clinics. The two may differ: people with undiagnosed diabetes or who do not attend these clinics are in the population but not on the list, so even a random sample from the list may be biased.
    1 mark: target population definition with example. 1 mark: sampling frame / list definition with example. 1 mark: explains how the difference may cause bias.
Q36Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to investigate the experiences of women who run small home businesses in rural Punjab. Discuss how the definition of the target population affects the choice of sampling technique and the generalisability of findings. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Definition: the population must be defined precisely – e.g. women aged 18+ living in villages in Punjab who earn money from a business run from home – so it is clear who is included. Because there is no list of such women, random sampling is impractical; snowball sampling through community contacts or an opportunity sample at village markets may be used. These techniques are likely to produce samples biased towards connected women in accessible villages, so findings generalise best to similar women. A broad definition (e.g. 'women entrepreneurs in Pakistan') would make generalisation from such a sample inappropriate, whereas a narrow definition makes the sample more representative but the findings apply to fewer people.
    1 mark: need to define population precisely (with example). 2 marks: link from definition/availability of a list to sampling technique choice. 2 marks: effect on representativeness/generalisability. 1 mark: trade-off between broad and narrow definitions.
Q37Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study of 60 commuters at a Lahore metro station found that 18 felt anxious in crowds. A newspaper reported: '30 % of people in Lahore suffer from crowd anxiety.' Calculate the percentage of the sample who felt anxious, and explain why the newspaper's claim is not justified. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 18 ÷ 60 × 100 = 30 %. The claim is not justified because the sample was 60 metro commuters at one station – not representative of all people in Lahore (e.g. people who avoid crowds may not use the metro at all, so anxiety may be underestimated, or the time of day may have affected results). The sample is also small, and 'felt anxious' is not the same as 'suffering' from an anxiety problem.
    B1: 30 %. 1 mark: sample not representative of the population of Lahore. 1 mark: specific reason (only metro users/one station/self-selection out of the metro). 1 mark: small sample or measurement issue (felt anxious ≠ disorder).
Q38Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a sampling frame that is incomplete can bias a sample, even if the sample is then drawn at random. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) If the sampling frame leaves out some members of the target population, those people have no chance of being selected, so the sample cannot represent them. For example, a list of registered mobile phone owners leaves out people without phones, who may be poorer or older, so the random sample is biased even though it was drawn at random.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark for explaining that excluded members have no chance of selection; 1 mark for linking this to a sample being biased; 1 mark for a clear example; 1 mark for explaining why the random draw does not correct the problem.
Q39Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the use of university students as samples in psychology research on the general population, in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: students are easy and cheap to recruit, and the sample is usually homogeneous, so the variables under study are less affected by individual differences; they are often willing to take part and can be tested in a controlled setting. Weaknesses: students are mostly young and well-educated, so findings may not apply to older adults or people in other cultures; their results may reflect the social environment of university rather than the general population.
    Level-based. 5–6 marks: two strengths and two weaknesses, each explained and linked to generalisation, with a judgement. 3–4 marks: one side developed or points less explained. 1–2 marks: description or unlinked points.
Q40Populations and samples · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A health survey was conducted using a sampling frame of telephone numbers in a district. Explain two ways in which this sampling frame could bias the findings on health. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) People without a telephone, often poorer or rural households, have no chance of being selected, so their health needs are not represented. People who own phones may also differ in age or income. These gaps mean the findings may overstate the health of the district as a whole.
    2 marks for each of two ways (excludes households without phones; over-represents some groups; rural or poorer groups under-represented; age or income imbalance), with 1 mark for explaining the effect on the findings.
Q41Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one advantage of using a large sample in a psychological study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one disadvantage of using a large sample. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual differences and unusual participants have less effect on the overall results, so the findings are more likely to be representative and generalisable to the target population.
    1 mark: reduces effect of individual differences/anomalies. 1 mark: more representative / generalisable / reliable.
  2. (b) It takes more time and money to recruit and test many participants, and it may be impractical, e.g. if each participant needs a one-hour interview.
    1 mark: time/cost. 1 mark: elaboration.
Q42Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why case studies often use a sample size of one, and one consequence of this for the research. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Case studies investigate a single person (or small group) in great depth, often because the condition is rare or unusual (e.g. a person with a specific brain injury), so it may only be possible to study one. Consequence: detailed qualitative data are obtained, but the findings may not be generalisable because that individual may be unique.
    1 mark: in-depth study of one person. 1 mark: rare/unusual cases. 1 mark: consequence – low generalisability (or rich detail).
Q43Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a larger sample usually gives more reliable results. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual differences and unusual participants have less effect on the overall results when there are more people, so the findings are more likely to show the true effect.
    1 mark for individual differences or unusual participants having less effect; 1 mark for the results being more reliable or closer to the true effect.
Q44Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a very large sample is not always better. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A very large sample takes more time, money and effort to recruit and test, and more people are exposed to any risks; a large study with poor procedures or a biased sample is still unreliable.
    1 mark for time, cost or practical burden (or extra exposure to risk); 1 mark for the point that a large sample cannot fix poor design or bias.
Q45Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a researcher might choose a small sample for a pilot study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A pilot study tests whether the procedures, materials and timings work, which needs only a few participants and saves time and money before the main study.
    1 mark for a pilot testing procedures, materials or timing; 1 mark for why a small sample is enough (cheap, quick, not for the final conclusions).
Q46Sample size · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Outline why a researcher studying a varied population, such as all pupils in a district, may need a larger sample than one studying a uniform group. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A varied population contains many different types of people, so a small sample may miss some types and the results may vary more between samples; a larger sample is more likely to include all the important sub-groups.
    1 mark for more variety needing more participants to capture it; 1 mark for a larger sample being more likely to include all sub-groups.
Q47Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study compared the mean reaction times of two groups, each containing only 5 participants. In Group A the reaction times (in milliseconds) were 312, 298, 305, 640 and 301. Calculate the mean reaction time for Group A, and explain how the small sample size affects the conclusions that can be drawn. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mean = 1856 ÷ 5 = 371.2 ms. One unusual score (640 ms) has a large effect on the mean of such a small group; without it the mean would be about 304 ms. With only 5 people, a single anomaly or individual difference can distort the comparison between groups, so the conclusion may not be reliable or generalisable.
    M1: sum 1856. A1: 371.2 ms. 1 mark: outlier 640 strongly affects a small sample. 1 mark: results less reliable/representative.
Q48Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A psychologist needs 60 participants who have a particular condition. In the population, 1 in 4 adults has the condition. The figures are hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of adults who must be screened to find 60 people with the condition, if 1 in 4 adults has it. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one practical problem of screening 240 adults to find a sample for a study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 60 ÷ 0.25 = 240 adults.
    M1 divide 60 by 1/4 (or multiply by 4); A1 240 adults.
  2. (b) Screening 240 adults takes a lot of time and money, which may exceed the research budget, and the staff needed for the screening may not be available.
    1 mark for a practical problem (time, cost, staff); 1 mark for linking it to the study's budget or timescale.
Q49Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Hypothetical reaction times (ms) for a group of six participants: 412, 398, 405, 421, 410 and 620.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean of all six reaction times. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean with the 620 ms score removed. Explain what this shows about small samples. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (412 + 398 + 405 + 421 + 410 + 620) ÷ 6 = 2666 ÷ 6 = 444.3 ms.
    M1 add the six scores (2666) and divide by 6; A1 444.3 ms.
  2. (b) (412 + 398 + 405 + 421 + 410) ÷ 5 = 2046 ÷ 5 = 409.2 ms. A single unusual score in a small sample has a large effect on the mean (it moves it by about 35 ms), so small samples are more affected by outliers.
    M1 remove 620 and divide the remaining total (2046) by 5; A1 409.2 ms; 1 mark for explaining the effect of an outlier on a small sample mean.
Q50Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'sampling error', and why it is larger in a smaller sample. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sampling error is the difference between the sample result and the true population value that arises because only some people were studied. In a small sample, a few unusual people have a bigger effect on the result, so the difference from the population is more likely to be large.
    1 mark for the definition (difference between sample and population due to chance); 1 mark for only some people being studied; 1 mark for smaller samples being more affected by unusual individuals.
Q51Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain two ways a researcher could decide on an appropriate sample size before starting a study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Use the sample sizes of similar published studies as a guide; consider the minimum number needed to show the effect of interest (for example, how many participants are needed in each group for a reliable comparison); take account of the budget, time and access to participants.
    1 mark for each of two sensible methods (previous studies, minimum needed for the analysis, budget or time constraints); 1 mark for explaining how each method helps.
Q52Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a sample of 150 can still be unrepresentative. Give one example of how the sample size would not solve the problem. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A large sample drawn in a biased way, for example only from one school or only volunteers, can still be unrepresentative because more people from the same biased group are included, so the bias remains.
    1 mark for the sample being drawn from a biased group; 1 mark for explaining that size does not remove bias; 1 mark for a clear example (volunteers only, one location).
Q53Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

A school has 1,200 pupils, and 40 of them have a disability. A researcher wants to survey a 10% random sample of all pupils. The figures are hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the sample size for a 10% sample of the school, and state how many pupils with a disability in the sample could be expected if the school has 40 such pupils. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 10% of 1,200 pupils = 120 pupils in the sample. If 40 of the 1,200 pupils (1 in 30) have a disability, the sample would contain 40 ÷ 1,200 × 120 = 4 such pupils. Four pupils is too few for any reliable conclusion about this group.
    1 mark for 120; 1 mark for 4 pupils with a disability; 1 mark for stating that this is too few to draw reliable conclusions about the subgroup.
Q54Sample size · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain how increasing each group from 10 to 40 participants would change the reliability of a comparison between the means of two groups. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) With 40 per group, the mean of each group is less affected by individual differences or unusual scores, so the difference between the means is more likely to reflect a real difference. The standard error of each mean falls as sample size rises, so the comparison is more reliable.
    1 mark for individual differences having less effect on each mean; 1 mark for the difference being more likely to reflect a real effect; 1 mark for a reference to the estimate becoming more precise or reliable.
Q55Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher plans a questionnaire study about smartphone use among 9000 secondary-school pupils in a district. She can afford to survey either 30 pupils chosen at random or 300 pupils from the two schools nearest her office. Evaluate both options and recommend which she should choose. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 30 random pupils: every pupil has an equal chance, so there is no systematic selection bias, but the sample is small, so chance variation and individual differences may make it unrepresentative. 300 pupils from two nearby schools: the larger sample reduces the effect of individual differences, but it is biased – pupils from two schools in one area may share similar backgrounds, school phone rules and internet access. Recommendation: e.g. the random sample, because bias cannot be removed by increasing size, whereas the random sample's error is due to chance only; or a justified compromise (random selection of schools, then pupils). Credit any justified decision.
    1 mark: evaluation of small random sample. 1 mark: evaluation of large biased sample. 1 mark: recommendation. 1 mark: justification (size does not cure bias).
Q56Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher has a budget for 40 questionnaires. She could survey 40 pupils from one school, or 20 pupils from each of two schools in different parts of a city. Explain which option is preferable for studying pupils' attitudes across the city, referring to sample size and representativeness. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Two schools are preferable: 20 pupils from each of two schools gives a sample from more than one area and school type, so it is more representative of the city. The total sample size is the same, so one school does not give more reliable results. However, two schools with only 20 each may still be too few to represent a whole city, so the findings should be treated cautiously.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark for choosing the two-school option; 1 mark for the same total sample size; 1 mark for representativeness across areas; 1 mark for a caution about the small number per school.
Q57Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Hypothetical study: 20% of people in a population are left-handed.

  1. (a) In a population, 20% of people are left-handed. Sample A has 15 people, of whom 5 are left-handed. Sample B has 150 people, of whom 27 are left-handed. Calculate the percentage left-handed in each sample to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the result from Sample A is less accurate than the result from Sample B. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sample A: 5 ÷ 15 × 100 = 33.3%. Sample B: 27 ÷ 150 × 100 = 18.0%.
    1 mark for 33.3%; 1 mark for 18.0%.
  2. (b) In a small sample, one or two individuals change the percentage a lot (here, one person changes it by 6.7 percentage points), so Sample A is more affected by chance; Sample B is larger, so its percentage is closer to the true 20%.
    1 mark for the small-sample effect (a single person has a large effect); 1 mark for Sample B being closer to the population value of 20%.
Q58Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Discuss the trade-off between sample size and the time, cost and ethics of a study, using one example. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) A larger sample gives more reliable and generalisable results, but needs more time and money to recruit and test, and more participants are exposed to any risks, which raises ethical issues. A smaller sample is cheaper and quicker and imposes less burden, but its results are less reliable and less generalisable. For example, a study of a rare condition may have to accept a small sample, and should explain the limits this places on the conclusions.
    Level-based. 5–6 marks: both sides of the trade-off discussed with an example, reaching a justified judgement. 3–4 marks: one side developed or example missing. 1–2 marks: descriptive.
Q59Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a researcher who has only 8 participants might choose to report the median rather than the mean for a set of scores that includes one very high score. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The mean is pulled towards the very high score, so it is not typical of most participants when the sample is small. The median is the middle score when the scores are ordered, so it is not affected by one extreme value and gives a better description of the typical participant.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark for the mean being affected by the extreme score; 1 mark for the median being the middle value; 1 mark for the median being unaffected by outliers; 1 mark for the median being a better typical value in a small sample.
Q60Sample size · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain whether a sample of 2,000 people who volunteered online is always better than a random sample of 200 people from the same population. Refer to sample size and sampling method in your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always. A larger sample reduces sampling error, but if the 2,000 are volunteers, they may differ systematically from the population, so the bias does not shrink with size. The random sample of 200 has no such bias, and its results may be more trustworthy for the population, although its sampling error is larger.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark for the larger sample reducing random error; 1 mark for volunteer bias not reducing with size; 1 mark for the random sample being free of systematic bias; 1 mark for the trade-off with larger sampling error.
