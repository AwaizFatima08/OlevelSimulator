Q1Self-reports · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A researcher plans a questionnaire to investigate mobile phone use among college students in Lahore.

  1. (a) Write one closed question the researcher could include in the questionnaire. [1]
  2. (b) Write one open question the researcher could include in the questionnaire. [1]
  3. (c) Explain one weakness of using closed questions in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 'Do you use your phone during meals with your family? Yes / No'
    1 mark: a question with fixed response options relevant to phone use.
  2. (b) 'Describe how using your phone affects the time you spend with your family.'
    1 mark: a question that allows participants to answer in their own words; must not be answerable with yes/no.
  3. (c) Students must choose from fixed answers that may not fit their real experience (e.g. they use the phone only sometimes), so the data may lack validity and detail.
    1 mark: forced choice / lack of detail; 1 mark: linked to phone use.
Q2Self-reports · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Write a rating-scale question that could be used to measure how stressed teachers feel at work. Your scale must have labelled anchor points. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one strength of using a rating scale rather than an open question. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 'How stressed do you feel at work most days?' 1 = not at all stressed, 2, 3, 4, 5 = extremely stressed.
    1 mark: question about stress with a numerical scale; 1 mark: both ends (anchor points) labelled.
  2. (b) It gives numerical (quantitative) data, so the mean stress of teachers in different schools can be compared easily and objectively.
    1 mark: quantitative / easy comparison; 1 mark: linked to teachers.
Q3Self-reports · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

Dr Malik asks recently widowed women in Multan to talk about their experiences. She has no list of questions and simply follows what each woman wants to talk about.

  1. (a) Identify which type of interview Dr Malik is using. [1]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why this type of interview is suitable for this topic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) An unstructured interview.
    1 mark: unstructured (no fixed questions, follows the conversation).
  2. (b) Grief is a sensitive and individual experience; an unstructured interview allows each widow to talk freely about what matters to her, building rapport and giving detailed, valid data.
    1 mark: flexibility / rapport / depth; 1 mark: linked to the sensitive topic.
Q4Self-reports · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Look at this question: "Do you usually sleep for at least seven hours a night? Yes / No". Identify whether it is an open or a closed question, and explain one advantage of using this type of question. [3]
  2. (b) Define the term social desirability bias as it applies to self-reports. [2]
  3. (c) Outline one way a researcher could reduce social desirability bias in a questionnaire. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Closed question: it offers a fixed choice (yes or no). Advantage: quick to answer and easy to analyse (for example, count the "yes" answers), so the data are quantitative.
    1 mark: closed. 2 marks: one advantage developed (quick to complete, easy to code and compare, reduces variation in how answers are written).
  2. (b) The tendency for participants to answer in a way that makes them look good or fits what they think is socially acceptable, rather than giving their true answer.
    2 marks: participants answer to look good / in a socially acceptable way, not truthfully. 1 mark for a vague idea without the link to the answer given.
  3. (c) Guarantee anonymity or confidentiality so that participants feel less pressure to look good. Other valid options: neutral wording, asking about "most people" rather than the participant, or indirect questions.
    2 marks: one method clearly outlined with the reason it reduces bias. Accept any sensible method.
Q5Self-reports · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A questionnaire on sleep asks: "How satisfied are you with your sleep?" Participants circle one number: 1 = very dissatisfied, 2 = dissatisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = satisfied, 5 = very satisfied.

  1. (a) Identify the type of scale used in this question. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why a researcher might include a neutral midpoint (3) on this five-point scale. [2]
  3. (c) Outline one limitation of this type of scale. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A rating scale (a numerical scale with five points), which is a closed-question format.
    1 mark: rating (Likert-style) scale; accept "closed question with a numbered scale".
  2. (b) A neutral midpoint lets participants who are undecided give a true answer, rather than being forced to choose a side, which could distort the data.
    2 marks: a neutral option lets undecided participants answer honestly; avoids forced choice distorting results.
  3. (c) The distance between points may not be equal for every respondent, and some people cluster answers near the middle or avoid the extremes (response style bias).
    2 marks: one limitation (unequal intervals, response style bias such as central tendency or extreme responding) outlined.
Q6Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe structured interviews and unstructured interviews, using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) A structured interview uses a fixed list of questions asked in the same order to every participant, e.g. asking every patient the same ten questions about symptoms; it is easy to compare and replicate but the interviewer cannot follow up interesting answers. An unstructured interview has no fixed questions beyond a topic: the interviewer follows the conversation, e.g. a therapist asking a client to talk about childhood; it gives rich, detailed data but answers cannot easily be compared, and interviewer bias is more likely.
    Up to 3 marks for each type: 1 for a description, 1 for an example, 1 for an elaboration (strength/weakness or feature such as standardisation, replicability, rapport, depth).
Q7Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A researcher interviews parents in Karachi face to face about how they discipline their children.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'social desirability' in self-report research. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why social desirability is a particular problem in this study. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one way to reduce this problem. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) When participants give answers that make them look good or match social norms, rather than the truth.
    1 mark: answers to appear favourable/acceptable; 1 mark: rather than truthful, so data are invalid.
  2. (b) Asking parents face to face how often they shout at or smack their children is sensitive; parents may underreport harsh discipline to avoid being judged by the interviewer.
    1 mark: identifies the sensitive behaviour; 1 mark: direction of bias (under-reporting) linked to face-to-face interview.
  3. (c) Use an anonymous postal or online questionnaire instead of a face-to-face interview, so parents feel their answers cannot be traced back to them.
    1 mark: anonymity/confidentiality/online questionnaire/filler questions; 1 mark: explanation of why it reduces the bias.
Q8Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical study interviews patients at a diabetes clinic in Islamabad about why they do or do not follow their doctor's advice.

  1. (a) Explain one strength of using a semi-structured interview in this study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of using a semi-structured interview in this study. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one ethical issue the researcher must consider and how to deal with it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The interviewer can ask set questions to all patients, giving some comparable data, but also follow up interesting answers (e.g. why a patient stopped taking medication), giving more detail.
    1 mark: combination of fixed questions and flexibility; 1 mark: linked to patients.
  2. (b) Different follow-up questions are asked to different patients, so answers are harder to compare, and the interviewer's own views may influence which points are explored (interviewer bias).
    1 mark: less standardised / interviewer bias / time-consuming; 1 mark: linked to the study.
  3. (c) Confidentiality: patients may disclose personal health information, so their names must not be recorded and data stored securely.
    1 mark: issue (confidentiality, privacy, right to withdraw, protection from distress); 1 mark: how to deal with it.
Q9Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Hamza is writing a questionnaire about public transport in Karachi. Three of his questions are:1 Don't you agree that the new bus service is excellent?2 Are the buses cheap and clean? Yes / No3 Do you often use public transport? Yes / No

  1. (a) Identify two problems with the questions written by Hamza. [2]
  2. (b) Rewrite question 3 so that it produces clearer data. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Question 1 is a leading question ('Don't you agree…'), pushing respondents towards 'yes'. Question 2 is a double-barrelled question asking about two things at once (price and cleanliness).
    1 mark each: leading question (Q1); double-barrelled (Q2). Accept: Q3 uses ambiguous terms ('often').
  2. (b) 'How many times did you use public transport in the last 7 days? 0 / 1–3 / 4–6 / 7 or more'
    1 mark: removes the vague word 'often'; 1 mark: gives specific, mutually exclusive response options.
Q10Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A researcher in Lahore asks 8 students to rate how much they agree with the statement "I exercise regularly" on a five-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The scores are: 4, 5, 3, 4, 2, 5, 4, 3.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean score. Show your working. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the data from a five-point Likert scale are usually treated as ordinal rather than interval data. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why the mean may give a misleading picture of these students' exercise habits. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 30 ÷ 8 = 3.75
    1 mark for the total of 30. 1 mark for 30 ÷ 8 = 3.75.
  2. (b) The scale puts responses in order (strongly disagree to strongly agree), but the distance between points, for example between "agree" and "strongly agree", may not be equal for every respondent, so only the order is secure.
    1 mark: ordinal means ranked or ordered. 1 mark: the gaps between points may not be equal. 1 mark: linked to the Likert scale in this study.
  3. (c) The sample is small (only 8 students), so the mean may not represent students in general. Also, the mean hides the spread of answers, which runs from 2 to 5.
    2 marks: any one valid reason linked to the data, such as the small sample, or that the average hides the range of scores. "The mean is wrong" alone earns no marks.
Q11Self-reports · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the difference between a structured interview and an unstructured interview. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one way that interviewer bias could affect the responses in a structured interview. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Structured: the interviewer asks a fixed set of pre-set questions in the same order, and answers are often closed, which makes them easy to compare. Unstructured: the interview is flexible, follow-up questions are asked depending on the conversation, and answers are open, giving richer detail.
    Up to 2 marks for each type: structured = fixed questions, same order, quantitative or comparable; unstructured = flexible, follow-up questions, open-ended, rich qualitative data. Accept any clear contrast.
  2. (b) The interviewer's tone, facial expression or slight changes in wording may encourage some answers over others, so the responses reflect the interviewer as well as the participant, reducing validity.
    2 marks: a specific interviewer behaviour (tone, expression, rewording) shapes participants' answers; linked to lower validity or accuracy.
Q12Self-reports · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A researcher in Jeddah has written a questionnaire to measure attitudes to healthy eating.

  1. (a) Explain how the researcher could test the reliability of her questionnaire using the test–retest method. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why a reliable questionnaire may still not be valid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give the same questionnaire to the same participants twice, with a gap of a few weeks (long enough that they don't remember their answers but not so long that their real attitudes change). Correlate the two sets of scores; a strong positive correlation (e.g. above +0.8) shows the questionnaire is reliable.
    1 mark: same participants, same questionnaire, twice; 1 mark: suitable time gap with justification; 1 mark: compare/correlate scores – a strong positive correlation indicates reliability.
  2. (b) It may consistently measure something other than intended – e.g. items about 'healthy eating' may really measure knowledge of nutrition rather than what people actually eat, so answers are consistent but not accurate.
    1 mark: consistency ≠ accuracy; 1 mark: example in context (e.g. social desirability gives the same false answers each time).
Q13Self-reports · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks

A psychologist wants to investigate the relationship between sleep habits and concentration in university students.

  1. (a) Describe how this study could be conducted as a self-report using a questionnaire. Include details of the sampling technique, the types of questions with examples, and how the data would be analysed. [10]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of your plan and suggest how it could be improved. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A possible plan: opportunity or stratified sample of 100 university students (e.g. equal numbers of first- and final-year students) from different faculties of a university in Lahore; online anonymous questionnaire with standardised instructions and consent page; closed questions (e.g. 'How many hours a night do you usually sleep on weekdays? less than 5 / 5–6 / 7–8 / more than 8'), rating scales (e.g. 'Rate your ability to concentrate in lectures: 1 = very poor to 7 = excellent'), and open questions (e.g. 'Describe what stops you sleeping before exams'); filler questions to reduce demand characteristics; pilot study to check clarity; quantitative data analysed as means/medians and bar charts by year group; qualitative answers coded into themes; ethics – informed consent, right to withdraw, confidentiality, debrief with sleep advice.
    Mark points: sampling technique and sample detail; recruitment; questionnaire format (online/paper, anonymous); at least two question types with examples; rating scale with anchors; filler questions/pilot; standardised instructions; analysis (descriptive statistics, graphs, thematic coding); ethics. Level 4 (9–10): a detailed, well-reasoned, coherent plan covering all the required features, with clear operationalisation and justified choices. Level 3 (6–8): a clear plan with most required features and some detail; minor omissions. Level 2 (3–5): a basic plan; several features missing or vague. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or largely irrelevant. 0: no creditworthy response.
  2. (b) Weakness: self-reported sleep hours may be inaccurate because students guess or overestimate, reducing validity. Improvement: ask students to keep a sleep diary for a week, or wear an activity tracker that records sleep, and compare the measures (triangulation).
    1 mark: identify a weakness of the plan; 1 mark: explain it in context; 1 mark: suggest an improvement; 1 mark: explain how the improvement helps.
Q14Self-reports · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical telecommunications company with 2,000 employees in branches across Pakistan wants to find out how employees feel about their managers.

  1. (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of collecting data with questionnaires rather than interviews in this study. [4]
  2. (b) The response rate was 18 %. Calculate how many questionnaires were returned. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: questionnaires can be sent to all 2,000 employees across the country quickly and cheaply, and anonymity may make employees more honest about their managers. Weakness: there is no chance to clarify unclear questions or follow up answers, and response rates may be low, so those who reply (e.g. very unhappy staff) may not be representative.
    2 marks for strength (identify + link to the large company / anonymity); 2 marks for weakness (identify + link).
  2. (b) 360
    0.18 × 2000 = 360.
Q15Self-reports · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A researcher gives a happiness questionnaire (scores out of 30) to six adults in Karachi, then gives the same questionnaire again two weeks later. Time 1 scores: 22, 30, 18, 25, 27, 15. Time 2 scores: 24, 29, 20, 26, 25, 17. The pairs are (Time 1, Time 2): (22,24), (30,29), (18,20), (25,26), (27,25), (15,17).

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by test-retest reliability. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) for these scores, using the formula for Pearson's r. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why a high test-retest correlation does not, on its own, show that the questionnaire is valid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The consistency of a measure over time: the same people complete the same test on two occasions, and their scores should be similar.
    2 marks: consistency over time; same participants and same test on two occasions with similar scores. 1 mark for "it gives the same result".
  2. (b) r = 0.97
    Method: find the means (Time 1 = 22.83, Time 2 = 23.5), then sum of products of deviations divided by the square root of the product of the sums of squared deviations. 1 mark for correct method; 1 mark for correct sums; 1 mark for r = 0.97 (accept ±0.01).
  3. (c) A questionnaire can be consistent (reliable) and still measure the wrong thing, for example social desirability or a general mood rather than happiness. Reliability does not prove validity.
    2 marks: reliable does not mean valid, with an explanation (for example, a consistent measure of the wrong construct).

  3. (c) Explain one weakness of the case study method for studying this patient. [2]

Q17Case studies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Q20Case studies · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

Psychologists are writing a case study of Ayesha, a 17-year-old from Multan with a rare memory condition. They collect data from interviews with Ayesha and her parents, a memory test completed at two time points, and a diary kept by Ayesha.

  1. (a) Identify one type of data collected in this case study. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest one way the researcher could improve the reliability of the case study's findings. [2]

Q21Case studies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A school psychologist in Islamabad carries out a case study of a 12-year-old boy who refuses to attend school.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'triangulation' in a case study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest three different sources of data the researcher could use to triangulate in this case study. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why triangulation increases the validity of the case study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Using several different methods or sources of data (e.g. interviews, observations, tests, records) to study the same case, and checking whether they agree.
    1 mark: several methods/sources; 1 mark: comparing them to check consistency/validity.
  2. (b) An interview with the boy about how he feels in class; an observation of his behaviour in lessons; school records of attendance and grades (or a questionnaire for his parents/teachers, or a psychometric test).
    1 mark each for three different, appropriate sources linked to the boy.
  3. (c) If different sources give the same picture, the conclusion does not rely on one possibly biased source.
    1 mark: agreement between sources makes conclusions more accurate/less dependent on one method's bias.
Q22Case studies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

    1 mark: issue (confidentiality/privacy/right to withdraw); 1 mark: why it is especially important in a case study.

  1. (a) Each person's brain and life experiences differ, so one person may not be typical of all people. The findings may be unique to this individual and their circumstances (low generalisability), so a general conclusion about humans could be wrong.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 for a named limitation (generalisability); 1 for explaining individual differences; 1 for linking to memory research; 1 for developing the point with an example.

  1. (a) Describe two methods a researcher could use to collect data in a case study of a person who has lost the use of a limb after an accident. [4]

  1. (a) For example: a structured interview or questionnaire about daily activities (self-report); observation of the person completing everyday tasks; a psychometric test of mood or memory; a diary kept by the participant; brain imaging to see the extent of damage.

Q25Case studies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why data from a retrospective case study may be less accurate than data collected at the time of events. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Memories are reconstructed over time, so details can be forgotten or distorted. The person may also present events in a favourable light (social desirability), so the account can be biased.
    Up to 2 marks for the memory point (forgetting, reconstruction, distortion). Up to 2 marks for the bias point (self-presentation, social desirability). Each point must be developed.

  2. (b) Suggest one way to make the case study more objective. [2]

Q27Case studies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks

A psychologist wants to carry out a case study of a child who is gifted at mathematics.

  1. (a) Describe how this case study could be conducted. Include at least two methods of data collection, how long it would last, and one ethical consideration. [10]
  2. (b) Explain one strength of your plan. [4]

  1. (a) Advantage: each participant acts as their own control, so individual differences are removed and fewer participants are needed. Disadvantage: order effects, such as practice or fatigue, may affect the second performance, although counterbalancing can reduce this.

Q68Experiments · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Four participants each complete two conditions in a repeated measures design: condition A (a quiet room) and condition B (a noisy room), both with the same reading test.

  1. (a) Explain how counterbalancing would be used for these four participants, and why it is used. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Two participants (for example 1 and 2) complete condition A (quiet room) and then condition B (noisy room). The other two (3 and 4) complete B and then A. Each condition is done first equally often, so any practice or fatigue effect is spread evenly across both conditions and cannot be the sole cause of the difference.

Show answer
  1. (a) Pair participants on a relevant variable, such as baseline reaction time measured in a first session or age. Q87Physiological measures · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how fMRI measures brain activity, and give one limitation of using fMRI to study emotion. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) fMRI detects changes in blood oxygenation (the BOLD signal). Brain regions that are more active use more oxygen, so blood flow to them increases and the magnetic signal changes. Limitation: fMRI is correlational, so an active region does not prove that it causes an emotion. Also, the BOLD response is slow, so fMRI cannot track rapid emotional changes in the way that EEG can.
    2 marks: explanation of the BOLD signal (blood oxygenation linked to activity). 2 marks: one limitation developed (correlational, poor temporal resolution or expensive equipment limiting use).
Q88Physiological measures · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks

  2. (b) Evaluate the use of EEG in this study, referring to one strength and one weakness. [4]
Show answer
