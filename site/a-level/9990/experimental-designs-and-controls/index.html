Q1Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Outline what is meant by an independent measures design. [1]
  2. (b) A hypothetical experiment in Quetta compares memory for pictures and memory for words using an independent measures design. Explain one weakness of this design for this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Different participants take part in each condition of the experiment.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Participant variables such as natural memory ability may differ between the picture group and the word group, so differences in recall may be due to the people rather than the type of material.
    1 mark weakness; 1 mark linked to context.
Q2Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by an 'order effect' in a repeated measures design. [2]
  2. (b) Describe how counterbalancing could be used in a repeated measures experiment on whether people type faster on a laptop keyboard or a phone screen. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A change in performance caused by the order in which the conditions are done rather than by the IV, e.g. doing better in the second condition through practice, or worse through fatigue or boredom.
    1 mark definition; 1 mark example (practice / fatigue / boredom).
  2. (b) Split participants into two halves: one half types on the laptop first and then the phone; the other half uses the phone first and then the laptop, so order effects are balanced across the conditions.
    1 mark: two groups in opposite orders; 1 mark: in context.
Q3Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a researcher could use a matched pairs design in a hypothetical experiment comparing two diets for weight loss among adults in Lagos. [3]
  2. (b) Give one disadvantage of using a matched pairs design in this study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Measure each volunteer's starting weight (and perhaps age and sex); pair people with similar starting weight; randomly allocate one member of each pair to diet A and the other to diet B.
    1 mark: pre-measure a relevant variable; 1 mark: form pairs; 1 mark: one of each pair to each condition (randomly).
  2. (b) It is time-consuming to measure and match people, and if one member of a pair drops out the other's data may be lost (or matching cannot be perfect on all variables).
    1 mark.
Q4Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

A hypothetical experiment with 48 participants in Hong Kong tests whether people compare shop prices faster when the prices are shown as a vertical bar chart or as a horizontal bar chart. Each participant does both conditions; half do vertical first and half do horizontal first.

  1. (a) How many participants should do the order 'vertical chart first, then horizontal chart'? [1]
  2. (b) Name the technique the researcher is using and state its purpose. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 24
    48 ÷ 2 = 24.
  2. (b) Counterbalancing (ABBA / two orders); to balance out order effects such as practice and fatigue between the two conditions.
    1 mark name; 1 mark purpose.
Q5Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study compares the spatial ability of left-handed and right-handed people. Explain why it must use an independent measures design. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Handedness is a characteristic of the participant that cannot be changed, so each person can only be in one condition (left-handed or right-handed).
    1 mark: IV is a participant characteristic / not manipulated; 1 mark: so cannot be in both conditions.
Q6Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a researcher could randomly allocate 30 participants to two conditions. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why random allocation is used. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give each participant a number from 1 to 30, put the numbers in a hat and draw them out; the first 15 drawn go to condition A and the remaining 15 to condition B (or use a random number generator).
    1 mark random method; 1 mark equal groups / how it is used.
  2. (b) So that each participant has an equal chance of being in either condition, spreading participant variables evenly and avoiding researcher bias in choosing groups.
    1 mark.

Q8Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical experiment compares how fast bilingual students in Karachi read a passage in Urdu and an equivalent passage in English. Each student reads both passages. Explain one strength of this design for this study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of this design for this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each student is compared with themselves, so differences in general reading ability or eyesight between students cannot explain the difference between the two languages.
    1 mark: removes participant variables; 1 mark: applied (reading ability, speed, eyesight).
  2. (b) Order effects: reading the first passage may give practice (or tiredness) that changes speed on the second, so the difference may be due to order rather than language.
    1 mark: order/practice/fatigue effect; 1 mark: applied.
Q9Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'participant variables' in an independent measures design. [2]
  2. (b) State how a researcher can reduce the effect of participant variables in an independent measures design. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Differences between the people in each group, such as age, ability or motivation, which might affect the DV and make the groups differ for reasons other than the IV.
    1 mark: individual differences between participants in different groups; 1 mark: may affect the DV/confound.
  2. (b) Randomly allocate participants to the conditions.
    1 mark.
Q10Experimental designs · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical matched pairs experiment compares two revision apps for O Level chemistry students in Faisalabad. Suggest two variables the students should be matched on and give a reason for each. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Previous chemistry test score, because students who already know more will score higher whichever app they use. 2. Hours of tuition per week, because extra teaching would also improve scores. (Also accept: age, school, English reading level, access to a phone.)
    For each: 1 mark relevant variable; 1 mark reason it could affect the DV.
Q11Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe repeated measures design and matched pairs design, using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Repeated measures: the same participants do every condition, e.g. each person takes a memory test in silence and with music. It removes participant variables between conditions and needs fewer participants, but suffers from order effects (practice, fatigue) and demand characteristics, controlled by counterbalancing. Matched pairs: different participants in each condition but each participant is paired with another who is similar on a key variable (e.g. IQ or age), one of each pair going into each condition. It reduces participant variables without order effects, but matching is time-consuming and never perfect.
    Up to 3 marks each: description (1), example (1), strength/weakness or control (1).
Q12Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study in Islamabad investigates whether students learn new Urdu poetry better when they study it at 8 am or at 8 pm. Explain which experimental design you would choose and why. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest how the researcher could use a repeated measures design instead. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Matched pairs (or independent measures): the same poem cannot be learned twice by the same person, so repeated measures would cause practice effects; matching on a pre-test of memory ability reduces participant variables between the 8 am and 8 pm groups.
    1 mark design; 1 mark reason against repeated measures (practice/learning the same material); 1 mark reason for chosen design.
  2. (b) Use two different but equally difficult poems (one for each time) and counterbalance which poem is learned at which time.
    1 mark.
Q13Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment tests whether visitors find their way through a large hospital in Kuala Lumpur faster using a printed map or a phone map. The measure is the time taken to walk from the entrance to the eye clinic.

  1. (a) Explain why the researcher should not use a repeated measures design. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest which design should be used and give one strength of it for this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) After the first condition participants would already know the route, so their time in the second condition would be quicker because of learning (a practice effect), not because of the type of map.
    1 mark practice/learning effect; 1 mark linked to route.
  2. (b) Independent measures: each participant uses only one map, so there are no order effects and they cannot work out the aim by comparing conditions.
    1 mark design; 1 mark strength.
Q14Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

In a hypothetical driving-simulator study in Sydney, each participant drives for one hour while talking on a hands-free phone and then for one hour while using a hand-held phone. Reaction time to hazards is measured.

  1. (a) Explain how a fatigue effect could affect the results of this study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two ways of reducing this problem. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) In the second hour participants will be tired and bored, so their reaction times will be slower whatever the condition; since the hand-held phone condition always comes second, it will appear worse than it really is.
    1 mark tiredness slows reactions; 1 mark confounds the second condition.
  2. (b) Counterbalance the order (half do hands-free first); give a rest break between conditions / shorten each session.
    1 mark each.
Q15Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical experiment in an office training centre in Lahore compares a new keyboard layout with a standard layout. Twelve learners took a typing pre-test and were formed into 6 matched pairs; one member of each pair was randomly allocated to each layout. After two weeks of practice, their typing speeds (words per minute) were:Pair 1: new 42, standard 38Pair 2: new 35, standard 36Pair 3: new 50, standard 44Pair 4: new 28, standard 25Pair 5: new 46, standard 41Pair 6: new 31, standard 30

  1. (a) Calculate the mean typing speed for each layout. Give your answers to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) State in how many of the pairs the learner using the new layout was faster. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why a matched pairs design was used here rather than a repeated measures design. [2]
  4. (d) Explain one problem that would arise if the learner using the new layout in pair 4 left the study before the final test. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) New layout: 38.7 words per minute; standard layout: 35.7 words per minute.
    New: (42+35+50+28+46+31) ÷ 6 = 232 ÷ 6 = 38.7 (1). Standard: (38+36+44+25+41+30) ÷ 6 = 214 ÷ 6 = 35.7 (1).
  2. (b) 5 of the 6 pairs (all except pair 2).
    Pairs 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 = 5.
  3. (c) With repeated measures each learner would practise on both layouts, so skill learned on the first layout would carry over to the second (an order/practice effect, and the layouts could interfere with each other); matched pairs avoids this while still controlling typing ability, because the pairs were matched on the pre-test.
    1 mark: order/practice or interference effect with repeated measures, in context; 1 mark: matching on the pre-test still controls the main participant variable.
  4. (d) The other member of pair 4 has no partner to be compared with, so their data are also lost (or must be excluded), reducing the sample to 5 pairs.
    1 mark: partner's data cannot be used / sample reduced.
Q16Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a hypothetical independent measures experiment in Doha, 60 adults were randomly allocated to an 8-week group exercise programme or an individual exercise programme (30 in each). Six people dropped out of the group programme and one dropped out of the individual programme.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of participants who dropped out of each condition. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of all participants who completed the study. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [1]
  3. (c) Explain how the uneven drop-out could affect the validity of comparing the two groups. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Group exercise: 20%; individual exercise: 3.3% (to 1 d.p.)
    6 ÷ 30 × 100 = 20% (1); 1 ÷ 30 × 100 = 3.3% (1).
  2. (b) 88.3%
    (60 − 7) ÷ 60 × 100 = 88.3%.
  3. (c) The people who stayed in the group-exercise condition may be the most motivated, so the groups are no longer equivalent; any difference in fitness may be due to motivation (a participant variable) rather than the type of exercise.
    1 mark groups no longer equal; 1 mark participant variable confounds result.
Q17Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical experiment, each participant does the conditions in the order A, B, B, A (for example, typing with a keyboard, then a touch screen, then the touch screen again, then the keyboard). Explain how this controls order effects. [3]
  2. (b) Give one weakness of the ABBA method. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each condition appears once early and once late in the sequence, so any practice or tiredness effect is spread equally across both conditions. The mean of each participant's two A trials can then be compared with the mean of the two B trials.
    1 mark: each condition in early and late positions; 1 mark: practice/fatigue affects both equally; 1 mark: compare averaged scores.
  2. (b) It takes longer and each participant does more trials, so boredom or fatigue increases; participants may also guess the aim.
    1 mark.
Q18Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study in Islamabad tests whether drinking strong coffee in the evening reduces the number of hours people sleep. Explain why a repeated measures design might cause problems, and how the researcher could still use it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Effects of caffeine may carry over: if the coffee night comes first, the person may sleep badly and then sleep more the following night, which would affect the no-coffee condition. The researcher could leave a gap of several days between the two nights (washout) and counterbalance the order, so that half the participants have coffee first and half no-coffee first.
    1 mark: carry-over/order effect identified; 1 mark: explained; 1 mark: time gap between conditions; 1 mark: counterbalancing.
Q19Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a hypothetical matched pairs experiment in Gujranwala, pairs of students with the same English grade learned vocabulary by a new game-based method or the old list method, and were tested (score out of 25).Pair | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6New method | 15 | 18 | 12 | 20 | 16 | 14Old method | 13 | 17 | 12 | 16 | 15 | 10

  1. (a) Calculate the difference in score for each pair (new method minus old method) and the mean difference. [2]
  2. (b) State the number of pairs in which the new method gave the higher score. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why it is useful to compare the two members of each pair, rather than just the overall means. [2]

  1. (a) Differences: 2, 1, 0, 4, 1, 4; mean difference 2

  2. (b) 5

Q20Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher needs 20 scores in each of two conditions. State how many participants are needed with (i) a repeated measures design and (ii) an independent measures design. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a researcher might still choose the independent measures design even though it needs more participants. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 20 (ii) 40
    (i) each participant gives a score in both conditions → 20 (B1); (ii) different people in each condition → 2 × 20 = 40 (B1).
  2. (b) There are no order effects, and participants see only one condition so they are less likely to guess the aim (fewer demand characteristics); the same materials can also be used in both conditions.
    1 mark: reason (no order effects / fewer demand characteristics / same materials / no carry-over); 1 mark: explained.
Q21Experimental designs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a hypothetical repeated measures experiment in Bahawalpur, 20 participants solved puzzles in condition A (quiet) and condition B (with a ticking clock). Ten did A then B; ten did B then A. Mean scores:Group | First test | Second testGroup 1 (A then B) | A = 12 | B = 16Group 2 (B then A) | B = 13 | A = 15

  1. (a) Calculate the overall mean score for condition A and for condition B. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean score for the first test and for the second test that participants did, whatever the condition. [2]
  3. (c) Using your answers, explain what the data show about order effects and about the IV. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A: 13.5; B: 14.5
    A: (12 + 15) ÷ 2 = 13.5 (B1); B: (16 + 13) ÷ 2 = 14.5 (B1).
  2. (b) First test: 12.5; second test: 15.5
    First: (12 + 13) ÷ 2 = 12.5; second: (16 + 15) ÷ 2 = 15.5 (B1 each).
  3. (c) Scores were 3 points higher on the second test whatever the condition, showing a strong practice effect. The difference between conditions was only 1 point, so the IV had little effect; counterbalancing stopped the practice effect from making one condition look better.
    1 mark: practice effect of 3; 1 mark: small condition effect / counterbalancing balanced it out.
Q22Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical repeated measures experiment with 36 students in Riyadh tests concentration in three conditions: silence, instrumental music, and speech playing in the background.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of different orders in which the three conditions could be presented. [1]
  2. (b) If each order is used equally, how many participants complete each order? [1]
  3. (c) How many participants do the 'speech' condition first? [1]
  4. (d) Explain why full counterbalancing of all orders is better than just using a single order for everyone. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6
    3 × 2 × 1 = 6.
  2. (b) 6
    36 ÷ 6 = 6.
  3. (c) 12
    2 of the 6 orders start with speech: 2 × 6 = 12 (or 36 ÷ 3).
  4. (d) With one order, the last condition would always benefit from practice or suffer from fatigue, confounding the results; using every order equally means each condition appears in each position equally often, so order effects cancel out.
    1 mark problem with single order; 1 mark how full counterbalancing solves it.
Q23Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment in schools in Karachi tests whether children aged 8–10 choose a healthy snack more often after watching a cartoon advert for fruit than after watching a cartoon advert for toys.

  1. (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of using an independent measures design rather than a repeated measures design in this study. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: each child sees only one advert, so they cannot compare the adverts and guess the aim (fewer demand characteristics) and there are no order effects such as boredom. Weakness: the two groups may differ in participant variables, e.g. some children may already like sweet snacks more, which could explain any difference in snack choice instead of the advert.
    2 marks each: point + context (children, adverts, snack choice).
Q24Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Plan a laboratory experiment to investigate whether chewing gum improves short-term memory, using a matched pairs design. Your plan should include details of the variables, the matching procedure, controls and how the data will be analysed. [10]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of your plan and suggest how it could be improved. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: IV – chewing sugar-free mint gum vs not chewing; DV – number of digits recalled in the correct order on a digit-span test (strings of increasing length, read out by recording). Sample: 40 university students; pre-test on a digit-span test, rank by score, pair adjacent participants, randomly allocate one of each pair to each condition. Controls: same room, same time of day, same recorded instructions and digit lists, gum given 2 minutes before the test, no other food or drink. Ethics: informed consent, right to withdraw, check for allergies, debrief. Data: compare mean (or median) digit span of the two conditions; bar chart.
    Level 4 (9–10): accurate and detailed plan covering IV and operationalised DV, clear matching procedure with random allocation within pairs, several controls, ethics and data analysis; replicable. Level 3 (6–8): most features with some detail. Level 2 (3–5): basic plan with matching mentioned but vague. Level 1 (1–2): little relevant detail. Max 4 if design is not matched pairs.
  2. (b) Weakness: matching only on digit span leaves other participant variables, such as anxiety, unmatched. Improvement: also measure anxiety with a short rating scale and match on both, or use repeated measures with two equivalent digit lists and counterbalancing.
    1 mark weakness; 1 mark explanation; 1 mark improvement; 1 mark why improvement helps.
Q25Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical repeated measures experiment tests reading comprehension (score out of 25) with phone notifications switched off (condition A) or on (condition B). The order was counterbalanced.Order 1 (A then B): A scores 18, 16, 19, 17, 20; B scores 14, 13, 15, 12, 16Order 2 (B then A): B scores 11, 12, 10, 13, 14; A scores 19, 20, 18, 21, 22

  1. (a) Calculate the mean score for condition A and for condition B, using all ten participants. [2]
  2. (b) Using the data, explain whether there is evidence of an order effect. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why the researcher can still draw a conclusion about the IV. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Condition A: 19; condition B: 13.
    A: (18+16+19+17+20+19+20+18+21+22) ÷ 10 = 190 ÷ 10 = 19 (1). B: (14+13+15+12+16+11+12+10+13+14) ÷ 10 = 130 ÷ 10 = 13 (1).
  2. (b) Yes, a practice effect: condition A mean was 18 when done first and 20 when done second; condition B mean was 12 when done first and 14 when done second, so each condition scored 2 more when done second.
    1 mark: A first 18 vs second 20; 1 mark: B first 12 vs second 14; 1 mark: names practice effect / both improve when second.
  3. (c) Because the design was counterbalanced, the practice effect was spread equally over both conditions, so the 6-point difference between A and B is still due to the notifications.
    1 mark.
Q26Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment tests whether students in a school in Accra understand a passage better when it is printed on paper or shown on a tablet.

  1. (a) Compare the repeated measures and independent measures designs for this study, and explain which you would recommend. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Repeated measures removes differences in reading ability between conditions and needs half as many students, but students would read two passages and could guess the aim or improve with practice; independent measures avoids order effects and guessing, but differences in reading ability between groups could confound the result. Recommendation: repeated measures with two passages of equal difficulty and counterbalancing, because reading ability varies widely between students and is the biggest threat to validity.
    1 mark repeated strength; 1 mark repeated weakness; 1 mark independent strength/weakness; 1 mark justified recommendation.
Q27Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical independent measures experiment in Lahore compared two ways of training adults to use a mobile banking app (video or face-to-face). 40 volunteers were randomly allocated to the two groups.

  1. (a) After random allocation, the mean ages of the two groups were 34 years (group A) and 21 years (group B). Explain why this is a problem for this study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest how the researcher could have avoided this problem, and explain one weakness of your suggestion. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Age could affect learning to use a smartphone banking app (younger people may already be more familiar with apps), so any difference in performance could be due to age rather than the type of training: age is a confounding participant variable.
    1 mark: age may affect the DV; 1 mark: confounds the IV.
  2. (b) Use a matched pairs design: pair participants of similar age (and app experience), then randomly assign one of each pair to each training type. Weakness: matching takes time, and if one member of a pair drops out, the other's data cannot be used; participants can only be matched on a few variables.
    1 mark: matched pairs on age / stratified random allocation; 1 mark: described; 1 mark: weakness.
Q28Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why studies comparing identical twins can be seen as a natural form of matched pairs design. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one limitation of using twins in this way. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Identical twins share all their genes and usually the same upbringing, so they are matched on many participant variables at once; giving one twin each condition controls these variables.
    1 mark: twins share genes/environment; 1 mark: so matched on participant variables.
  2. (b) Twins are a small and possibly unusual group (they grow up together and may influence each other), so the results may not generalise to non-twins, and twins may still differ in experiences.
    1 mark: limitation (unrepresentative/hard to find/still differ/influence each other); 1 mark: explained.
Q29Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks
  1. (a) Plan a field experiment using a repeated measures design to investigate whether the tempo of background music affects how fast people walk through a shopping arcade in Karachi. Your plan must include the hypothesis, the variables, how you will deal with order effects and how you will collect the data. [10]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of your plan and suggest how it could be improved. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hypothesis: shoppers walk faster through the arcade when fast music (e.g. 140 bpm) is played than when slow music (e.g. 70 bpm) is played. IV: tempo of music (fast/slow) — same volume, same type of instrumental music. DV: time (s) taken to walk a marked 20 m stretch, recorded by a hidden observer with a stopwatch or by video. Design: repeated measures by using the same regular shoppers (e.g. staff or regular visitors) who agree to walk the route each day; or the same arcade/time slots. Order effects: counterbalance — half do fast first, half slow first, on different days; same time of day. Controls: same weekday times, similar crowd levels, observers blind to hypothesis. Ethics: public place, no personal data, debrief where possible. Sampling: opportunity/volunteer sample of e.g. 30 regular visitors.
    Level 4 (9–10): detailed, coherent plan with hypothesis, operationalised IV and DV, repeated measures clearly explained, counterbalancing, data collection and several controls/ethics, all applied to the arcade; Level 3 (6–8): most features with some detail; Level 2 (3–5): basic plan, some features missing or not applied; Level 1 (1–2): minimal. Single long answer.
  2. (b) Weakness: participants who walk the route repeatedly may guess the aim and change their speed (demand characteristics), or learn the route and walk faster the second time. Improvement: use an independent measures design with random passers-by each session (and a larger sample), or use a cover story and add filler days with no music change.
    1 mark: weakness identified; 1 mark: explained in context; 1 mark: improvement; 1 mark: how it would help.
Q30Experimental designs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical simulator study in Lahore compares the braking reaction times of young drivers (18–25 years) and older drivers (60–70 years), each driving with and without a hands-free phone conversation.

  1. (a) This study has two independent variables. For each IV, state which experimental design should be used and explain why. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the age-group comparison cannot show that age itself causes any difference in reaction time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Age group: independent measures — a participant cannot be both young and older, so different people must be in each age group. Phone conversation: repeated measures — each driver can drive with and without a conversation, which removes individual differences in driving skill and reaction speed (with the order counterbalanced).
    Age: 1 mark independent measures; 1 mark reason (cannot be in both groups/pre-existing characteristic). Phone: 1 mark repeated measures; 1 mark reason (controls participant variables/fewer participants) or matched pairs with justification.
  2. (b) Age was not manipulated or randomly allocated, so the two groups may also differ in other ways, such as driving experience, eyesight or familiarity with phones, any of which could explain the difference.
    1 mark: IV not manipulated / no random allocation (quasi-experiment); 1 mark: other variables differ between age groups.
Q31Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'standardisation' in an experiment. [1]
  2. (b) A hypothetical experiment tests whether colour of ink (red or blue) affects how well students in Multan remember a list of words. Give two ways the procedure could be standardised. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Keeping the procedure, instructions and conditions the same for every participant.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Every participant is given the same list of 20 words printed in the same font size; every participant gets exactly 2 minutes to study the list (also: same instructions, same room).
    1 mark each, linked to the study.
Q32Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the purpose of a control condition in an experiment. [2]
  2. (b) A hypothetical study tests whether a 10-minute meditation app reduces exam anxiety in Karachi students. Suggest a suitable control condition. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) It provides a baseline in which the IV is absent, so the experimental condition can be compared with it to see whether the IV had an effect.
    1 mark baseline/no IV; 1 mark for comparison.
  2. (b) Students listen to a 10-minute recording of neutral information (e.g. a weather report) through the same app at the same time.
    1 mark (same length / same setting, no meditation).
Q33Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical memory experiment, all participants see the same 20 pictures. Explain why the researcher presents the pictures in a different random order to each participant. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pictures near the start and end of a list are usually remembered better; randomising the order stops particular pictures always benefiting from their position, so position does not bias the results.
    1 mark position effects; 1 mark randomising spreads them out.
Q34Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical study, the same researcher read the same script to every participant in a calm, neutral voice. Explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) To standardise the procedure: different researchers or tones of voice could affect participants' behaviour (experimenter effect), which would be an extraneous variable.
    1 mark standardisation; 1 mark avoids experimenter effects.
Q35Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by an 'extraneous variable'. [2]
  2. (b) A hypothetical experiment in Multan tests whether chewing gum helps students concentrate during a test. Suggest one extraneous variable and how it could be controlled. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any variable other than the IV that might affect the DV, if it is not controlled.
    1 mark: variable other than the IV; 1 mark: could affect the DV.
  2. (b) Noise in the room — test all students in the same quiet room. (Also accept: time of day, test difficulty, hunger.)
    1 mark: relevant variable; 1 mark: suitable control.
Q36Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between an extraneous variable and a confounding variable. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) An extraneous variable is any variable that could affect the DV. A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that has actually varied with the IV (e.g. one condition was always tested in the morning), so it is mixed up with the IV and we cannot tell which caused the change.
    1 mark: extraneous = could affect DV; 1 mark: confounding = varies systematically with IV/has affected results.
Q37Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Write two sentences of standardised instructions for participants in a hypothetical experiment where they must spot spelling mistakes in a page of text in 3 minutes. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why standardised instructions are a form of control. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. 'You will see a page of text that contains some spelling mistakes. You have 3 minutes to circle as many mistakes as you can; please work in silence and stop when I say "stop".'
    1 mark: explains the task clearly; 1 mark: gives time limit/what to do, same for everyone.
  2. (b) Every participant receives the same information, so differences in performance cannot be caused by differences in what they were told.
    1 mark.
Q38Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why participants in a laboratory experiment should be tested in the same room at the same time of day. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Conditions such as light, noise, temperature and tiredness can affect performance; keeping room and time the same means these situational variables cannot explain differences between conditions.
    1 mark: room/time can affect performance (situational variables); 1 mark: keeping them constant controls them.
Q39Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a 'single-blind' procedure. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a single-blind procedure is used. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants do not know which condition they are in (or the aim of the study), although the researcher does.
    1 mark: participants unaware of condition/aim; 1 mark: researcher knows.
  2. (b) It reduces demand characteristics, because participants cannot change their behaviour to fit what they think is expected.
    1 mark.
Q40Controls and standardisation · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study in Hyderabad tests whether a vitamin tablet improves memory in older adults. Explain why a placebo group is needed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) People who believe they have taken something helpful may try harder or feel more confident, which could improve memory scores. A placebo group (an identical-looking tablet with no vitamin) shows how much improvement comes from belief alone, so the real effect of the vitamin can be found.
    1 mark: expectation/belief can change the DV; 1 mark: placebo separates the effect of the vitamin from expectation.
Q41Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment in Ankara tests whether a cup of coffee increases alertness in the afternoon. Alertness is measured by a reaction-time test.

  1. (a) Explain why the researcher should include a placebo condition. [2]
  2. (b) Describe how a double-blind procedure could be used in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants who know they have had caffeine may expect to feel more alert and try harder; a placebo (decaffeinated coffee that looks and tastes the same) controls for these expectations, so any difference is due to caffeine itself.
    1 mark expectations / demand characteristics; 1 mark placebo isolates effect of caffeine.
  2. (b) A colleague prepares coded cups so that neither the participants nor the researcher who gives the drinks and measures alertness knows which cups contain caffeine until all the data are collected.
    1 mark participants unaware; 1 mark researcher unaware (with method).
Q42Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Write a set of standardised instructions for participants in a hypothetical experiment in which they solve anagrams under time pressure. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why standardised instructions are important. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 'You will be shown 15 jumbled words, one at a time, on the screen. Rearrange the letters to make a real English word and type it in the box. You have 20 seconds for each word. If you cannot solve one, press Next. Please work alone and do not use your phone. You may stop at any time. Press Start when ready.'
    1 mark task explained; 1 mark time limit/what to do if stuck; 1 mark clear, neutral, includes right to withdraw; must not reveal the hypothesis.
  2. (b) So all participants receive the same information and differences in the DV are not caused by differences in what they were told.
    1 mark.
Q43Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe controls and standardisation, using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Controls are ways of keeping extraneous variables from affecting the DV, so that only the IV changes, e.g. keeping room temperature, noise and time of day the same, using a control group or placebo, random allocation, counterbalancing. Standardisation is a type of control in which the procedure is identical for everyone, e.g. the same instructions read from a script, the same materials and timing. Both increase validity (the effect is due to the IV) and reliability (the study can be repeated).
    Up to 3 marks each: description (1), example (1), why it matters (1).
Q44Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why it is harder to control variables in a field experiment than in a laboratory experiment. [2]
  2. (b) A hypothetical field experiment in a park in Lahore tests whether a dog increases the number of people who smile at a walker. Suggest two controls the researcher could use. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a natural setting the researcher cannot control things like noise, crowds or weather, which may vary between conditions, whereas a lab lets these be kept the same.
    1 mark: real setting variables vary; 1 mark: contrast with lab.
  2. (b) The same walker, wearing the same clothes, walks the same 200 m path at the same time of day in both conditions; trials are done only on dry days.
    1 mark each.
Q45Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment in Dhaka tests whether children who learn spelling with tablets do better than children who learn with books. The researcher believes tablets are better and personally tests every child.

  1. (a) Explain how the researcher's expectations could affect the results. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two controls that would reduce this experimenter effect. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researcher may unconsciously give more encouragement or extra time to the children with tablets, making their scores higher because of the researcher's behaviour rather than the tablets.
    1 mark unconscious bias / cues; 1 mark effect on scores.
  2. (b) Give instructions by a recording; have the tests marked by someone who does not know which group each child was in (blind marking).
    1 mark each.
Q46Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical study at a bird sanctuary in Sindh records how often parrots interact with two types of enrichment toy (a puzzle feeder or a hanging rope).

  1. (a) Suggest two controls the researcher should use when observing the parrots. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why controlling the time of observation is important. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Observe at the same time each day (e.g. 30 minutes after the morning feed); use the same observer standing at the same distance behind a screen so the birds are not disturbed.
    1 mark each, in context.
  2. (b) Birds are more active at certain times (e.g. early morning, before feeding), so observing at different times in different conditions could change the number of toy interactions regardless of the toy type.

Q47Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical experiment tests whether a radio advert for a juice drink is more persuasive when it uses a famous singer's voice or an unknown person's voice. Participants rate how much they want to buy the drink from 1 to 10.

  1. (a) Give two variables, other than the IV, that should be kept the same in the two conditions. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the researcher should allocate participants to the conditions randomly. [2]

  1. (a) The length and content of the advert / the volume at which it is played; the room and time of day (also: the same products shown on the rating sheet).

Q48Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical study in a school in Sukkur compared a new English textbook with the old one. Class 1 used the old textbook with Mr Ahmed; Class 2 used the new textbook with Ms Rubina. After a term, Class 2 scored higher.

  1. (a) Identify one uncontrolled variable in this study. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how this variable could affect the conclusion. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest how the study could be improved to control this variable. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The teacher (each class had a different teacher). (Also accept: the class itself / class ability.)
    1 mark.
  2. (b) If the teacher of class 2 is more experienced or enthusiastic, class 2 may score higher because of the teacher, not the textbook, so the researcher cannot conclude that the new textbook is better.
    1 mark: teacher could affect scores; 1 mark: so the IV is confounded / conclusion invalid.
  3. (c) Have the same teacher teach both classes (one with each textbook), or randomly allocate students and teachers to the textbooks.
    1 mark.
Q49Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'demand characteristics'. [2]
  2. (b) Describe two ways of reducing demand characteristics in a hypothetical experiment testing whether people eat more snacks while watching television. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cues in a study that help participants guess its aim, which may lead them to change their behaviour (e.g. to help or to spoil the study).
    1 mark: cues that reveal the aim; 1 mark: participants change behaviour as a result.
  2. (b) 1. Use an independent measures design so each participant sees only one condition and is less able to guess the aim. 2. Use a single-blind procedure, telling participants the study is about rating the TV programme while secretly weighing the snack bowls.
    1 mark each: independent measures; single-blind/cover story; covert measurement; filler tasks.

Q51Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical experiment, Researcher 1 tested all the participants in condition A and Researcher 2 tested all the participants in condition B. Explain why this is a problem. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two ways of solving this problem. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researchers may differ in manner, tone or expectations (experimenter effects), so differences between conditions could be caused by the researcher rather than the IV: researcher is confounded with condition.
    1 mark: researchers differ/experimenter effects; 1 mark: confounded with the IV.
  2. (b) 1. Have each researcher test equal numbers of participants in both conditions. 2. Give both researchers the same script and training, and keep them blind to the hypothesis (double-blind).
    1 mark each.
Q52Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe two ways in which Milgram standardised his procedure. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why standardisation was important in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. The experimenter used the same four prods in the same order whenever a participant hesitated. 2. The same learner, 'Mr Wallace' (a confederate who was never really shocked), and the same experimenter were used, and the learner's protests followed the same script (pounding on the wall at 300 volts, then silence). (Also accept: same rigged draw, same shock generator, same 45-volt sample shock, same room at Yale.)
    For each way: 1 mark identified; 1 mark described with detail.
  2. (b) Because every participant had the same experience, the level of obedience could be compared fairly across participants and the study could be replicated by other researchers to check reliability.
    1 mark: fair comparison/consistency; 1 mark: allows replication/reliability.
Q53Controls and standardisation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a hypothetical study in Islamabad, 40 university students were randomly allocated to use a sleep-tracking app or no app (control) for a month. Mean nightly sleep (hours):Group | Before | AfterApp | 5.8 | 6.9Control | 5.9 | 6.5

  1. (a) Calculate the mean increase in sleep for each group. [2]
  2. (b) Using your answers, explain why the control group was important. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why the control group's sleep improved. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) App: 1.1 hours; control: 0.6 hours
    App: 6.9 − 5.8 = 1.1 (B1); control: 6.5 − 5.9 = 0.6 (B1).
  2. (b) The control group also slept more (perhaps because they were in a study or because exams finished), so without it the researcher might have thought the app caused all 1.1 hours. The app's effect is better estimated as about 0.5 hours.
    1 mark: control group improved too; 1 mark: true effect = difference (0.5 h).
  3. (c) Being in a study made them think about sleep more (or a change in season/exam pressure affected everyone).
    1 mark.
Q54Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical laboratory experiment in Singapore tests whether people change their answers to match others in an online group chat.

  1. (a) Explain how the researcher could use a cover story to reduce demand characteristics. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one ethical problem with using a cover story and how it could be dealt with. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Tell participants the study is about which colours people prefer for a new website, so they do not know conformity is being measured and behave naturally when they hear others' answers.
    1 mark cover story example; 1 mark explanation of how it reduces demand characteristics.

Q55Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the use of strict controls in laboratory experiments. Refer to one strength and one weakness. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: strict controls (same room, time, instructions) remove extraneous variables, so the researcher can be confident that the IV caused the change in the DV (high internal validity) and the study can be replicated. Weakness: the more controlled the situation, the more artificial it becomes, so participants may behave differently from real life (low ecological validity) and may respond to demand characteristics.
    2 marks each: point + explanation (example credited).
Q56Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical field experiment at a shopping mall in Karachi tests whether passers-by are more likely to help a woman who drops a bag of oranges if she is carrying a baby than if she is not.

  1. (a) Suggest two variables that the researchers could control in this field experiment, and explain how. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one variable that the researchers could not control, and its possible effect. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. The confederate: use the same confederate, wearing the same clothes, dropping the same bag of oranges in the same way each time. 2. Time and place: run all trials at the same entrance, at the same time on weekdays, so crowd levels are similar.
    For each: 1 mark variable; 1 mark how it is controlled.
  2. (b) The number and type of people nearby (e.g. some passers-by in a hurry, groups of friends) — this could affect whether help is given, independently of the IV.
    1 mark: uncontrollable variable; 1 mark: its effect on helping.
Q57Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a placebo and a double-blind procedure could be used in a hypothetical experiment to test whether an energy drink improves exam performance among students in Lahore. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Recruit students and randomly allocate them to two groups. One group drinks the energy drink; the other drinks a placebo drink that looks, smells and tastes the same but contains no caffeine or sugar. Drinks are prepared in identical coded cans by a third person, so neither the students nor the researchers giving the drink and marking the test know who had which (double-blind). All take the same practice exam in the same room at the same time 30 minutes later. The codes are revealed after marking, and scores compared.
    1 mark: random allocation into two groups; 1 mark: placebo identical in appearance/taste; 1 mark: participants unaware; 1 mark: researchers/markers unaware (third party codes); 1 mark: standardised test conditions; 1 mark: codes broken after marking / compare scores.
Q58Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In a hypothetical memory experiment, all participants see the same 30 words in the same order. Explain why this might be a problem and how presenting the words in a randomised order for each participant would solve it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Words at the start and end of a list are usually remembered better (primacy and recency), so in a fixed order the same words always benefit and features of those particular words could affect results. Randomising the order for each participant means each word appears in different positions, so position effects are spread evenly and do not bias the results towards particular words or conditions.
    1 mark: problem — position (primacy/recency) effects; 1 mark: fixed order means the same words always advantaged / confound; 1 mark: randomising spreads positions; 1 mark: bias removed/results fairer.
Q59Controls and standardisation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a hypothetical experiment in Abbottabad, 30 students revised with flashcards and were tested at 9 am (mean score 72 %). Another 30 students revised by watching videos and were tested at 3 pm (mean score 64 %).

  1. (a) Calculate the difference between the mean scores of the two conditions. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the researcher cannot conclude that the type of revision caused this difference. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest a better procedure. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8
    72 − 64 = 8 (B1).
  2. (b) All the flashcard group were tested in the morning and all the video group in the afternoon, so time of day is a confounding variable: students may be more alert in the morning, which could explain the higher scores.
    1 mark: time of day varies with condition; 1 mark: alertness/tiredness could cause the difference.
  3. (c) Test half of each group in the morning and half in the afternoon (or test everyone at the same time), so time of day is balanced across conditions.
    1 mark: balance or hold constant the time of day; 1 mark: explanation.
