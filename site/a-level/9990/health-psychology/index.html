Q1Health promotion · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline what is meant by 'unrealistic optimism' in health psychology. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one way unrealistic optimism could make a health promotion campaign about road safety less effective. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A belief that you are less likely than other people to experience negative health events (and more likely to experience positive ones), so you may ignore health warnings.
    1 mark: less likely than others; 1 mark: effect on health behaviour.
  2. (b) Young drivers may accept that speeding causes accidents but believe 'it won't happen to me' because they think they are better drivers than average, so they do not change their behaviour.
    1 mark: 'not me' thinking; 1 mark: applied to the campaign.
Q2Health promotion · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline what is meant by 'perceived susceptibility' in the health belief model. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why a teenager in Multan might not wear sunscreen even though she knows that sun exposure causes skin cancer. Refer to perceived barriers. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Perceived susceptibility is a person's belief about how likely they are to get a particular health problem; a higher belief in personal risk is expected to motivate health-protective behaviour.
    1 mark for a belief about personal risk of a condition; 1 mark for the expected effect on behaviour.
  2. (b) She may believe sunscreen is sticky, expensive, or makes her skin look oily, so the perceived costs outweigh the benefits of protection.
    1 mark for a perceived barrier (cost, inconvenience, appearance); 1 mark for explaining how the barrier outweighs perceived benefits or risk.
Q3Health promotion · Core · Paper 3 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'self-efficacy' and give one example of how it could be used to encourage people to stop smoking. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Self-efficacy is a person's belief in their own ability to carry out a behaviour successfully. Example: giving smokers practice in refusing cigarettes in social situations to build their confidence.
    1 mark for a definition (belief in own ability to perform the behaviour); 1 mark for an appropriate example.
Q4Health promotion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Dr Farah runs a dental clinic in Multan and wants to persuade teenagers to stop chewing gutka. Using psychological research on fear appeals, suggest how she could design her health promotion message. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Use a low-to-moderate level of fear rather than very frightening images, because research on fear arousal (Janis and Feshbach) found the minimal-fear message produced the most change in dental behaviour while strong fear produced least (people may avoid or deny a very frightening message). Combine the warning with clear, specific, achievable advice (e.g. where to get help to quit, what to chew instead), so teenagers feel able to act.
    1 mark: moderate/low fear; 1 mark: research evidence; 1 mark: give clear specific actions; 1 mark: explanation (defensive avoidance / self-efficacy) applied to Dr Farah's clinic.
Q5Health promotion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the study by Janis and Feshbach on fear arousal in health promotion. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Aim: to see whether the amount of fear in a health message affects behaviour change. High-school students were randomly given one of three versions of a 15-minute illustrated lecture on dental hygiene: strong fear (vivid images and warnings of pain, infection and decay), moderate fear, or minimal fear; a control group heard a lecture on a different topic. Dental hygiene practices were measured by questionnaire before and one week after. The minimal-fear group showed the greatest change towards the recommended practices and the strong-fear group the least. Conclusion: too much fear may make people avoid or dismiss the message.
    1 mark each for up to 6 points: aim; sample (high-school students in the USA); three fear levels; control group; self-report questionnaire before and after; result (minimal most change, strong least); conclusion.
Q6Health promotion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the determinism versus free will debate. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why using a token economy to improve worksite safety (Fox et al.) is deterministic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Determinism is the view that behaviour is caused by forces outside our control (e.g. reinforcement, biology); free will is the view that people choose their own behaviour.
    1 mark each side.
  2. (b) It assumes miners' safe behaviour is controlled by rewards: giving trading stamps for accident-free periods reinforced safety, so behaviour is seen as shaped by consequences rather than chosen freely.
    1 mark: behaviour caused by reinforcement; 1 mark: link to Fox et al. (stamps / mines / fewer days lost).
Q7Health promotion · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Ms Sadia runs a health campaign in Peshawar to reduce the amount of salt that families add to their food. Using the health belief model, suggest two ways she could design the campaign to increase the chances that families change their behaviour. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. make risk personal and relevant (perceived susceptibility and severity: high blood pressure and stroke, with local examples); show that cutting salt is easy and low-cost (reduce perceived barriers, e.g. using herbs and spices); give cues to action such as posters in shops and mosques; build self-efficacy through a simple reduction plan.

Q8Health promotion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how implementation intentions could be used to increase the uptake of seasonal flu vaccination among older adults. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Implementation intentions are 'if–then' plans that link a situation to a specific action (e.g. 'If I get my pension collection letter, then I will book my flu jab at the clinic on the same day'). They reduce the gap between intention and action by making the plan automatic when the cue occurs.
    1 mark for defining implementation intentions as if–then plans; 1 mark for an example linked to flu vaccination; 1 mark for explaining how they bridge the intention–behaviour gap; 1 mark for linking the cue to an automatic response.
Q9Health promotion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one weakness of using fear arousal in health promotion campaigns. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way in which fear arousal messages could be made more effective. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Very high fear can cause people to deny the risk or avoid the message, so the campaign may have less effect; Janis and Feshbach (1953) found that a high-fear dental message produced less behaviour change than a milder one.
    1 mark for identifying a weakness (defensive avoidance or reduced effect at high fear); 1 mark for a supporting explanation or reference.
  2. (b) Pair the fear message with clear, specific actions the audience can take and reassure them that the actions are achievable (high self-efficacy and response efficacy).
    1 mark for a method; 1 mark for explaining why it increases effectiveness.
Q10Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the 'Food Dudes' health promotion programme (Tapper et al.) for increasing children's fruit and vegetable consumption, including a discussion about generalisability. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: high ecological validity – carried out in real schools during normal lunchtimes; objective measures of food eaten (weighing what is left) give reliable quantitative data; clear practical application – combines modelling by admired peers (the Food Dudes videos) with rewards, using social learning and operant principles; effects were maintained at follow-up. Weaknesses / issues: generalisability – UK primary schools may not represent other cultures or older children, and food preferences and school meal systems differ (e.g. in Pakistan); rewards may produce extrinsic motivation that fades once rewards stop; ethical issues are minor but involve children; difficult to separate the effect of the videos from the rewards.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced evaluation with at least four developed points, generalisability discussed in depth, linked to Food Dudes throughout. Level 3 (6–8): mostly developed points, generalisability included. Level 2 (3–5): basic points, little elaboration. Level 1 (1–2): vague or descriptive. Max 6 without generalisability.
Q11Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 24 marks
  1. (a) Plan a field experiment to investigate whether a health promotion programme increases handwashing before lunch in primary schools in Lahore. Your plan must include a named sampling technique and a way of measuring handwashing. [10]
  2. (b) Describe the psychological knowledge on which your plan is based. [8]
  3. (c) Explain one reason for your choice of sampling technique. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example: IV – schools receiving a 4-week programme (cartoon video of child 'hygiene heroes', posters and sticker rewards for washing) vs schools continuing as normal. DV – percentage of pupils who wash hands with soap before lunch, recorded by trained observers using a checklist (washes with soap for at least 20 seconds), plus soap used (grams) per week. Sample: stratified sample of 8 government and private schools, randomly assigned to conditions; pupils aged 7–9. Procedure: baseline observation for a week, programme, observation immediately after and 3 months later. Controls: same observation times, observers blind to condition. Ethics: permission from head teachers and parents, children's assent, anonymity. Data: compare mean percentages; bar chart.
    Level 4 (9–10): clear, detailed, replicable plan with IV/DV, named and justified sampling technique, operationalised measure, procedure, controls and ethics. Level 3 (6–8): most features with some detail. Level 2 (3–5): basic plan. Level 1 (1–2): little relevant content.
  2. (b) Health promotion strategies: modelling (social learning) as in the Food Dudes programme, where children copied admired peer models and received small rewards, increasing fruit and vegetable consumption; positive reinforcement and token economies (Fox et al.) increasing safe behaviour; fear appeals (Janis and Feshbach) showing that strong fear is not the most effective, so a positive, low-fear message is used; health promotion in schools reaching many children at an age when habits form.
    Level 4 (7–8): accurate, detailed description of relevant theory/studies clearly linked to the plan. Level 3 (5–6): accurate with some detail. Level 2 (3–4): limited. Level 1 (1–2): vague.
  3. (c) Stratified sampling ensures both government and private schools are represented in proportion, because hygiene facilities (soap, water) and family background differ between them; this increases generalisability to Lahore's schools. It also reduces bias compared with an opportunity sample of schools that volunteer, which may already be more health-conscious. Weakness acknowledged: it needs a full list of schools and takes time.
    Level 3 (5–6): reason well explained, linked to the plan and to the psychology, with evaluation. Level 2 (3–4): reason explained with some link. Level 1 (1–2): reason stated.
Q12Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate health promotion in worksites and communities, including a discussion about individual and situational explanations. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: worksite token economies (Fox et al.) produced large, lasting reductions in days lost through accidents – real-world, objective data, high ecological validity; community campaigns reach many people and can change norms. Issues: situational – these programmes assume behaviour can be changed by altering the environment (rewards, information, social norms), which ignores individual differences such as unrealistic optimism, personality or motivation, so some people will not respond; individual approaches (e.g. tailored advice) may be needed alongside. Other weaknesses: extrinsic rewards may stop working when removed; difficult to control extraneous variables in field settings, so cause and effect is uncertain; cultural generalisability.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, at least four developed points, individual/situational debate discussed thoroughly with examples. Level 3 (6–8). Level 2 (3–5). Level 1 (1–2). Max 6 without the debate.
Q13Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the health belief model as an explanation of why people take up health-protective behaviour, such as vaccination, in a community in Pakistan. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: the model identifies clear components (susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy) that can be measured; it has been used to design campaigns and explain screening attendance; it highlights barriers that campaigns can address. Weaknesses: it is mainly based on rational decision-making and ignores emotion, habit and social norms; it does not predict behaviour well on its own (the intention–behaviour gap); cultural and religious beliefs (e.g. about vaccines) may be more important than the model's components; it is mostly tested with correlational or self-report data, so causal claims are limited. Application: a campaign in Pakistan would need to address local beliefs and trusted messengers as well as the model's components.
    Mark bands: 1–3 description of the model only; 4–6 some strengths and weaknesses with limited evidence; 7–8 balanced evaluation with specific research or application; 9–10 well-supported evaluation that links the model to the cultural context and considers the limits of rational decision models.
Q14Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a government should focus health promotion on individual behaviour change or on changing the environment, using the example of food labelling in shops in Karachi. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual approach: education and labelling help individuals choose healthier food; it respects choice, but relies on people understanding and acting on labels and may increase inequality (people with low literacy or limited income may be less able to act). Situational approach: changing the environment (e.g. reducing sugar in shop products, placing healthy options at eye level, or taxing sugary drinks) affects everyone without relying on individual decisions; but it may be seen as restricting choice. Balanced conclusion: a combined approach is likely to be the most effective.
    1–2 marks for description of one approach; 3–4 marks for each approach evaluated with a reason (max 4); 5–6 marks for application to food labelling in Karachi; 7–8 marks for a reasoned, balanced discussion with a justified conclusion.
Q15Health promotion · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A health campaign in Karachi reached 5,000 adults. Of these, 1,250 said they intended to reduce their use of sugary drinks, and 300 were later found to have actually reduced their use. Calculate (i) the percentage of people reached who intended to change, (ii) the percentage of those who intended to change who actually changed, and (iii) the intention–behaviour gap in percentage points (the difference between the percentage who intended to change and the percentage of all people reached who actually changed). Give each answer to 1 decimal place. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 1250 ÷ 5000 × 100 = 25.0%. (ii) 300 ÷ 1250 × 100 = 24.0%. (iii) Percentage of all people who changed = 300 ÷ 5000 × 100 = 6.0%; gap = 25.0 − 6.0 = 19.0 percentage points.
    1 mark for (i) 25.0%; 1 mark for (ii) 24.0%; 1 mark for 6.0% actual change of all reached; 1 mark for 19.0 percentage points.
Q16Stress · Core · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the General Adaptation Syndrome (Selye). [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Selye proposed that the body responds to any prolonged stressor in the same three stages. Alarm reaction: the stressor is detected, the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands release adrenaline, heart rate and blood pressure rise – 'fight or flight'. Resistance: if the stressor continues, the body adapts, with stress hormones such as cortisol staying high; the person seems to cope but resources are being used up. Exhaustion: after long-term stress, the body's resources are depleted, the immune system is weakened and stress-related illnesses (e.g. ulcers, high blood pressure) can develop. Based on experiments in which rats were exposed to different stressors.
    1 mark each for up to 6 points: same response to all stressors; alarm stage with detail; resistance stage with detail; exhaustion stage with detail; link to illness/immune suppression; based on animal research.
Q17Stress · Core · Paper 3 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Outline the role of the hypothalamus, the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal medulla in the initial stress response. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The hypothalamus detects a threat and activates the sympathetic nervous system. The sympathetic branch stimulates the adrenal medulla to release adrenaline, which raises heart rate and breathing rate and sends more blood to the muscles, preparing the body to fight or flee.
    1 mark for the hypothalamus detecting the threat and activating the sympathetic nervous system; 1 mark for the adrenal medulla releasing adrenaline; 1 mark for one physiological effect of adrenaline on the body.

Q20Stress · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks

Q23Stress · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks

  2. (b) Explain one reason why the study of work stress in civil servants (Chandola et al.) is nomothetic. [2]

Q24Stress · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Q25Stress · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of using a daily stress diary to measure stress in nurses. [4]

Q26Stress · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why daily hassles may be a better predictor of psychological health than major life events, referring to research by Kanner et al. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Kanner et al. found that scores on a hassles scale (minor everyday irritations such as misplacing things, traffic, concerns about weight) were a better predictor of psychological symptoms than life-events scores. Hassles happen often and accumulate, so they produce continuous low-level stress; major life events are rare and their effect may operate partly through the hassles they create (e.g. divorce leads to cooking, money worries). Uplifts may also buffer the effect of hassles.
    1 mark Kanner finding; 1 mark frequency/accumulation; 1 mark life events act via hassles; 1 mark further point (uplifts / individual appraisal).
Q27Stress · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Ms Mehwish, a teacher in Hyderabad, has tension headaches caused by stress.

  1. (a) Explain how biofeedback could be used to help Ms Mehwish manage her tension headaches. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of biofeedback compared with stress inoculation training. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Electrodes on her forehead measure muscle tension (EMG) and turn it into a tone or a display; she learns relaxation techniques and watches/hears the signal fall as she relaxes the muscles; the feedback rewards successful relaxation (operant conditioning); with practice she can relax without the machine and use the skill at work when she feels tension starting.
    1 mark each: physiological measure; feedback signal; learning to control it by relaxation/reinforcement; transfer to everyday life.
  2. (b) Biofeedback deals only with the physical symptoms and does not address the thoughts or situations causing her stress, so the headaches may return; SIT also teaches cognitive coping skills.
    1 mark weakness; 1 mark comparison.
Q28Stress · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the use of life-events scales, such as the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (Holmes and Rahe), to predict stress-related illness. [10]
Show answer

Q72The patient-practitioner relationship · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

In a hypothetical screening clinic in Karachi, 200 people were checked for diabetes. Later tests showed that 40 of them really had diabetes. The clinic's doctor diagnosed 50 people as having diabetes, of whom 34 really had it. (A type I error = diagnosing a healthy person as ill; a type II error = diagnosing an ill person as healthy.)

  1. (a) Calculate the number of type I errors made. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the number of type II errors made. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage of healthy people who were wrongly diagnosed as ill. [1]
  4. (d) Explain why practitioners might make more type I errors than type II errors. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 16
    50 diagnosed ill − 34 truly ill = 16 healthy people diagnosed as ill.
  2. (b) 6
    40 ill − 34 detected = 6 ill people diagnosed as healthy.
  3. (c) 10%
    Healthy people = 200 − 40 = 160; 16 ÷ 160 × 100 = 10%.
  4. (d) Doctors usually think it is safer to diagnose a healthy person as ill than to miss a real illness, because missing an illness could have serious consequences for the patient (and for the doctor). So when unsure they tend to diagnose illness and order further tests, which increases type I errors. The cost is unnecessary anxiety, tests and treatment for healthy patients.
    1 mark: missing illness seen as worse; 1 mark: so err on the side of diagnosing; 1 mark: consequence for patients.
Q73The patient-practitioner relationship · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the use of patient satisfaction questionnaires to measure the quality of doctor–patient consultations. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: quick to complete; allows large samples; gives a standardised score that can be compared across clinics; it reflects patients' views of care. Weaknesses: satisfaction may be high even when care is poor (patients may not know what good care is, or be grateful and reluctant to criticise); social desirability; questionnaires may not capture the detail of the communication; response rates may be low and biased; satisfaction is not the same as adherence or health outcomes; questions may not be tailored to the patient's language or culture. Improvement: combine with observation of consultations or outcome measures.
    1–2 marks for a description of questionnaires; 3–4 marks for one strength and one weakness explained; 5–6 marks for a balanced evaluation with specific issues; 7–8 marks for a conclusion with suggested improvements.
Q74The patient-practitioner relationship · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether patient-centred consultations are more effective than doctor-centred consultations at improving attendance at follow-up appointments after a diagnosis of hypertension. Refer to research evidence and to the limitations of that evidence. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Patient-centred consultations may improve understanding, satisfaction and trust, which can make patients more likely to attend follow-ups. Evidence: studies often compare styles using observation and patient questionnaires, and show links between patient-centred communication and satisfaction or recall. Limitations: much of this evidence is correlational, so the style may reflect other differences (e.g. more motivated patients or longer consultations); attendance is often taken from clinic records, which may be incomplete; findings may not transfer across cultures or healthcare systems; doctor-centred consultations may be efficient in short appointments. Conclusion: patient-centred consultations are probably helpful, but the evidence does not show with certainty that they are more effective; a controlled study with random allocation of consultation style would be needed.
    Mark bands: 1–3 limited description of the two styles; 4–6 evidence and at least one limitation with reasons; 7–8 balanced evaluation of effectiveness, quality of evidence and generalisation; 9–10 well-supported conclusion with a justified suggestion for a stronger study.
