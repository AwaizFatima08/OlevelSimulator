Original questions written by HomiLabs in the style of the exam, with answers and mark schemes. They are not Cambridge past paper questions and have not been reviewed by Cambridge. Try each one before opening the answer.

Q1Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe the materials used in the Revised Eyes Test. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 36 black-and-white photographs of the eye region of different actors' and actresses' faces, taken from magazines. Each photo was surrounded by four words describing mental states: one target word and three foils. A glossary defined every word.
    1 mark each for any three of: 36 photographs; eye region only; black and white; from magazines; four words per photo (one target, three foils); foils of the same emotional valence as the target; glossary of word meanings; similar numbers of male and female faces.
Q2Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Identify the four groups of participants in Baron-Cohen et al.'s study. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Group 1: 15 adults with Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism; Group 2: 122 adults from the general population; Group 3: 103 students at Cambridge University; Group 4: 14 adults from the general population, matched on IQ with Group 1.
    1 mark for each group. Numbers not required but credit them as detail.
Q3Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'theory of mind'. [1]
  2. (b) Outline one way the findings of Baron-Cohen et al.'s study could be used in everyday life. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The ability to attribute mental states (beliefs, intentions, emotions) to oneself and to other people, and to understand that others' mental states can differ from one's own.
    B1 for the ability to understand/attribute mental states to others.
  2. (b) The Eyes Test could be used as part of an assessment to help identify adults who may have subtle difficulties understanding others' feelings, so they can be offered support or social skills training.
    1 mark: application. 1 mark: explained, ethical and linked to the study (subtle deficits, adults, eyes).
Q4Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why Baron-Cohen et al. included an IQ-matched group as well as the group from the general population. [2]
  2. (b) State what the Eyes Test is designed to measure. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) To check whether low Eyes Test scores in the Asperger syndrome/high-functioning autism group could be explained simply by lower general intelligence. The IQ-matched group had similar IQ but scored much higher, so intelligence alone does not explain the difference.
    1 mark for controlling intelligence (IQ as a possible confounding variable); 1 mark for explaining that the IQ-matched group scored higher, so the low score is not just due to intelligence.
  2. (b) Theory of mind: the ability to recognise another person's thoughts or feelings from the eye region of a face.
    B1: theory of mind / recognising mental states from the eyes.
Q5Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the researchers used a forced-choice format (four words to choose from) rather than asking participants to describe what the person in each photograph was feeling. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Choosing from four words makes scoring quick and objective, so every participant's answers can be compared in the same way across the four groups; free description would need subjective judgements of whether an answer was correct.
    1 mark for objective or standardised scoring; 1 mark for comparability across participants or groups. Accept that it limits the richness of answers.
Q6Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) The mean Eyes Test scores (out of 36) were: Asperger syndrome/high-functioning autism group 21.9, general population 26.2, students 28.0, IQ-matched group 30.9. Calculate the difference between the mean of the Asperger/autism group and the mean of the IQ-matched group. [1]
  2. (b) Express the Asperger/autism group's mean as a percentage of the maximum score. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [1]
  3. (c) Explain what these results suggest about theory of mind in adults with Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 9.0
    30.9 − 21.9 = 9.0
  2. (b) 60.8%
    21.9 ÷ 36 × 100 = 60.83… → 60.8%
  3. (c) They scored lowest of all groups, even below people of the same IQ, which suggests adults with AS/HFA have a specific difficulty in attributing mental states to others from the eyes (a subtle theory of mind deficit), not just lower general intelligence.
    1 mark: AS/HFA group lowest, including below IQ-matched group. 1 mark: conclusion – subtle theory of mind deficit that is not explained by IQ.
Q7Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline two problems with the original Eyes Test that Baron-Cohen et al. tried to solve in the revised version. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The original had only two answer choices, so participants could score 50% by guessing and many people scored near the top (ceiling effect); the revised test used four choices. The original had only 25 items, giving a narrow range of scores; the revised test had 36. (Also: original foils were often the semantic opposite of the target, making it too easy; some items could be solved by basic emotions or gaze direction; there were more female faces.)
    2 marks per problem: 1 for the problem in the original, 1 for how the revision fixed it. Problems: two-choice forced format/guessing; ceiling effect; too few items (25 → 36); foils were opposites (→ same emotional valence); basic emotions/gaze direction cues; unequal male/female faces; no glossary (→ glossary added).
Q8Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why participants in Baron-Cohen et al.'s study also completed the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) questionnaire. [2]
  2. (b) State the direction of the correlation found between AQ scores and Eyes Test scores. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) To measure autistic traits in all participants, so the researchers could test whether scores on the Eyes Test were related (negatively correlated) to the number of autistic traits a person has.
    1 mark: AQ measures autistic traits/self-reported characteristics. 1 mark: to test the correlation with Eyes Test scores (prediction of a negative correlation).
  2. (b) Negative – the higher the AQ score, the lower the Eyes Test score.
    B1: negative correlation (with explanation of meaning).
Q9Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one strength of the sampling of the Asperger syndrome/high-functioning autism group in Baron-Cohen et al.'s study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of this group's sample. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) All had a clinical diagnosis of AS or HFA, so the researchers could be confident the group really had the condition being studied, which increases the validity of comparisons with the other groups.
    1 mark: identified strength (diagnosed by clinicians / matched on IQ with Group 4). 1 mark: explained in context.
  2. (b) There were only 15 participants, all of them men, so the results may not represent all adults with AS/HFA, e.g. women, reducing generalisability.
    1 mark: weakness (small, 15 people; all male; volunteers). 1 mark: consequence for generalisability.
Q10Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline one reason why a low score on the Eyes Test might not reflect poor theory of mind. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one way researchers could test whether the Eyes Test measures theory of mind rather than general vocabulary. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A participant may not know the meaning of one of the four target words, so they choose the wrong word for a language reason rather than a social one; or they may be tired or not paying attention.
    1 mark for a vocabulary or word-meaning reason; 1 mark for an attention, fatigue or motivation reason. Accept other sensible non-mentalising reasons.
  2. (b) Give participants a vocabulary test with similarly difficult words and compare the scores with the Eyes Test. If the Eyes Test still separates the groups, and the vocabulary test does not, the test is more likely to measure theory of mind.
    1 mark for a suggested comparison (a vocabulary task, a control task, or a second theory of mind measure); 1 mark for explaining what the result would show (validity).
Q11Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the Eyes Test was scored, including the range of possible scores. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each of the 36 photographs scored one point if the participant chose the correct word out of four options. Scores therefore range from 0 to 36.
    1 mark for one point per correct answer; 1 mark for the four-option choice; 1 mark for the range 0 to 36.
Q12Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Two friends are arguing about the validity of the Revised Eyes Test. Bilal says it is a valid measure of theory of mind; Sana says it is not. Explain one argument for each friend. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bilal: the test was carefully developed – target words were checked by judges, foils had the same emotional valence, a glossary removed vocabulary problems – and people with AS/HFA scored lower, as predicted, so it seems to measure the ability to read mental states. Sana: the photographs are static, black-and-white images of eyes only, whereas in real life we judge mental states from whole faces, voices, movement and context, so the test may not measure everyday theory of mind (low ecological/construct validity).
    2 marks for Bilal (point + link to the test's design or results). 2 marks for Sana (point + link, e.g. static images, eyes only, no context, adults only).
Q13Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Baron-Cohen et al.'s study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your evaluation points must be about the use of quantitative data. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: quantitative data (scores out of 36, AQ scores) allowed objective comparison of the four groups and correlation with AQ; standardised procedure (same 36 photos, same instructions, glossary) increased reliability; inclusion of control groups (general population, students, IQ-matched) meant differences could be attributed to AS/HFA rather than IQ. Weaknesses: quantitative scores do not show why a participant chose a word or how they reasoned – no qualitative insight; low ecological validity – static photos of eyes only; small, mainly male AS/HFA group limits generalisability; the student group was not representative of the general population.
    Level 4 (8–10): two strengths and two weaknesses, each developed with examples from the study, quantitative data point discussed in detail, balanced. Level 3 (5–7): four points with some explanation including quantitative data, or three detailed points. Level 2 (3–4): limited explanation/links. Level 1 (1–2): brief generic points.
Q14Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A clinician in Karachi wants to use the Revised Eyes Test in the assessment of an adult referred for possible Asperger syndrome. Explain how she could use the test alongside other information, and give one reason why the test should not be used on its own. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) She could combine the score with a clinical interview, the person's developmental history and a questionnaire such as the AQ, comparing the score with the norms for the general population. The test should not be used alone because many adults with low scores do not have autism, and it measures only one aspect of social cognition, so it cannot confirm a diagnosis.
    Up to 2 marks for describing how the test is combined with other information (interview, history, AQ, comparison with norms); 1 mark for a reason it should not be used alone (only one aspect; low scores occur in people without autism; not diagnostic); 1 mark for applying the answer to this adult.
Q15Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Baron-Cohen et al.'s study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your points must be about the construct validity of the Eyes Test as a measure of theory of mind. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: the same 36 photographs and four-word forced choice were used for every group, so scores are standardised and can be compared; the IQ-matched group makes it more likely that the low scores reflect social cognition rather than general intelligence. Weaknesses: a low score may reflect a vocabulary problem or lack of attention rather than poor theory of mind, so the test may not measure only the construct it claims to measure (weak construct validity); the Asperger syndrome/high-functioning autism group was small (15 men), so results may not generalise to women or to all autistic adults.
    Level-based. 9–10 marks: two strengths and two weaknesses, each developed and linked to the study, with at least one point on construct validity and a balanced judgement. 5–8 marks: points present but less developed, or no clear construct validity point. 1–4 marks: descriptive or unlinked points.
Q16Andrade (doodling) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe what the participants in the doodling condition were asked to do in Andrade's study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) While listening to a monotonous 2½-minute mock telephone message about a party, they shaded in printed squares and circles on a sheet of paper. They were told that speed and neatness did not matter. They also had to write down the names of people who would be coming to the party.
    1 mark each for any three of: shade printed shapes (squares and circles); while listening to the recording; told neatness/speed unimportant; write down names of party guests (monitoring task); same message as control group.
Q17Andrade (doodling) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Identify the independent variable and one dependent variable in Andrade's study. [2]
  2. (b) Name the experimental design used. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) IV: whether or not participants doodled (doodling group vs control group). DV: number of names correctly monitored (written down) OR number of names and places recalled in the surprise memory test.
    1 mark IV; 1 mark DV.
  2. (b) Independent measures design.
    B1.
Q18Andrade (doodling) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Andrade asked participants to write down the names of people coming to the party while they listened. Explain why this 'monitoring' task was included. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It gave a direct measure of attention during the call (how many target names were correctly detected), separate from the later memory test, so Andrade could see whether doodling affected concentration as well as recall.
    1 mark: measures attention/concentration during the task. 1 mark: allows comparison with recall / shows if doodlers were paying attention.
Q19Andrade (doodling) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Suggest why Andrade used a monotonous (boring) mock telephone message rather than an interesting story. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A boring message makes daydreaming more likely; the researcher wanted to see whether doodling could keep attention on the message when attention would normally drift, so any effect on memory could be seen.
    1 mark for linking a boring task to daydreaming or mind-wandering; 1 mark for linking doodling to keeping attention on the message (the hypothesis).
Q20Andrade (doodling) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State one ethical consideration in asking participants to sit through a boring task, and say how it could be reduced. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Protection from harm: participants may become bored, irritated or tired. This could be reduced by letting them leave at any time and by debriefing them afterwards.
    1 mark for the ethical issue (protection from harm or distress, or right to withdraw); 1 mark for a way to reduce it (right to withdraw, debriefing, keeping the task short).
Q21Andrade (doodling) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In a surprise memory test the doodling group recalled a mean of 7.5 items and the control group recalled a mean of 5.8 items. Calculate the percentage by which the doodling group's mean recall was higher than the control group's. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the memory test was a surprise. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 29%
    M1: (7.5 − 5.8) ÷ 5.8 × 100. A1: 29.3… → 29%.
  2. (b) If participants had known they would be tested, they might have tried to rehearse the information, and the control group might have rehearsed more than the doodlers; a surprise test means recall reflects attention during the call rather than deliberate memorising.
    1 mark: prevents deliberate rehearsal/memorising strategies. 1 mark: so the DV measures attention/incidental learning, increasing validity.
Q22Andrade (doodling) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the conclusion of Andrade's study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the participants were tested after they had already taken part in another study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Doodling while doing a boring task improves concentration and memory, perhaps because it reduces daydreaming, which would otherwise use up cognitive resources.
    1 mark: doodling aids attention/recall. 1 mark: explanation – reduces daydreaming / keeps arousal at a suitable level.
  2. (b) Andrade wanted them to feel slightly bored or tired, so that the task would encourage daydreaming; this made it more likely that any benefit of doodling (reducing daydreaming) could show up.
    1 mark: to make them bored/increase likelihood of daydreaming. 1 mark: needed to test the idea that doodling reduces daydreaming.
Q23Andrade (doodling) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe the sample in Andrade's study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why this sample may limit the generalisability of the findings. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 40 adults aged 18–55 from the participant panel of a research unit in Cambridge (UK), who were paid for taking part; 20 were allocated to each condition.
    1 mark each for any two of: 40 participants; aged 18–55; from a Cambridge research unit's participant panel / volunteers; paid; 20 per condition.
  2. (b) The participants were volunteers on a research panel who regularly take part in studies, and were all from one place in the UK, so people from other cultures or those unused to psychology tasks may respond differently to doodling.
    1 mark: feature of sample. 1 mark: why it limits generalisation.
Q24Andrade (doodling) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

In a hypothetical follow-up study, 24 participants in a doodling group recalled a mean of 9.0 items from a message, and 24 participants in a non-doodling group recalled a mean of 7.2 items. The study is hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage by which the doodling group's mean recall was higher. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why a researcher should be careful about using this result to recommend doodling in school lessons. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (9.0 − 7.2) ÷ 7.2 × 100 = 25.0%.
    M1 subtract the means (1.8) and divide by the control mean (7.2); A1 25.0%.
  2. (b) Only 24 people per group took part, so a chance difference could explain the result. A list of names and places is also much simpler than a real lesson, so the result may not apply to learning complex material.
    1 mark for a sample or chance point (small groups, one study); 1 mark for an ecological validity or generalisation point about the simple task.
Q25Andrade (doodling) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a researcher might measure daydreaming directly, rather than only testing recall, when testing Andrade's explanation. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way daydreaming could be measured during the task. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Recall alone cannot show that doodling reduced daydreaming; measuring daydreaming (for example with a self-report scale) would show whether the mechanism proposed in the explanation really happened.
    1 mark for explaining that recall does not test the proposed mechanism; 1 mark for explaining that measuring daydreaming tests the explanation directly.
  2. (b) Ask participants to rate on a scale how much their thoughts drifted away from the message, or to say at intervals whether they were thinking about something else.
    1 mark for a method (self-report rating, thought-sampling at intervals); 1 mark for the detail of how it would work.
Q26Andrade (doodling) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how Andrade controlled extraneous variables in her study. Refer to two controls. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) All participants heard the same recorded message read in the same monotonous voice at the same volume, so differences in recall could not be due to the message. Both groups were given the same instructions to write down names of people coming to the party, and were tested in the same conditions after another study, so tiredness/boredom was similar in both groups.
    2 marks per control: 1 for identifying it, 1 for explaining why it matters. Controls: same recording/standardised message; same monitoring instructions; same pre-test conditions (after another study); doodle sheet the same for all; same surprise recall test; random allocation to conditions.
Q27Andrade (doodling) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Andrade's study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your evaluation points must be about ecological validity. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: high control (same recording, instructions, conditions) allowed a cause–effect link between doodling and recall; quantitative data (names monitored, items recalled) allowed objective comparison; the task resembled an everyday situation (listening to a phone message), giving some ecological validity. Weaknesses: ecological validity is limited – doodling was imposed (shading shapes) rather than spontaneous, and people do not usually doodle on demand in a lab; independent measures design means participant variables (e.g. memory ability) might explain differences; sample of panel volunteers limits generalisability; mild deception about the memory test.
    Level 4 (8–10): two strengths and two weaknesses, each developed and linked to the study, ecological validity discussed in detail. Level 3 (5–7): four points with some explanation including ecological validity. Level 2 (3–4): limited explanation. Level 1 (1–2): brief generic points.
Q28Andrade (doodling) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Mr Qureshi teaches a two-hour evening lecture in Karachi and notices that some students doodle in their notebooks. Using Andrade's study, suggest advice he could give his students. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) He could tell students that simple doodling (such as shading shapes) during long, dull parts of the lecture is acceptable and may help them pay attention and remember more, because Andrade found doodlers recalled about 29% more than non-doodlers. He should advise that the doodling be simple so it does not distract from listening.
    1 mark: advice allowing/encouraging simple doodling in boring tasks. 1 mark: link to findings (better recall / 29% more). 1 mark: explanation – reduces daydreaming, or caution that doodling must be simple and low demand.
Q29Andrade (doodling) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A teacher in Lahore asks whether pupils should be allowed to doodle during a lesson to help them remember. Using Andrade's study, explain two reasons why the answer might be yes and one reason why it might be no. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Yes: doodling may stop pupils daydreaming during dull parts, so more of the lesson is attended to; doodling may improve recall of spoken information. No: Andrade's task was simple and not a real lesson, so doodling may distract from complex material; the study has not shown that doodling helps in real classrooms.
    1 mark each for two reasons why yes (reduces daydreaming or keeps attention; improves recall of spoken material); 1 mark for a reason why no (complex material, distraction, lack of classroom evidence); 1 mark for explanation of the reason to the teacher's setting.
Q30Andrade (doodling) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Andrade's study is taken to show that doodling improves memory. Explain one alternative explanation for a difference in recall between the two groups that does not depend on doodling improving attention. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual differences: even after random allocation, the doodling group might contain participants who were naturally better at remembering lists, so the difference could be chance or differences between people rather than the doodling itself. A participant who enjoyed the task may also have been more motivated to listen.
    1 mark for naming an alternative (chance, individual differences, motivation, or demand characteristics); up to 3 marks for explaining how it could produce the difference without doodling improving attention.
Q31Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a target-present line-up and a target-absent line-up. [2]
  2. (b) State what is meant by a 'false positive' identification. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a target-present line-up the person (or character) the witness saw is included among the faces; in a target-absent line-up the person seen is not included, so the correct response is to reject the line-up.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Choosing someone from a target-absent line-up, i.e. identifying an innocent person (foil) as the target.
    B1.
Q32Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Define the terms 'correct rejection' and 'hit' as used in line-up research. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Correct rejection: in a target-absent line-up, correctly saying that the culprit is not present. Hit: in a target-present line-up, correctly identifying the person who was the culprit.
    1 mark for each correct definition (in the right type of line-up).
Q33Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Outline two instructions a police officer should give a witness before showing a line-up. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The culprit may or may not be in the line-up; the witness does not have to choose anyone and may say the person is not there; the witness should take as long as they need.
    1 mark for each relevant instruction (culprit may be absent; no obligation to choose; "not there" is acceptable; no time pressure). Accept any two.
Q34Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Identify one extraneous variable that a line-up study should control, and suggest how the researcher could control it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The time the witness is allowed to view the culprit at the crime, which should be the same for all participants; the researcher can use a standard recording or a fixed viewing time.
    1 mark for a suitable extraneous variable (viewing time, lighting, delay before the line-up); 1 mark for a control (standard viewing time, same recording, same delay).
Q35Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Pozzulo et al. compared young children with adults. Outline one finding about children's line-up identifications. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one way this finding could be applied by police in Pakistan when a child is a witness. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children were less likely than adults to correctly reject a target-absent line-up – they made more false positive identifications, choosing a face even though the target was not there.

Q37Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical study showed target-absent line-ups to 50 children and 50 adults. Children made a false positive identification on 22 of the 50 trials; adults made a false positive on 9 of the 50 trials. The study is hypothetical.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of target-absent trials on which (i) children and (ii) adults made a false positive identification. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why this difference does not prove that children's memory is poorer than adults' memory. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) Children: 22 ÷ 50 × 100 = 44%. (ii) Adults: 9 ÷ 50 × 100 = 18%.
    1 mark for 44%; 1 mark for 18%.
  2. (b) Children may want to please the adult or may think they must choose someone, so their false positives may reflect social compliance or response bias rather than memory. They may also misunderstand the procedure.

Q38Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why each line-up needs several foils (other people who are not the culprit) as well as the suspect. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) If the suspect were the only person who matched the description, he would stand out, and the witness could pick him for that reason rather than from memory. Several foils make the choice depend on memory and reduce false identifications.

Q39Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Outline one ethical issue in using children as witnesses in a line-up study, and explain one way the issue could be reduced. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Protection from harm: a child may feel distressed or upset at being asked to pick out a person, or may feel they have failed. This could be reduced by making the task clear, telling children they can say "not there", and debriefing them.

Q40Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Suggest one way a line-up study could record a witness's confidence, and explain why this information is useful to researchers. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ask the witness to rate how confident they are in their choice on a scale (for example 0 to 100) immediately after the decision. Confidence is useful because researchers can check whether high-confidence identifications are more often correct, which matters for how far a witness's certainty should be trusted.

Q41Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Researchers have suggested that children's false positive responding might have a cognitive explanation or a social explanation. Explain the difference between these two explanations. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cognitive: the child's errors come from limits in mental processing – e.g. a weaker memory for the face, or difficulty comparing the faces with the memory and deciding that none matches. Social: the child may know the target is not there but chooses anyway because they feel pressure to give an answer to an adult, believe the adult expects them to pick someone, or want to be helpful.

Q42Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Pozzulo et al.'s study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your evaluation points must be about the use of children as participants. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: comparing children with adults under the same controlled procedure allowed clear conclusions about age differences; quantitative data (correct identifications, false positives) were objective and easy to compare; the findings have useful applications to police line-up procedures. Use of children: findings are directly relevant to child witnesses, but children may misunderstand instructions or feel pressure, need parental consent, and must be protected from distress. Weaknesses: low ecological validity – seeing a target in a study is not the same as witnessing a real crime with stress and consequences; children's responses may be affected by demand characteristics; ethical issues with children.

Q43Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a line-up procedure that is fair for adults might be unfair for a seven-year-old witness. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A seven-year-old may not understand that the culprit may be absent, may be unable to hold the faces in memory during a long line-up, may find it hard to say that they are not sure, and may want to please the adult conducting the procedure, so the procedure is not equally fair.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark each for two different reasons related to understanding, memory load, language or compliance; 1 mark for a clear link to why the procedure is unfair (e.g. more false positives).
Q44Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how using both target-present and target-absent line-ups allows a researcher to measure two different kinds of error in a witness's identification. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a target-absent line-up, any identification is a false positive, so this measures the tendency to pick an innocent person. In a target-present line-up, a witness who does not identify the culprit has made a miss (false negative), so this measures the failure to recognise the person who was really there.
    1 mark for explaining a false positive measured in target-absent line-ups; 1 mark for explaining a miss or false negative measured in target-present line-ups; 1 mark each for explaining why one type of line-up cannot measure the other.
Q45Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the use of laboratory line-up studies to explain the accuracy of real eyewitnesses, in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: the researcher controls the target, the procedure and the time, so cause and effect can be tested; quantitative counts of hits and false positives allow objective comparison between children and adults. Weaknesses: participants know the situation is not real, so they may not take it as seriously as a real witness, reducing ecological validity; real crimes involve stress, delays and suggestive questioning that are not present in the lab, so results may not generalise to real eyewitnesses.
    Level-based. 9–10 marks: two strengths and two weaknesses, each explained and linked to line-up research, with a balanced judgement. 5–8 marks: some explanation but less developed or unbalanced. 1–4 marks: descriptive, unlinked points.
