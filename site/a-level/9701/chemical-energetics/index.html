Q1Calorimetry (q = mcΔT), neutralisation & combustion · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A student in a Lahore college lab mixes 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid with 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide in a polystyrene cup. Both solutions start at 24.3 °C. The highest temperature reached is 31.0 °C.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy released, in J, in this experiment. Assume the density of the mixture is 1.00 g/cm³ and its specific heat capacity is 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the enthalpy change of neutralisation, in kJ/mol. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the experiment is carried out in an expanded polystyrene cup with a lid. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2801 J (2.80 × 10³ J)
    M1 mass = 100 g; A1 q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.7 = 2800.6 J
  2. (b) −56.0 kJ/mol
    M1 n(H₂O) = 0.0500 × 1.00 = 0.0500 mol; A1 ΔH = −2.8006 ÷ 0.0500 = −56.0 kJ/mol (negative sign needed)
  3. (c) Polystyrene is a good insulator and the lid reduces heat loss to the surroundings.
    B1 reduces heat loss / good insulator

Assume the mixture has a density of 1.00 g/cm³ and a specific heat capacity of 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is the temperature rise?A 6.8 °CB 13.6 °CC 27.3 °CD 57.0 °C [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 13.6 °C

Q4Calorimetry (q = mcΔT), neutralisation & combustion · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A spirit burner containing ethanol is used to heat 200 g of water in a copper can. The temperature of the water rises from 21.0 °C to 45.5 °C. The burner loses 1.12 g in mass. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy transferred to the water, in kJ. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol, in kJ/mol, from these results. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (Mr of ethanol = 46.0) [2]
  3. (c) The data-book value is −1367 kJ/mol. Give two reasons why the experimental value is less exothermic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20.48 kJ
    B1 q = 200 × 4.18 × 24.5 = 20482 J = 20.48 kJ
  2. (b) −841 kJ/mol
    M1 n = 1.12 ÷ 46.0 = 0.02435 mol; A1 ΔH = −20.48 ÷ 0.02435 = −841 kJ/mol
  3. (c) Heat lost to the surroundings (air, can); incomplete combustion of ethanol.
    1 mark each for any two of: heat loss to surroundings / heating the can and air; incomplete combustion (soot seen); evaporation of ethanol from the wick; conditions not standard (water formed as vapour)
Q5Calorimetry (q = mcΔT), neutralisation & combustion · Standard · Paper 3 style · 7 marks

A student places 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol/dm³ copper(II) sulfate solution in an insulated cup and records the temperature every minute. At 3.5 minutes an excess of zinc powder is added and the mixture is stirred.Time / min: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8Temperature / °C: 22.0, 22.0, 22.0, 22.0, 40.5, 44.0, 43.4, 42.8, 42.2Assume the solution has density 1.00 g/cm³ and specific heat capacity 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. (a) The cooling after 5 minutes is linear. By extrapolating this cooling line back to 3.5 minutes, find the maximum temperature the mixture would have reached with no heat loss. Hence find ΔT. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the enthalpy change, in kJ/mol, for Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why the zinc is used as a powder and in excess. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 44.9 °C; ΔT = 22.9 °C
    M1 cooling rate 0.6 °C per minute; temperature at 3.5 min = 44.0 + 0.6 × 1.5 = 44.9 °C; A1 ΔT = 44.9 − 22.0 = 22.9 °C
  2. (b) −191 kJ/mol
    M1 q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 22.9 = 4786 J; M1 n(CuSO₄) = 0.0250 mol (limiting); A1 ΔH = −191 kJ/mol
  3. (c) Powder reacts fast, so the reaction finishes before much heat is lost; excess ensures all the CuSO₄ reacts, so moles reacting are known.
    B1 powder: large surface area, fast reaction, less heat loss during reaction; B1 excess: all copper(II) ions react / CuSO₄ is limiting
Q6Calorimetry (q = mcΔT), neutralisation & combustion · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

40.0 cm³ of 1.50 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, is mixed with 60.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide. The temperature rises by 7.9 °C. Assume the solution has a density of 1.00 g/cm³ and a specific heat capacity of 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. (a) Show by calculation which reagent is in excess. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the enthalpy change of neutralisation of ethanoic acid, in kJ/mol, to 3 significant figures. [3]
  3. (c) The value for hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide is about −57 kJ/mol. Explain why the value for ethanoic acid is less exothermic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) NaOH is in excess (0.0720 mol NaOH v 0.0600 mol CH₃COOH).
    B1 n(acid) = 0.0400 × 1.50 = 0.0600; n(NaOH) = 0.0600 × 1.20 = 0.0720, so NaOH in excess
  2. (b) −55.0 kJ/mol
    M1 q = 100 × 4.18 × 7.9 = 3302.2 J; M1 moles water = 0.0600 (limiting acid); A1 ΔH = −55.0 kJ/mol
  3. (c) Ethanoic acid is a weak acid, only partly ionised; some energy is absorbed to ionise (break O–H bonds in) the acid as it reacts.
    B1 weak acid / partially dissociated; B1 energy needed to complete dissociation (endothermic), reducing the overall energy released

On a trip to Murree, a camping stove burns propane, C₃H₈. The standard enthalpy change of combustion of propane is −2220 kJ/mol. The stove heats 1.50 kg of water from 8 °C to 100 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy needed to heat the water, in kJ. [1]
  2. (b) Only 45% of the energy released by burning propane reaches the water. Calculate the mass of propane, in g, burned. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (Mr of propane = 44.0) [3]

  2. (b) 25.4 g

Q8Hess's law cycles · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Standard enthalpy changes of combustion: carbon (graphite) −394 kJ/mol; carbon monoxide −283 kJ/mol.

  1. (a) Write an equation, with state symbols, for the standard enthalpy change of formation of carbon monoxide. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why this enthalpy change cannot easily be measured directly. [1]
  3. (c) Use the data to calculate the standard enthalpy change of formation of CO, in kJ/mol. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g)
    B1 correct equation with state symbols, 1 mol CO
  2. (b) Burning carbon in limited oxygen also forms some CO₂ (and leaves unburnt carbon), so pure CO is not formed.
    B1 CO₂ also forms / cannot stop at CO
  3. (c) −111 kJ/mol
    M1 cycle: ΔHf(CO) = ΔHc(C) − ΔHc(CO); A1 = −394 − (−283) = −111 kJ/mol

Q10Hess's law cycles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Standard enthalpy changes of combustion: C(graphite) −394 kJ/mol; H₂(g) −286 kJ/mol; C₂H₅OH(l) −1367 kJ/mol.

  1. (a) Draw (describe) a Hess cycle linking the formation of ethanol from its elements with the combustion products. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the standard enthalpy change of formation of ethanol, in kJ/mol. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Elements 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l) (ΔHf); both elements and ethanol go down to 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l) with excess O₂.

  2. (b) −279 kJ/mol

Q11Hess's law cycles · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Iron is extracted in a blast furnace by the reaction Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO₂(g).Standard enthalpy changes of formation / kJ/mol: Fe₂O₃(s) −824; CO(g) −111; CO₂(g) −394.What is the standard enthalpy change of this reaction?A −25 kJ/molB +25 kJ/molC +541 kJ/molD −2339 kJ/mol [1]

Q12Hess's law cycles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A cement works near Dera Ghazi Khan needs the enthalpy change for making quicklime. Two reactions with hydrochloric acid are measured:CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) ΔH₁ = −15 kJ/molCaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ΔH₂ = −193 kJ/mol

  1. (a) Calculate ΔH for the thermal decomposition CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), in kJ/mol. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why ΔH for this decomposition is found indirectly rather than by heating calcium carbonate in a calorimeter. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) +178 kJ/mol
    M1 ΔH = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂; A1 = −15 − (−193) = +178 kJ/mol
  2. (b) The reaction needs a high temperature (about 800 °C or more), so it is impossible to measure the heat absorbed with a simple calorimeter; and it is hard to make decomposition complete.
    B1 high temperature needed / continuous heating; B1 cannot measure energy taken in / decomposition incomplete
Q13Hess's law cycles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Two experiments are done. In each, the solid is added to 50.0 g of water in an insulated cup (specific heat capacity 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹).Experiment 1: 3.99 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ — temperature rises by 7.6 K.Experiment 2: 6.24 g of CuSO₄·5H₂O — temperature falls by 1.1 K.

  1. (a) Calculate ΔH for dissolving each solid, in kJ/mol. (Mr: CuSO₄ = 159.6, CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.6) [3]
  2. (b) Use a Hess cycle to calculate ΔH for CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s), in kJ/mol, to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why this enthalpy change cannot be found by direct measurement. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Anhydrous: −63.5 kJ/mol; hydrated: +9.20 kJ/mol
    M1 n = 3.99 ÷ 159.6 = 6.24 ÷ 249.6 = 0.0250 mol; A1 anhydrous: −50.0 × 4.18 × 7.6 ÷ 25.0 = −63.5; A1 hydrated: +50.0 × 4.18 × 1.1 ÷ 25.0 = +9.2
  2. (b) −72.7 kJ/mol
    M1 ΔH = ΔH(anhydrous) − ΔH(hydrated); A1 = −63.5 − (+9.2) = −72.7 kJ/mol
  3. (c) Water cannot be added in exactly the right amount; the solid would dissolve/partly hydrate, so the reaction cannot be made to go to completion cleanly.
    B1 cannot control hydration / excess water dissolves solid / slow, incomplete reaction

    B1 endothermic / energy needed to break bonds

Show answer
  1. (a) (i) neither: two moles of NaCl formed. (ii) neither: water should be liquid under standard conditions. (iii) formation only: one mole of CH₄ from elements in standard states. (iv) combustion of CO only: CO₂ is formed from a compound, not from elements.

  2. (b) O₂(g) is oxygen's standard state, so no change occurs in forming it; O₃ is a different form made from O₂ with an energy change.

Bond energies / kJ/mol: C=C 610; C–C 350; C–H 410; H–H 436. This reaction is used to harden vegetable oils in ghee factories.

  1. (a) Calculate the enthalpy change for C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), in kJ/mol. [3]
  2. (b) State why a value found from bond energies may differ from the accurate value. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) −124 kJ/mol
    M1 bonds broken: C=C + H–H = 610 + 436 = 1046 (4 C–H unchanged); M1 bonds made: C–C + 2 C–H = 350 + 820 = 1170; A1 ΔH = 1046 − 1170 = −124 kJ/mol
  2. (b) Bond energies are average values from many compounds.
    B1 average bond energies

  1. (a) Calculate the enthalpy change for CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g), in kJ/mol. [3]
  2. (b) The standard enthalpy change of combustion of methane is −890 kJ/mol. Give two reasons why your answer differs from this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) −818 kJ/mol
    M1 broken: 4(410) + 2(496) = 2632; M1 made: 2(805) + 4(460) = 3450; A1 ΔH = 2632 − 3450 = −818 kJ/mol
  2. (b) Bond energies are averages; the standard value forms liquid water, releasing extra energy when water vapour condenses.
    B1 average bond energies; B1 water as liquid in standard ΔHc (condensation exothermic)

Show answer
  1. (a) B −119 kJ/mol
    Only one C–H and one Cl–Cl bond break: 410 + 242 = 652; one C–Cl and one H–Cl bond form: 340 + 431 = 771; ΔH = 652 − 771 = −119 kJ/mol. A has the sign reversed; C leaves out the H–Cl bond formed; D counts only the bonds made.

  1. (a) Calculate the bond energy of the N–H bond, in kJ/mol, to 3 significant figures. [3]
  2. (b) Use the bond energy of N≡N to explain why the Haber process needs an iron catalyst. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 391 kJ/mol
    M1 broken: 944 + 3(436) = 2252; M1 made: 6E(N–H); −92 = 2252 − 6E; A1 E = 2344 ÷ 6 = 390.7 ≈ 391 kJ/mol
  2. (b) N≡N is very strong (944 kJ/mol), giving a high activation energy; the catalyst gives an alternative route with lower Ea so the rate is acceptable.
    B1 strong triple bond / high Ea; B1 catalyst lowers Ea via alternative pathway
Q25Reaction pathway diagrams & activation energy · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

For a reaction, the activation energy is +75 kJ/mol and ΔH = −40 kJ/mol.

  1. (a) Describe the reaction pathway diagram for this reaction. Say where the reactants and products are and how Ea and ΔH are shown. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction, in kJ/mol. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Products lower than reactants; curve rises to a maximum (transition state) then falls; Ea is from reactants up to the peak; ΔH is the drop from reactants to products (downward arrow).
    B1 products below reactants (exothermic); B1 Ea arrow from reactant level to top of curve; B1 ΔH arrow from reactants down to products
  2. (b) 115 kJ/mol
    B1 Ea(reverse) = 75 + 40 = 115 kJ/mol
Q26Reaction pathway diagrams & activation energy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

An endothermic reaction has ΔH = +30 kJ/mol. Without a catalyst the forward activation energy is 120 kJ/mol; with a catalyst it is 70 kJ/mol.

  1. (a) Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction with and without the catalyst, in kJ/mol. [2]
  2. (b) State the value of ΔH with the catalyst and explain. [1]
  3. (c) Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction, using the idea of a Boltzmann distribution. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Without catalyst: 90 kJ/mol; with catalyst: 40 kJ/mol.
    B1 120 − 30 = 90; B1 70 − 30 = 40
  2. (b) +30 kJ/mol; the catalyst does not change the energies of reactants or products.
    B1 unchanged with reason
  3. (c) It provides an alternative pathway with lower Ea, so a larger fraction of molecules have energy ≥ Ea; more successful collisions per second.
    B1 alternative route, lower Ea; B1 more particles with E ≥ Ea (greater area under the curve beyond Ea) so more effective collisions
Q27Reaction pathway diagrams & activation energy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

  1. (a) State the overall enthalpy change of the reaction, and whether it is exothermic or endothermic. [1]
  2. (b) Identify the rate-determining step and give a reason. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the activation energy for the overall reverse reaction (products → reactants), in kJ/mol. [1]
  4. (d) State how the diagram shows that the intermediate could, in principle, be detected. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) −45 kJ/mol; exothermic.
    B1 both
  2. (b) Step 1 (reactants → intermediate), because its activation energy (85 kJ/mol) is larger than that of step 2 (60 − 20 = 40 kJ/mol).
    B1 step 1; B1 compares Ea of each step: 85 v 40
  3. (c) 130 kJ/mol
    B1 from −45 up to highest transition state +85: 85 − (−45) = 130
  4. (d) It sits in an energy minimum (a dip) between the two peaks, so it has a finite lifetime.
    B1 local minimum / trough
Q28Exothermic & endothermic reactions · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A sports first-aid cold pack used at a cricket ground contains solid ammonium nitrate and a pouch of water. When the pouch is broken, the pack becomes cold.

  1. (a) State whether dissolving ammonium nitrate in water is exothermic or endothermic, and give the sign of ΔH. [1]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of bonds broken and formed, why some reactions are exothermic. [2]
  3. (c) Give one everyday exothermic reaction. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Endothermic; ΔH is positive.
    B1 both
  2. (b) Breaking bonds absorbs energy and making bonds releases energy; if more energy is released forming new bonds than is absorbed breaking bonds, the reaction is exothermic.
    B1 bond breaking endothermic, bond making exothermic; B1 energy released > energy absorbed
  3. (c) Burning natural gas in a stove (or respiration, neutralisation, hand warmers).
    B1 any valid example

Q30Enthalpy calculations from experimental data · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 7 marks

To find ΔHf of magnesium oxide, two reactions with excess dilute hydrochloric acid (100 cm³ each time) are carried out in an insulated cup. Assume the solution has density 1.00 g/cm³ and specific heat capacity 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.Experiment 1: 0.240 g Mg ribbon, temperature rise 10.9 °C.Experiment 2: 0.400 g MgO powder, temperature rise 3.5 °C.

  1. (a) Calculate ΔH₁ for Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) and ΔH₂ for MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l), in kJ/mol, to 3 significant figures. (Ar: Mg = 24.3; Mr: MgO = 40.3) [3]
  2. (b) Use a Hess cycle and ΔHf(H₂O(l)) = −286 kJ/mol to calculate the standard enthalpy change of formation of MgO, in kJ/mol. [2]
  3. (c) Each thermometer reading has an uncertainty of ±0.05 °C. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in ΔT for experiment 2. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest one change that would reduce the percentage uncertainty in experiment 2 and explain why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) ΔH₁ = −461 kJ/mol; ΔH₂ = −147 kJ/mol
    M1 q₁ = 100 × 4.18 × 10.9 = 4556 J, n(Mg) = 0.00988; A1 ΔH₁ = −461; A1 q₂ = 1463 J, n(MgO) = 0.00993, ΔH₂ = −147
  2. (b) −600 kJ/mol
    M1 ΔHf(MgO) = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂ + ΔHf(H₂O); A1 = −461 − (−147) − 286 = −600 kJ/mol (ecf)
  3. (c) 2.9%
    B1 ±0.1 ÷ 3.5 × 100 = 2.9%
  4. (d) Use a larger mass of MgO (or less acid solution) so that ΔT is larger.
    B1 bigger ΔT gives smaller percentage uncertainty
