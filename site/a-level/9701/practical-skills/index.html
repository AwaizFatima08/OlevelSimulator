Q1Planning: method & apparatus · Core · Paper 5 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A student plans to investigate how temperature affects the rate of the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid by timing how long a cross under the flask takes to disappear. List the apparatus needed. [3]
  2. (b) Describe how the student should bring the solutions to each temperature and when to start and stop the clock. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest how many temperatures should be used and over what range. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Conical flask; measuring cylinders (or burettes) for each solution; thermometer; water bath (or kettle/ice to set temperature); stopwatch; paper with a cross.
    1 mark for each pair: flask + measuring cylinders; thermometer + water bath; stopwatch + marked cross.
  2. (b) Warm both solutions separately in the water bath to the required temperature before mixing (record temperature of mixture); start the clock as the acid is added; stop when the cross can no longer be seen from above.
    B1 heat separately before mixing; B1 start on mixing, stop when cross disappears.
  3. (c) At least five temperatures, e.g. 20–60 °C.
    B1.
Q2Planning: method & apparatus · Standard · Paper 5 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Plan an experiment to find x in hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄·xH₂O, by heating. Your plan should include the apparatus, the measurements to be taken, and how you will make sure all the water has been removed. [6]
  2. (b) Suggest why a lid is placed loosely on the crucible during heating. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Weigh an empty crucible (and lid); add about 2–3 g of the hydrated salt and reweigh; heat gently at first then strongly with a Bunsen burner on a pipe-clay triangle; cool (in a desiccator) and weigh; heat to constant mass (repeat heating, cooling and weighing until two masses agree within 0.01 g); calculate mass of water and of anhydrous MgSO₄, convert to moles and find the ratio.
    1 mark each for: crucible on pipe-clay triangle / tripod / Bunsen; weigh empty crucible; weigh crucible + hydrated salt (suitable mass, 1–5 g); heat, cool, weigh; heat to constant mass described; processing: moles water ÷ moles MgSO₄ = x.
  2. (b) To prevent loss of solid by spitting, while letting water vapour escape.
    B1.
Q3Planning: method & apparatus · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 8 marks

A student in Peshawar plans to find the percentage of calcium carbonate in hen eggshells. 1.20 g of dried, crushed eggshell is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol/dm³ HCl (an excess). When fizzing stops, the remaining acid is titrated with 0.100 mol/dm³ NaOH; the mean titre is 22.20 cm³.CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

  1. (a) Explain why a back titration is used rather than titrating the eggshell directly with acid. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage by mass of calcium carbonate in the eggshell. (Mr CaCO₃ = 100.1) [4]
  3. (c) Give two practical steps that would make the result more accurate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Eggshell is an insoluble solid that reacts slowly; the end-point would be difficult to see. Adding a known excess of acid lets the reaction go to completion, then the remaining acid can be titrated accurately.
    B1 solid insoluble / slow reaction; B1 known excess reacts fully, remaining acid titrated easily.
  2. (b) 95.0 %
    M1 n(HCl) added = 0.0500 × 0.500 = 0.0250; M1 n(HCl) excess = n(NaOH) = 0.00222; M1 n(HCl) reacted = 0.02278 → n(CaCO₃) = 0.01139; A1 × 100.1 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = 95.0 %.
  3. (c) Grind/crush the eggshell and wash and dry it (remove membrane); warm the mixture or wait until fizzing stops to ensure complete reaction; repeat titrations to concordant titres; use a suitable indicator such as methyl orange or phenolphthalein.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q4Evaluation: errors, reliability & improvements · Core · Paper 3 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In an experiment to measure an enthalpy change of neutralisation, the reactants are mixed in an uncovered glass beaker. Explain why the calculated value of the enthalpy change is less exothermic than the true value. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two improvements. [2]
  3. (c) The student repeats the experiment three times and gets similar results. Explain why this improves reliability but not accuracy if the same apparatus is used. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Heat is lost to the surroundings (through the glass, which is a good conductor, and from the open top), so the measured temperature rise is too small.
    B1 heat loss; B1 temperature rise too small → magnitude too small.
  2. (b) Use an insulated (polystyrene) cup; fit a lid; take temperature readings over time and extrapolate back to the time of mixing.
    1 mark each for any two.
  3. (c) Repeats reduce random error/show consistency, but a systematic error such as heat loss is present every time.
    B1.
Q5Evaluation: errors, reliability & improvements · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

A student in Multan adds marble chips to dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask, quickly inserts a bung with a delivery tube, and collects the carbon dioxide in an upturned 100 cm³ measuring cylinder full of water.

  1. (a) Identify two sources of error in this method and say for each whether it makes the volume of gas measured too high or too low. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest an improvement for each error you gave. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Gas escapes while the bung is being put in → too low. Carbon dioxide dissolves slightly in the water → too low. (Also: measuring cylinder scale reading difficult / large graduations; air displaced by acid added → too high.)
    B1 each error + B1 each correct direction (max 4).
  2. (b) Put the acid in a small tube inside the flask and tip it after sealing (or use a divided flask); collect the gas in a gas syringe (or over hot water / saturated with CO₂).
    B1 each improvement linked to the error.
Q6Planning: preparing standard solutions & dilutions · Core · Paper 5 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Calculate the mass of anhydrous sodium carbonate needed to make 250.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ solution. (Mr Na₂CO₃ = 106.0) [2]
  2. (b) Describe how to prepare the solution accurately. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.65 g
    M1 n = 0.100 × 0.2500 = 0.0250 mol; A1 × 106.0 = 2.65 g.
