Q1Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by rectification. [1]
  2. (b) A single diode is connected in series with a resistor across a sinusoidal a.c. supply. Describe the variation with time of the pd across the resistor. [2]
  3. (c) State the name given to this type of rectification and one disadvantage of it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The conversion of an alternating current (or voltage) into a direct current (one-directional).
    B1: a.c. → d.c. (current in one direction only).
  2. (b) Positive half-cycles of the sine wave appear; during the negative half-cycles the pd is zero.
    B1: half-cycles of one polarity only (same shape as input); B1: zero for alternate half-cycles (diode reverse-biased).
  3. (c) Half-wave rectification; output is zero for half the time (large ripple, less power).
    B1: half-wave; B1: no output for half of each cycle / mean power only half / needs large smoothing capacitor.
Q2Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Four ideal diodes D1–D4 form a bridge rectifier. The a.c. supply is connected between points P and Q. A load resistor R is connected between points X and Y. The diodes are connected as follows: D1 from P to X, D2 from Q to X, D3 from Y to Q, D4 from Y to P (each diode conducts in the direction stated, e.g. D1 allows current from P to X only).

  1. (a) During the half-cycle when P is positive with respect to Q, state which two diodes conduct, and the direction of the current in R (from X to Y or from Y to X). [2]
  2. (b) During the next half-cycle, when Q is positive with respect to P, state which two diodes conduct and the direction of the current in R. [2]
  3. (c) State the name of this type of rectification and describe the waveform of the pd across R. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) D1 and D3; from X to Y
    B1: D1 (P→X) and D3 (Y→Q); B1: current X to Y.
  2. (b) D2 and D4; still from X to Y
    B1: D2 (Q→X) and D4 (Y→P); B1: current still X to Y.
  3. (c) Full-wave rectification; a series of positive humps with no gaps — every half-cycle appears with the same polarity.
    B1: full-wave; B1: both halves of each cycle give output of the same polarity (|sin| shape).
Q3Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State the condition relating the time constant RC of the smoothing circuit to the period T of the a.c. supply for good smoothing. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why this condition is needed. [2]
  3. (c) For a 50 Hz half-wave supply and a load of 2.0 kΩ, suggest a suitable minimum capacitance, explaining your choice. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) RC must be much greater than T.
    B1: RC ≫ T.
  2. (b) So that only a small fraction of the charge leaks away between peaks, keeping the fall in pd (ripple) small.
    B1: capacitor discharges very little in one period; B1: pd remains close to peak value — small ripple.
  3. (c) About 100 µF or more (RC = 0.2 s, ten times the 20 ms period).
    B1: period 20 ms; B1: choose C so RC ≥ about 10T, e.g. C ≥ 100 µF (accept other justified values).
Q4Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) One diode in a bridge rectifier breaks and becomes an open circuit. Describe and explain the effect on the output waveform. [2]
  2. (b) State the effect of this fault on the ripple of the smoothed output, giving a reason. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The output becomes half-wave: one set of half-cycles is lost because the current path for those half-cycles goes through the broken diode.
    B1: half-wave output (every other hump missing); B1: in that half-cycle the conducting pair includes the faulty diode, so no complete circuit.
  2. (b) Ripple increases (about doubles) because the capacitor discharges for twice as long between peaks.
    B1: larger ripple with reason.
Q5Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

An ideal diode is connected in series with a 1.5 kΩ resistor to a sinusoidal supply of peak voltage 6.0 V.

  1. (a) Calculate the peak current in the resistor. [1]
  2. (b) State the fraction of each cycle for which there is a current in the resistor, and explain why. [2]
  3. (c) The diode is reversed. State the effect on the output. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.0 mA
    I = V₀/R = 6.0/1500 = 0.0040 A (A1).
  2. (b) One half; the diode conducts only when forward biased, i.e. during one half-cycle.
    B1: half; B1: diode has (near) infinite resistance when reverse biased.
  3. (c) The other half-cycles are passed: the pd across the resistor is now negative (opposite polarity) during the other half-cycles.
    B1: output of opposite polarity in the alternate half-cycles.
Q6Rectification & smoothing · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a mobile phone charger must rectify the mains supply before it can charge the battery. [2]
  2. (b) State one reason why a smoothing capacitor is also included. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The battery needs current in one direction to store energy chemically; alternating current would charge and discharge it in turn, giving no net charging.
    B1: charging requires one-way (d.c.) current; B1: a.c. reverses direction every half-cycle so average charge flow is zero.
  2. (b) To reduce the variation (ripple) of the output so that electronic circuits receive a nearly steady pd.
    B1: steadier d.c. / reduces ripple.
Q7Rectification & smoothing · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A capacitor is connected in parallel with the load resistor of a rectifier circuit.

  1. (a) Explain how the capacitor smooths the output pd. [3]
  2. (b) State and explain the effect on the ripple of (i) increasing the capacitance, (ii) increasing the load resistance. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) As the rectified pd rises, the capacitor charges to the peak value; when the rectified pd falls below the capacitor pd, the diode stops conducting and the capacitor discharges through the load, keeping the pd high until the next peak recharges it.
    B1: capacitor charges as rectified pd rises (to peak); B1: when input falls, capacitor discharges through the load resistor (diode reverse biased so it cannot discharge back through the supply); B1: discharge is slow (time constant ≫ period), so pd falls only a little before the next peak.
  2. (b) (i) ripple decreases; (ii) ripple decreases — both increase the time constant RC so the capacitor discharges more slowly.
    B1: both reduce ripple; B1: larger RC → slower discharge.
Q8Rectification & smoothing · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A half-wave rectifier fed from a 50 Hz supply gives a peak output of 12.0 V. It is smoothed by a 2200 µF capacitor and the load resistance is 1.0 kΩ.

  1. (a) Calculate the time constant of the smoothing circuit. [1]
  2. (b) Assume the capacitor discharges for a full period of the supply between peaks. Calculate the minimum pd across the load. [2]
  3. (c) Hence state the peak-to-peak ripple voltage. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.2 s
    τ = RC = 1000 × 2200 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.2 s (A1).
  2. (b) 11.9 V
    Discharge time = 1/50 = 0.020 s (M1); V = 12.0e^(−0.020/2.2) = 11.9 V (A1).
  3. (c) 0.11 V
    12.0 − 11.9 = 0.11 V (A1).

Q11Rectification & smoothing · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

The output of a smoothed rectifier varies between a maximum of 8.6 V and a minimum of 7.9 V.

  1. (a) Describe the shape of the smoothed output waveform over one ripple cycle. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the peak-to-peak ripple and express it as a percentage of the maximum output. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the fraction of the charge on the capacitor at the peak that flows away through the load before the next recharge. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A short steep rise as the capacitor recharges near each peak, followed by a longer, gradual (exponential) fall as it discharges through the load.
    B1: brief charging near peak; B1: longer slow exponential decay.
  2. (b) 0.7 V; 8.1%
    8.6 − 7.9 = 0.7 V (A1); 0.7/8.6 × 100 = 8.1% (A1).
  3. (c) 0.081
    Q ∝ V: (8.6 − 7.9)/8.6 = 0.081 (A1).
Q12Rectification & smoothing · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A rectified output is smoothed by a capacitor connected across a load resistor.

  1. (a) For each of the following changes, state whether the ripple increases, decreases or stays the same, giving a reason: (i) replacing a half-wave rectifier by a full-wave rectifier; (ii) connecting a second identical load resistor in parallel with the first; (iii) increasing the supply frequency from 50 Hz to 400 Hz. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) decreases; (ii) increases; (iii) decreases
    (i) B1 decreases, B1 capacitor discharges for half as long between peaks; (ii) B1 increases, B1 load resistance halves, so RC halves and discharge is faster; (iii) B1 decreases, B1 shorter time between peaks so less discharge.
Q13Rectification & smoothing · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A 50 Hz supply is rectified to give a peak of 9.0 V across a 1.0 kΩ load smoothed by a 1000 µF capacitor.

  1. (a) Calculate the ripple voltage for half-wave rectification, assuming the capacitor discharges for one whole period. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the ripple voltage for full-wave rectification. [1]
  3. (c) Comment on the ratio of your answers. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.178 V
    τ = 1.0 s; ΔV = 9.0(1 − e^(−0.020/1.0)) (M1) = 0.178 V (A1).
  2. (b) 0.0896 V
    ΔV = 9.0(1 − e^(−0.010/1.0)) = 0.0896 V (A1).
  3. (c) About 2 : 1 — full-wave halves the ripple, because the discharge time is halved (and RC ≫ T so the fall is nearly linear).
    B1: ratio ≈ 2 with reason.
Q14Rectification & smoothing · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A full-wave rectifier connected to a 50 Hz supply gives a peak output of 15 V across a 470 Ω load. A smoothing capacitor is to be connected across the load.

  1. (a) State the time between successive peaks of the rectified output. [1]
  2. (b) Assuming the capacitor discharges for this whole time, calculate the minimum capacitance that keeps the ripple to no more than 5% of the peak pd. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why the actual ripple will be slightly smaller than this calculation suggests. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.010 s
    B1: full-wave → two peaks per cycle → 1/100 s.
  2. (b) 415 µF
    V/V₀ = 0.95 = e^(−0.010/RC) (M1); RC = 0.010/ln(1/0.95) = 0.195 s (M1); C = 0.195/470 = 4.15 × 10⁻⁴ F (A1).
  3. (c) The capacitor starts recharging before the next peak, so it discharges for slightly less than 0.010 s.
    B1: discharge time is less than the full interval between peaks.
Q15Rectification & smoothing · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A sinusoidal supply of peak voltage 20 V is rectified and connected to a 100 Ω resistor. The diodes are ideal.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean power in the resistor with full-wave rectification (no smoothing). [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the mean power with half-wave rectification is half of this, and calculate it. [2]
  3. (c) Hence calculate the r.m.s. voltage of the half-wave rectified output. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.0 W
    Full-wave output has the same r.m.s. value as the sine wave: V_rms = 20/√2 = 14.1 V (M1); P = V²/R = 2.0 W (A1).
  2. (b) 1.0 W
    B1: output is zero for half of each cycle and identical otherwise, so the energy per cycle is halved; A1: 1.0 W.
  3. (c) 10 V
    V_rms = √(PR) = √(1.0 × 100) = 10 V (= V₀/2) (A1).
Q16Rectification & smoothing · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Q20Alternating current & r.m.s. values · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the r.m.s. current. [1]

Q22Alternating current & r.m.s. values · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

An electric kettle is rated at 2.0 kW for use on a 230 V r.m.s. supply.

Q24Alternating current & r.m.s. values · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

  1. (a) The current in a resistor of resistance R is I = I₀ sin ωt. Write down an expression for the instantaneous power and state the mean value of sin² ωt over one cycle. [2]
  2. (b) Hence show that I_rms = I₀/√2. [2]
  3. (c) For I₀ = 4.0 A and R = 25 Ω, calculate the mean power. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) P = I₀²R sin² ωt; mean of sin² ωt = ½
    B1: P = I²R = I₀²R sin² ωt; B1: ½ (graph of sin² is symmetrical about ½).
  2. (b) Mean power = ½I₀²R = I_rms²R, so I_rms = I₀/√2.

Q30Alternating current & r.m.s. values · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A signal generator produces a voltage V = 4.0 + 3.0 sin ωt (in volts): a steady 4.0 V with a sinusoidal variation of amplitude 3.0 V added to it.

  1. (a) Explain why the r.m.s. value of this voltage is not simply 4.0 + 3.0/√2. [1]
  2. (b) Given that the mean value of sin ωt over a cycle is zero and the mean value of sin² ωt is ½, calculate the r.m.s. value of the voltage. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the mean power this voltage would produce in a 10 Ω resistor. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The r.m.s. value depends on the mean of V², and (a + b)² ≠ a² + b² in general; the r.m.s. values do not add.
    B1: r.m.s. found from mean of square; values do not simply add.
  2. (b) 4.53 V
    V² = 16 + 24 sin ωt + 9 sin² ωt (M1); mean = 16 + 0 + 4.5 = 20.5 V² (M1); r.m.s. = √20.5 = 4.53 V (A1).
  3. (c) 2.05 W
    P = 20.5/10 = 2.05 W (A1).
