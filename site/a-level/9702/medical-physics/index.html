Q1X-rays, attenuation & CT · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how X-rays are produced in an X-ray tube. [3]
  2. (b) The accelerating potential difference is 80 kV. Calculate the minimum wavelength of the X-rays produced. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Electrons from a heated cathode are accelerated through a large p.d. to the anode; they strike a metal target and decelerate rapidly, emitting X-ray photons (braking radiation); characteristic lines come from inner-shell electron transitions in the target atoms.
    B1: electrons emitted by thermionic emission and accelerated through a high p.d. B1: electrons hit a (high-melting-point, e.g. tungsten) metal target. B1: rapid deceleration produces X-ray photons (continuous spectrum) / electron transitions in target atoms give characteristic lines.
  2. (b) 1.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m
    C1: eV = hc/λ_min (all KE of one electron → one photon). A1: λ_min = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 80 × 10³) = 1.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
Q2X-rays, attenuation & CT · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A parallel beam of X-rays passes through 4.0 cm of muscle. The linear attenuation coefficient of muscle for these X-rays is 0.20 cm⁻¹.

  1. (a) State what is meant by the linear attenuation coefficient µ. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the fraction of the incident intensity that emerges from the muscle. [2]
  3. (c) State one reason why a parallel beam is assumed in this calculation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The fraction (proportion) of the intensity of the beam absorbed/removed per unit thickness of the material (µ in I = I₀e^(−µx)).
    B1: fractional decrease in intensity per unit distance travelled (or the constant µ in I = I₀e^(−µx)).
  2. (b) 0.449
    C1: I/I₀ = e^(−0.20 × 4.0). A1: 0.449.
  3. (c) So that any decrease in intensity is due only to attenuation, not to the beam spreading out.
    B1: no reduction in intensity due to divergence of the beam.
Q3X-rays, attenuation & CT · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A metal filter used in an X-ray machine has a linear attenuation coefficient of 0.46 mm⁻¹ for X-rays of a particular energy.

  1. (a) Show that the half-value thickness x½ is related to µ by x½ = ln 2 / µ. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the half-value thickness of the filter material. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the thickness that reduces the intensity to 1/32 of its incident value. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) ½ = e^(−µx½), so µx½ = ln 2
    M1: I = I₀/2 when x = x½: ½ = e^(−µx½). A1: ln 2 = µx½.
  2. (b) 1.51 mm
    A1: x½ = 0.693/0.46 = 1.51 mm.
  3. (c) 7.53 mm
    A1: 1/32 = (½)⁵, so 5 half-value thicknesses = 7.53 mm.
Q4X-rays, attenuation & CT · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by the sharpness of an X-ray image, and suggest one way it is improved. [2]
  2. (b) State what is meant by the contrast of an X-ray image. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why a thin aluminium filter is placed in the X-ray beam before it reaches the patient. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sharpness is how clearly the edges/boundaries of structures are defined; improved by a smaller area of the target hit by electrons (smaller focal spot), or a lead grid / aperture to produce a narrower, more parallel beam.
    B1: edges well defined / how clearly boundaries are seen. B1: small anode area (focal spot) / collimate beam / lead grid in front of detector.
  2. (b) The difference in degree of blackening (brightness) between different areas/tissues of the image.
    B1: difference in blackening between structures.
  3. (c) It absorbs the low-energy (long-wavelength) X-rays, which would be absorbed in the skin and tissue near the surface without reaching the detector, so they would increase the patient's dose without improving the image.
    B1: removes low-energy X-ray photons. B1: these would only be absorbed by the patient (skin) — dose without contributing to the image.

Q8X-rays, attenuation & CT · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a CT scan produces a three-dimensional image of a section of the body. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest one advantage and one disadvantage of a CT scan compared with a single X-ray image. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Images of many thin slices are taken; for each slice, an X-ray tube rotates around the patient and detectors on the opposite side record the intensity from many directions; a computer calculates the attenuation for each voxel and builds an image of each slice; images of successive slices are combined to give a 3D image that can be viewed from any angle.
    B1: image of a thin slice (section) taken. B1: X-ray tube (and detectors) rotate around the patient, many directions in the plane of the slice. B1: computer deduces value for each voxel/pixel of the slice. B1: many slices combined into 3D image that can be rotated.
  2. (b) Advantage: 3D image / soft tissues and structures at different depths can be distinguished (no overlapping shadows). Disadvantage: much larger radiation dose (and higher cost, longer time).
    B1: advantage, e.g. 3D, can be viewed from any angle, better contrast for soft tissue, no overlap. B1: disadvantage, e.g. higher dose.

Q15Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a piezoelectric transducer produces a pulse of ultrasound. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how the same transducer detects the reflected pulse. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) An alternating p.d. at ultrasonic frequency is applied across a piezoelectric crystal; the crystal changes shape (expands and contracts) with the p.d.; it vibrates (resonantly when the frequency matches its natural frequency), sending out ultrasound waves.
    B1: alternating p.d. applied across the crystal. B1: crystal changes shape/thickness with the p.d. B1: it vibrates at the frequency of the p.d. (ultrasonic, >20 kHz), producing ultrasound (large amplitude at resonance).
  2. (b) The returning ultrasound makes the crystal vibrate (change shape), which produces an alternating p.d. across it that is detected and processed.
    B1: reflected wave makes the crystal vibrate / deform. B1: this generates an alternating e.m.f. across the crystal.
Q16Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Data for two tissues:muscle: density 1075 kg m⁻³, speed of ultrasound 1590 m s⁻¹fat: density 925 kg m⁻³, speed of ultrasound 1450 m s⁻¹

  1. (a) Define specific acoustic impedance. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the specific acoustic impedance of each tissue. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the intensity reflection coefficient at a fat–muscle boundary. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The product of the density of a medium and the speed of sound (ultrasound) in it: Z = ρc.
    B1: Z = density × speed of sound in the medium.
  2. (b) muscle 1.71 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹; fat 1.34 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹
    A1: muscle: 1075 × 1590 = 1.71 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹. A1: fat: 925 × 1450 = 1.34 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹.
  3. (c) 0.0146
    C1: α = (Z₂ − Z₁)²/(Z₂ + Z₁)². A1: α = 0.0146.

Q18Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Q21Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the wavelength in soft tissue at each frequency. [2]

Q22Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Ultrasound enters a fat layer from a transducer, then passes into muscle and is partly reflected at bone. Specific acoustic impedances: fat 1.34 × 10⁶, muscle 1.71 × 10⁶, bone 7.80 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the intensity reflection coefficients at the fat–muscle and muscle–bone boundaries. [2]
  2. (b) Ignoring absorption, calculate the fraction of the intensity entering the fat that returns to the transducer as the echo from the muscle–bone boundary. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why structures lying behind a bone are difficult to image with ultrasound. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) fat–muscle 0.0147; muscle–bone 0.410
    A1: ((1.71 − 1.34)/(1.71 + 1.34))² = 0.0147. A1: ((7.80 − 1.71)/(7.80 + 1.71))² = 0.410.
  2. (b) 0.398
    C1: transmitted into muscle: 1 − 0.0147. C1: reflected at bone then transmitted back through the fat–muscle boundary: × 0.410 × (1 − 0.0147). A1: 0.398.
  3. (c) A large fraction of the ultrasound is reflected at the bone surface (and bone absorbs strongly), so little passes beyond it.
    B1: large reflection at the bone boundary — little intensity transmitted.
Q23Ultrasound & acoustic impedance · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A coupling gel has specific acoustic impedance 1.50 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹. At the boundary between the gel and a tissue of greater acoustic impedance, the intensity reflection coefficient is measured to be 0.0025.

  1. (a) Show that (Z_T − Z_G)/(Z_T + Z_G) = 0.050, where Z_T and Z_G are the specific acoustic impedances of the tissue and the gel. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate Z_T. [2]
  3. (c) The speed of ultrasound in the tissue is 1560 m s⁻¹. Calculate the density of the tissue. [1]
  4. (d) State the fraction of the incident intensity transmitted into the tissue. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) √0.0025 = 0.050 (Z_T > Z_G so the positive root)
    B1: α = ((Z_T − Z_G)/(Z_T + Z_G))², square root = 0.050.
  2. (b) 1.66 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹
    C1: Z_T − Z_G = 0.050(Z_T + Z_G) → Z_T = Z_G × 1.05/0.95. A1: Z_T = 1.66 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹.
  3. (c) 1060 kg m⁻³
    A1: ρ = Z/c = 1.66 × 10⁶/1560 = 1060 kg m⁻³.
  4. (d) 0.9975 (99.75%)
    B1: 1 − α = 0.9975.
Q24PET scanning · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by a tracer. [1]
  2. (b) Describe what happens when a positron emitted by the tracer meets an electron. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how detecting the gamma photons allows an image of tracer concentration to be produced. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A radioactive substance (radionuclide attached to a compound) introduced into the body that is absorbed by tissue and whose emissions show where it has gone.
    B1: substance containing a radioactive nuclide that is absorbed by the tissue being studied.
  2. (b) They annihilate: the total mass of the positron and electron is converted into energy in the form of two gamma photons.
    B1: annihilation of particle and antiparticle. B1: all mass converted to energy of two γ-ray photons.
  3. (c) The two photons travel in opposite directions and are detected at the same time by detectors in a ring around the patient; the event lies on the line joining the two detectors; the difference in arrival times locates the point, and many such events build up an image.
    B1: photons detected simultaneously by detectors on opposite sides of the ring → event lies on the line between them. B1: arrival-time difference / many lines intersecting give the location; number of events shows concentration.
Q25PET scanning · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

In a PET scanner, a positron and an electron, both effectively at rest, annihilate to produce two gamma photons. Mass of electron and of positron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy of each gamma photon, in J and in MeV. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the wavelength of each gamma photon. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.512 MeV
    C1: total energy 2m_ec², shared between two photons, so each has m_ec². A1: 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J. A1: 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴/1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 0.512 MeV.
  2. (b) 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m
    C1: λ = hc/E. A1: λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
Q26PET scanning · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain, using a conservation law, why the two gamma photons travel in opposite directions. [2]
  2. (b) The tracer contains fluorine-18, of half-life 110 minutes. Calculate the fraction of the fluorine-18 nuclei remaining 3.0 hours after the tracer is made. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why a short half-life is chosen for a PET tracer. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The total momentum before annihilation is (approximately) zero; momentum is conserved, so the two photons must have equal and opposite momenta.
    B1: initial momentum zero (particles at rest). B1: conservation of momentum → equal and opposite momenta → opposite directions (and equal energies).
  2. (b) 0.322
    C1: N/N₀ = (½)^(t/t½) with t = 180 min. A1: (½)^(180/110) = 0.322.
  3. (c) The tracer's activity falls quickly after the scan, so the patient's total radiation dose is small.
    B1: small dose to patient / tracer does not stay active in the body for long.
Q27PET scanning · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

In a PET scanner, two detectors A and B lie directly opposite each other on the ring, 0.80 m apart. Two gamma photons from one annihilation event are detected; the photon at A arrives 0.60 ns before the photon at B. Speed of gamma photons = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the distance of the annihilation point from the centre of the line joining the detectors, and state which detector it is nearer to. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why detectors on opposite sides of the ring record an event only if the two photons arrive within a few nanoseconds of each other. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 9.00 cm from the centre, nearer detector A
    C1: if the event is a distance x from the centre, one photon travels x further than the other: path difference = 2x. C1: 2x = cΔt = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.60 × 10⁻⁹. A1: x = 9.00 cm, nearer A (which receives first).
  2. (b) Photons from the same annihilation arrive almost simultaneously; requiring coincidence rejects unrelated photons (from different events or scattered photons), so only genuine pairs define a line.
    B1: photons from one annihilation reach the detectors at nearly the same time (maximum difference ~ ring diameter/c). B1: rejects photons from separate events / scatter.
Q28PET scanning · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A patient is injected with a fluorine-18 glucose tracer of activity 370 MBq. The half-life of fluorine-18 is 110 minutes.

  1. (a) Calculate the activity of the tracer 75 minutes after injection, when the scan begins. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the number of fluorine-18 nuclei in the dose at the time of injection. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the patient is asked to rest quietly between injection and scan when imaging glucose uptake in the brain. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 231 MBq
    C1: A = A₀(½)^(t/t½). A1: A = 370 × (½)^(75/110) = 231 MBq.
  2. (b) 3.52 × 10¹²
    C1: λ = ln 2/(110 × 60 s) = 1.05 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. A1: N = A/λ = 370 × 10⁶ / λ = 3.52 × 10¹².
  3. (c) Muscle activity would take up the glucose-based tracer, reducing the amount reaching the brain and adding unwanted signal from muscles.
    B1: active muscles absorb the tracer / tracer goes where glucose is used.
Q29PET scanning · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

In a PET scan the positron and electron are at rest when they annihilate. Mass of electron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; speed of light = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the momentum of each gamma photon. [2]
  2. (b) Each decay produces one positron, which annihilates. Calculate the total power emitted as gamma radiation when the activity of the tracer in the body is 200 MBq. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why it is an advantage that gamma photons of this energy are produced, compared with lower-energy photons. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.73 × 10⁻²² N s
    C1: p = E/c = m_ec²/c = m_ec. A1: p = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 2.73 × 10⁻²² N s.
  2. (b) 3.28 × 10⁻⁵ W
    C1: energy per annihilation = 2m_ec² = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J. C1: P = activity × energy per decay. A1: P = 200 × 10⁶ × 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.28 × 10⁻⁵ W.
  3. (c) High-energy photons are very penetrating, so most leave the body without being absorbed; this gives a strong signal at the detectors and less energy deposited in the patient.
    B1: penetrating — most escape the body. B1: detectable signal / lower absorbed dose / less scattering.
Q30PET scanning · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A PET tracer uses fluorine-18, half-life 110 minutes.

  1. (a) The tracer is produced in a cyclotron and takes 2.5 hours to reach the hospital and be injected. Calculate the activity it must have when it leaves the cyclotron so that 370 MBq is available at injection. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one problem with using a positron emitter with a half-life of only 2 minutes, and one problem with a half-life of 2 days. [2]
  3. (c) State what is meant by annihilation in terms of mass and energy. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 952 MBq
    C1: A = A₀(½)^(t/t½), so A₀ = A × 2^(t/t½). C1: t = 150 min, t/t½ = 1.36. A1: A₀ = 370 × 2^(150/110) = 952 MBq.
  2. (b) 2 minutes: decays before it can be transported and absorbed by the tissue (needs a cyclotron on site); 2 days: remains active in the body long after the scan, giving the patient an unnecessary dose.
    B1: very short: too little activity left by the time of the scan / needs on-site production. B1: long: prolonged exposure / higher dose to patient (and others).
  3. (c) A particle meets its antiparticle and their total mass (together with their kinetic energy) is converted into energy in the form of photons (two γ-ray photons).
    B1: particle and antiparticle; all mass converted to photon energy.
