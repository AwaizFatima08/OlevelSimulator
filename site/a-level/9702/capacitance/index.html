Q1Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A 470 µF capacitor is connected to a 12.0 V d.c. supply until fully charged.

  1. (a) Define capacitance. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the charge stored. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the energy stored. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Charge stored per unit potential difference (C = Q/V).
    B1: ratio of charge (on one plate) to potential difference between the plates.
  2. (b) 0.00564 C
    Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 = 0.00564 C (A1).
  3. (c) 0.0338 J
    W = ½CV² (M1) = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = 0.0338 J (A1).
Q2Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Two capacitors have capacitances 2.0 µF and 6.0 µF.

  1. (a) Calculate the combined capacitance when the capacitors are connected in series. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the combined capacitance when they are connected in parallel. [1]
  3. (c) When connected in series to a battery, state which capacitor has the larger potential difference across it, giving a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.5 µF
    1/C = 1/2.0 + 1/6.0 (M1) → C = 1.5 µF (A1).
  2. (b) 8.0 µF
    C = 2.0 + 6.0 = 8.0 µF (A1).
  3. (c) The 2.0 µF capacitor
    B1: capacitors in series carry the same charge; B1: V = Q/C so the smaller capacitance has the larger pd.
Q3Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

The flash unit in a camera uses a 150 µF capacitor charged to 330 V.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy stored in the capacitor. [2]
  2. (b) The capacitor discharges through the flash tube in 1.0 ms. Calculate the average power delivered. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why a capacitor, rather than the 1.5 V battery directly, is used to power the flash. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8.17 J
    W = ½CV² (M1) = ½ × 150 × 10⁻⁶ × 330² = 8.17 J (A1).
  2. (b) 8170 W
    P = W/t = 8.17/1.0 × 10⁻³ = 8170 W (A1).
  3. (c) The capacitor can release its energy very quickly (very high power/current) at high pd; the battery cannot.
    B1: capacitor discharges rapidly, giving high power that a battery cannot deliver.

Q5Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Show that the combined capacitance C of capacitors C₁ and C₂ in series is given by 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂. [3]
  2. (b) State two practical uses of capacitors in electrical circuits. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Same charge Q on each; V = V₁ + V₂; Q/C = Q/C₁ + Q/C₂; divide by Q.
    B1: charge on each capacitor is the same, Q; B1: total pd = sum of pds; B1: V = Q/C substitution and division by Q.
  2. (b) e.g. smoothing a rectified supply; storing energy for a flash or back-up supply; timing circuits.
    B1 each for any two: smoothing; energy store (flash, back-up memory, defibrillator); timing (RC); blocking d.c.
Q6Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A 3.0 µF capacitor is connected in series with a parallel combination of a 4.0 µF capacitor and a 2.0 µF capacitor. The network is connected to a 9.0 V battery and the capacitors are fully charged.

  1. (a) Calculate the total capacitance of the network. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the charge on the 3.0 µF capacitor. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the potential difference across the 4.0 µF capacitor and the charge on it. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.0 µF
    Parallel pair = 6.0 µF (M1); series with 3.0 µF: 1/C = 1/3 + 1/6 → 2.0 µF (A1).
  2. (b) 18 µC
    Q = CV = 2.0 µF × 9.0 V = 18 µC (A1).
  3. (c) 3.0 V; 12 µC
    pd across 3.0 µF = 18/3.0 = 6.0 V (M1); pd across parallel pair = 9.0 − 6.0 = 3.0 V (A1); Q = 4.0 × 3.0 = 12 µC (A1).
Q7Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Use a graph of potential difference V against charge Q for a capacitor to show that the energy stored is W = ½QV. [2]
  2. (b) A capacitor is charged to pd V by a battery of e.m.f. V through a resistor. Show that only half the energy supplied by the battery is stored in the capacitor, and state what happens to the rest. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) V ∝ Q gives a straight line through the origin; work done = area under the V–Q graph = ½QV.
    M1: work done adding small charge ΔQ at pd V is VΔQ, so energy = area under V–Q graph; A1: graph is a straight line through the origin, area of triangle = ½QV.
  2. (b) Battery supplies QV (charge Q moved through e.m.f. V); capacitor stores ½QV; the other ½QV is dissipated as thermal energy in the resistance.
    B1: energy from battery = QV (constant e.m.f.); B1: stored = ½QV, so half; B1: rest dissipated as heat in the resistor/wires.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy stored when the supercapacitor is fully charged. [1]
  2. (b) The lamp circuit works until the pd falls to 1.0 V. Calculate the energy available to the circuit. [2]
  3. (c) The circuit takes a constant power of 0.50 W. Calculate how long it can run. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 182 J
    ½ × 50 × 2.7² = 182 J (A1).
  2. (b) 157 J

  2. (b) Determine the energy stored when the pd is 6.0 V. [1]

Q10Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A capacitor stores 0.40 J of energy when the pd across it is 20 V.

  1. (a) Calculate the capacitance. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the pd needed for the capacitor to store 1.6 J. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the charge stored at this pd. [1]
Show answer

Q13Capacitors, combinations & energy stored · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A technician has a box of identical 100 µF capacitors, each with a maximum safe working voltage of 25 V.

  1. (a) Describe, without a diagram, an arrangement of the smallest number of these capacitors that has a total capacitance of 100 µF and can safely be used at 50 V. Justify your answer. [4]
  2. (b) Calculate the total energy stored by the arrangement at 50 V, and the energy stored in each capacitor. [2]

  1. (a) Four capacitors: two in series (50 µF, 50 V rating), and two such series pairs connected in parallel (100 µF).
    B1: two in series share 50 V, so 25 V each (safe); B1: series pair has capacitance 50 µF; B1: two pairs in parallel → 100 µF; B1: 4 is the minimum (one or two cannot give both 50 V rating and 100 µF; three in series give 33 µF). Also accept two parallel pairs (200 µF each) connected in series: 100 µF, 25 V across each.

Q16Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks

A 470 µF capacitor charged to 9.0 V is discharged through a 22 kΩ resistor.

  1. (a) Calculate the time constant of the circuit. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the pd across the capacitor 5.0 s after the discharge starts. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 10.3 s
    τ = RC = 22 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 10.3 s (A1).
  2. (b) 5.55 V
    V = V₀e^(−t/RC) (M1) = 9.0 × e^(−5.0/10.3) = 5.55 V (A1).

Q18Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A charged capacitor is discharged through a resistor.

  1. (a) Explain why the current in the resistor decreases with time. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the charge decreases exponentially rather than linearly. [2]
  3. (c) State the effect on the discharge time of increasing the resistance. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) As charge leaves the capacitor, the pd across it (V = Q/C) falls; the current I = V/R therefore falls.
    B1: charge decreases so pd decreases; B1: I = V/R decreases.
  2. (b) The rate of loss of charge (current) is proportional to the charge remaining, so equal fractions are lost in equal times.
    B1: rate of discharge ∝ Q (since I = Q/RC); B1: constant fraction lost in equal time intervals → exponential.
  3. (c) It increases (time constant RC larger).
    B1: slower discharge, τ = RC larger.

Q22Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

  4. (d) The half-time is short (about 7 s), so reaction time is a large fraction of it; use a data logger with a voltage sensor, or use a larger capacitor/resistor.
    B1: short time → large % uncertainty from reaction time; B1: data logger / repeat and average / larger RC.
Q26Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A capacitor of capacitance C discharges through a resistor of resistance R.

  1. (a) By applying Kirchhoff's second law to the circuit, show that dQ/dt = −Q/(RC), where Q is the charge on the capacitor. [2]
  2. (b) State the solution of this equation for a capacitor with initial charge Q₀. [1]
  3. (c) For C = 680 µF and R = 4.7 kΩ, calculate the time for the charge to fall to 10% of Q₀. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q/C = IR with I = −dQ/dt (charge decreasing), so dQ/dt = −Q/RC.
    M1: pd across C = pd across R: Q/C = IR; A1: I = −dQ/dt → result.
  2. (b) Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC)
    B1.
  3. (c) 7.36 s
    0.10 = e^(−t/RC) → t = RC ln 10 (M1) = 3.20 × 2.303 = 7.36 s (A1).
Q27Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

In a stairwell light timer, a 220 µF capacitor is charged to 6.0 V and then discharges through a resistor. The light switches off when the capacitor pd falls to 2.0 V. The delay required is 15 s.

  1. (a) Show that the required resistance is given by R = t/(C ln(V₀/V)). [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the resistance needed. [2]
  3. (c) Resistors are available in values of 47 kΩ, 56 kΩ, 68 kΩ and 82 kΩ. State which one gives a delay closest to 15 s and calculate that delay. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) V = V₀e^(−t/RC) → ln(V₀/V) = t/RC → R = t/(C ln(V₀/V)).
    M1: take natural logs; A1: rearranged correctly.
  2. (b) 62100 Ω
    R = 15/(220 × 10⁻⁶ × ln 3) (M1) = 62100 Ω (A1).
  3. (c) 68 kΩ; 16.4 s
    Closest to 62.1 kΩ is 56 kΩ or 68 kΩ: delays 13.5 s and 16.4 s (M1); 68 kΩ gives the closer delay (A1).

  1. (a) Calculate the time constant. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate how long the memory is kept, in minutes. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the fraction of the stored energy that has been used when the memory fails. [1]

    τ = RC = 10 × 10³ × 0.47 = 4700 s (A1).

Q29Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

To find an unknown capacitance, a student charges the capacitor and lets it discharge through a 100 kΩ resistor, timing how long the voltmeter reading across the capacitor takes to halve. The time is 6.9 s.

  1. (a) Calculate the capacitance, assuming the voltmeter takes no current. [2]
  2. (b) The voltmeter actually has a resistance of 10 MΩ and is connected across the capacitor. Calculate the corrected capacitance. [2]
  3. (c) Comment on whether the voltmeter resistance matters in this experiment. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 99.5 µF
    t½ = RC ln 2 (M1); C = 6.9/(1.00 × 10⁵ × ln 2) = 9.95 × 10⁻⁵ F (A1).
  2. (b) 101 µF
    Discharge resistance = 100 kΩ ∥ 10 MΩ = 99.0 kΩ (M1); C = 6.9/(99000 × ln 2) = 1.01 × 10⁻⁴ F (A1).
  3. (c) Only slightly: about a 1% difference, small compared with typical capacitor tolerances.
    B1: difference ≈ 1%, so negligible for most purposes (but would matter if R were comparable to the meter resistance).
Q30Discharging a capacitor (time constant) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A 470 µF capacitor charged to 12.0 V begins to discharge at t = 0 through a 10 kΩ resistor.

  1. (a) Calculate the pd across the capacitor at t = 3.0 s. [2]
  2. (b) At t = 3.0 s a switch opens so that a 20 kΩ resistor is added in series with the 10 kΩ resistor. Calculate the pd at t = 8.0 s. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the charge that has flowed through the 10 kΩ resistor between t = 0 and t = 8.0 s. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6.34 V
    τ₁ = 10 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.70 s (M1); V = 12e^(−3.0/4.70) = 6.34 V (A1).
  2. (b) 4.45 V
    New R = 30 kΩ, τ₂ = 14.1 s (M1); start from 6.34 V (M1); V = 6.34e^(−5.0/14.1) = 4.45 V (A1).

    ΔQ = C(12 − 4.45) = 0.00355 C (A1).
