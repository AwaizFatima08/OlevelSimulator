Q1Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A hydrogen spectral line has a wavelength of 656.3 nm when measured in a laboratory. In the spectrum of light from a distant galaxy, the same line is observed at 669.4 nm. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) State what is meant by redshift. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the speed at which the galaxy is moving away from the Earth. [2]
  3. (c) The Hubble constant is 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. Calculate the distance to the galaxy. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The increase in the observed wavelength (decrease in frequency) of electromagnetic radiation because the source is moving away from the observer.
    B1 increase in wavelength / decrease in frequency due to the source receding
  2. (b) 5.99 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
    Δλ/λ = v/c (C1); v = (669.4 − 656.3)/656.3 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 5.99 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) 2.60 × 10²⁴ m
    v = H₀d (C1); d = 5.99 × 10⁶/2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.60 × 10²⁴ m (A1)
Q2Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State Hubble's law. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how the redshift of light from distant galaxies leads to the idea that the Universe is expanding. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how an expanding Universe supports the Big Bang theory. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The recession speed of a galaxy is (approximately) proportional to its distance from the Earth: v ≈ H₀d.
    B1
  2. (b) Light from (almost) all distant galaxies is redshifted, so they are all moving away from us; the further away a galaxy is, the greater its redshift (speed), as expected if space itself is expanding.
    B1 (almost) all distant galaxies show redshift so are receding; B1 recession speed increases with distance (Hubble's law), so the Universe is expanding
  3. (c) If galaxies are moving apart now, they were closer together in the past; tracing back, the whole Universe was once concentrated in an extremely small, hot, dense region from which it expanded.
    B1 galaxies were closer together in the past; B1 Universe began from a single point / very small, hot, dense state (Big Bang)
Q3Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) The Andromeda galaxy is moving towards the Milky Way at 300 km s⁻¹. Calculate the observed wavelength of the 656.3 nm hydrogen line in its spectrum. [2]
  2. (b) State the name given to this shift. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why Andromeda's motion does not contradict the idea that the Universe is expanding. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 655.6 nm
    Δλ = λv/c = 656.3 × 3.00 × 10⁵/3.00 × 10⁸ = 0.656 nm (C1); approaching so λ decreases: 655.6 nm (A1)
  2. (b) blueshift
    B1
  3. (c) Andromeda is very close, so the gravitational attraction between the galaxies is greater than the effect of expansion; Hubble's law applies to distant galaxies.
    B1

  1. (a) Calculate the fractional change in wavelength Δλ/λ. [1]

  3. (c) State how the observed frequency compares with the laboratory frequency. [1]

Q5Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A recent value of the Hubble constant H₀ is 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m.

  1. (a) Show that 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ is about 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. [2]
  2. (b) Assuming that galaxies have always moved apart at their present speeds, the age of the Universe is approximately 1/H₀. Explain why. [2]
  3. (c) Estimate the age of the Universe in years. (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s) [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹
    M1 68 × 10³ m s⁻¹ ÷ 3.09 × 10²² m; A1 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹
  2. (b) A galaxy at distance d moving at v = H₀d has taken t = d/v = 1/H₀ to travel from the starting point, the same for every galaxy.
    B1 time = distance/speed; B1 d/v = d/(H₀d) = 1/H₀, independent of d
  3. (c) 1.44 × 10¹⁰ years
    t = 1/H₀ = 4.54 × 10¹⁷ s (C1); ÷ 3.16 × 10⁷ = 1.44 × 10¹⁰ years (A1)
Q6Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Radio telescopes detect the 21 cm emission from hydrogen gas, which has a frequency of 1420 MHz in the laboratory. From one galaxy this radiation is received at a frequency of 1385 MHz. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) State whether the observed frequency is consistent with a redshift, and explain your answer. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the speed of recession of the galaxy. [2]
  3. (c) Using H₀ = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, calculate the distance to the galaxy in Mpc. (1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m) [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Yes: the observed frequency is lower, so the wavelength is longer (redshift).
    B1
  2. (b) 7.39 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
    Δf/f = v/c (C1); v = 35/1420 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 7.39 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) 109 Mpc
    d = v/H₀ = 3.36 × 10²⁴ m (C1); = 109 Mpc (A1)

Q8Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) A galaxy has Δλ/λ = 0.050. Calculate its recession speed. [1]
  2. (b) The relation Δλ/λ ≈ v/c is an approximation. State the condition for it to be valid. [1]
  3. (c) Light from a very distant quasar has Δλ/λ = 2.0. Explain why the equation cannot be used to find the quasar's speed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
    v = 0.050 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 1.5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ (B1)
  2. (b) The speed of the source must be much less than the speed of light (v ≪ c).
    B1
  3. (c) It would give v = 2c, which is impossible; at such large redshifts v is not ≪ c, so the approximation fails.
    B1 gives v greater than c; B1 approximation only valid for v ≪ c
Q9Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

An oxygen emission line with a laboratory wavelength of 500.7 nm is observed at 515.7 nm in the spectrum of a galaxy. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the recession speed and the distance of the galaxy, using H₀ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate how long, in years, the light has taken to reach the Earth. Ignore the expansion of the Universe during the light's journey. (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s) [2]
  3. (c) Explain why astronomers say that observing very distant galaxies is like looking back in time. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) v = 8.99 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹; d = 3.91 × 10²⁴ m
    C1 Δλ/λ = 15.0/500.7; A1 v = 8.99 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹; A1 d = v/H₀ = 3.91 × 10²⁴ m
  2. (b) 4.12 × 10⁸ years
    t = d/c = 1.30 × 10¹⁶ s (C1); = 4.12 × 10⁸ years (A1)

Q10Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 9 marks

An astronomer measures the distance d and recession speed v of five galaxies.d/Mpc: 20, 45, 80, 120, 160v/km s⁻¹: 1350, 3200, 5500, 8600, 11 000

  1. (a) Explain why a graph of v against d can be used to test Hubble's law, and state what the gradient represents. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the gradient of the best-fit line using the first and last data points. [2]
  3. (c) Convert your value of H₀ into s⁻¹ and hence estimate the age of the Universe in years. (1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m, 1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s) [3]
  4. (d) The distances have uncertainties of about ±10 %. Suggest two reasons why distances to galaxies are hard to measure accurately. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hubble's law v = H₀d predicts a straight line through the origin; gradient = H₀.
    B1 straight line through origin expected; B1 gradient = H₀
  2. (b) 68.9 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹
    C1 (11 000 − 1350)/(160 − 20); A1 68.9 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹
  3. (c) H₀ = 2.23 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹; age ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years
    C1 H₀ = 68.9 × 10³/3.09 × 10²² = 2.23 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹; C1 age = 1/H₀ = 4.48 × 10¹⁷ s; A1 ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years
  4. (d) Any two of: the luminosity of standard candles must be calibrated and is known only approximately; dust absorbs light, reducing the measured radiant flux; individual stars (e.g. Cepheids) in distant galaxies are faint and hard to resolve; the radiant flux of faint objects is hard to measure precisely.
    B1 each for any two sensible reasons relating to distance measurement (not to the recession speed)
Q11Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The fractional changes in wavelength Δλ/λ of a spectral line from two galaxies A and B are 0.012 and 0.036. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Explain why the ratio of the distances of the galaxies can be found without knowing the Hubble constant, and state its value. [2]
  2. (b) Galaxy A is found from Cepheid variable stars to be 52 Mpc away. Determine the Hubble constant in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. [2]
  3. (c) State the distance to galaxy B, and explain why astronomers rely on redshift, rather than Cepheids, for galaxies much further away than B. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Δλ/λ ∝ v ∝ d, so d_B/d_A = 0.036/0.012 = 3.0.
    M1 Δλ/λ = v/c and v = H₀d so d ∝ Δλ/λ; A1 3.0
  2. (b) 69.2 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹
    v = 0.012 × 3.00 × 10⁵ = 3600 km s⁻¹ (C1); H₀ = 3600/52 = 69.2 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) 156 Mpc. Cepheids in very distant galaxies are too faint to be resolved/measured, but spectral lines (and hence redshift) can still be measured.
    B1 156 Mpc; B1 individual Cepheids too faint at large distances
Q12Redshift, Hubble's law & Big Bang · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A Type Ia supernova is observed in a distant galaxy. At its peak, its luminosity is known to be 2.0 × 10³⁶ W and the radiant flux intensity measured at the Earth is 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ W m⁻². Spectral lines from the galaxy show Δλ/λ = 0.0140. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the distance to the galaxy. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the recession speed of the galaxy. [1]
  3. (c) Hence determine a value for the Hubble constant in s⁻¹. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why values of H₀ from many galaxies at different distances are needed for a reliable result. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.90 × 10²⁴ m
    F = L/(4πd²) (C1); d = √(2.0 × 10³⁶/(4π × 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁴)) = 1.90 × 10²⁴ m (A1)
  2. (b) 4.20 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
    v = 0.0140 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 4.20 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ (B1)
  3. (c) 2.21 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹
    H₀ = v/d (C1) = 4.20 × 10⁶/1.90 × 10²⁴ = 2.21 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (A1)
  4. (d) Individual galaxies have their own random (peculiar) velocities and distance errors; averaging/a graph of many galaxies reduces their effect.
    B1
Q13Stellar radii (Wien's law, Stefan-Boltzmann) · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Take the Wien's displacement law constant λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K.

  1. (a) State Wien's displacement law. [1]
  2. (b) The spectrum of the Sun has its peak intensity at a wavelength of about 500 nm. Estimate the surface temperature of the Sun. [2]
  3. (c) State the assumption made about the Sun in (b). [1]

  2. (b) 5800 K

    B1

Q15Stellar radii (Wien's law, Stefan-Boltzmann) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Take the Wien's displacement law constant λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K.

  1. (a) Describe how the surface temperature of a star can be found from its spectrum. [2]
  2. (b) The spectrum of a star peaks at 420 nm. Calculate its surface temperature. [2]
  3. (c) State whether this star is hotter or cooler than the Sun (peak at about 500 nm), giving a reason. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Measure the intensity of the star's radiation at different wavelengths and find the wavelength λ_max at which it is greatest; then T = (2.9 × 10⁻³ m K)/λ_max (Wien's law).

  2. (b) 6900 K

    B1
Q16Stellar radii (Wien's law, Stefan-Boltzmann) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A red supergiant star has a luminosity of 4.0 × 10³¹ W. The peak of its emission spectrum is at a wavelength of 850 nm. Take the Wien's displacement law constant λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K. Take the Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

  1. (a) Calculate the surface temperature of the star. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the radius of the star. [2]
  3. (c) The radius of the Earth's orbit is 1.5 × 10¹¹ m. Comment on the size of this star. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3410 K
    T = 2.9 × 10⁻³/850 × 10⁻⁹ (C1) = 3410 K (A1)
  2. (b) 6.44 × 10¹¹ m
    r = √(L/(4πσT⁴)) (C1) = √(4.0 × 10³¹/(4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 3410⁴)) = 6.44 × 10¹¹ m (A1)

    B1
Q17Stellar radii (Wien's law, Stefan-Boltzmann) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A white dwarf star has a surface temperature of 25 000 K and a luminosity of 2.1 × 10²⁵ W. Take the Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. Take the Wien's displacement law constant λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K.

  1. (a) Calculate the radius of the white dwarf. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the wavelength at which its emission peaks and state the region of the electromagnetic spectrum. [2]

Show answer
  1. (a) 8.7 × 10⁶ m
    r = √(L/(4πσT⁴)) (C1) = √(2.1 × 10²⁵/(4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 25 000⁴)) = 8.7 × 10⁶ m (A1)
  2. (b) 116 nm; ultraviolet
    B1 λ = 2.9 × 10⁻³/25 000 = 1.16 × 10⁻⁷ m = 116 nm; B1 ultraviolet
  3. (c) The white dwarf is about the size of the Earth: its surface area is tiny, so although T⁴ is large, L = 4πσr²T⁴ is small.
    B1 radius comparable to Earth's; B1 small surface area outweighs high T in L = 4πσr²T⁴

  3. (c) 2500 K
    B1 T = 2.90 × 10⁻³/1.16 × 10⁻⁶ = 2500 K
  4. (d) Stars are not perfect black bodies / absorption in the star's atmosphere or by dust changes the spectrum / the peak is broad and hard to locate.
    B1

Q21Stellar radii (Wien's law, Stefan-Boltzmann) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A nearby bright star is 8.6 light-years from the Earth. The radiant flux intensity of its radiation at the Earth is 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ W m⁻² and the peak of its spectrum is at 290 nm. 1 light-year = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. Take the Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. Take the Wien's displacement law constant λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K.

  1. (a) Calculate the luminosity of the star. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the surface temperature of the star. [1]
  3. (c) Determine the radius of the star. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why this method gives only an estimate of the radius. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 9.15 × 10²⁷ W
    d = 8.6 × 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 8.14 × 10¹⁶ m (C1); L = 4πd²F = 9.15 × 10²⁷ W (A1)
  2. (b) 10000 K
    T = 2.9 × 10⁻³/290 × 10⁻⁹ = 10000 K (B1)
  3. (c) 1.13 × 10⁹ m
    r = √(L/(4πσT⁴)) (C1) = 1.13 × 10⁹ m (A1)
  4. (d) The star is not a perfect black body / the peak wavelength and the flux (affected by absorption) are hard to measure precisely.

  1. (a) Determine the factor by which the luminosity of the star changes. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the factor by which the peak wavelength of its spectrum changes, and describe the change in the star's colour. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how a star can become more luminous even though its surface becomes cooler. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 625 times greater
    M1 L ∝ r²T⁴; A1 100² × (½)⁴ = 10 000/16 = 625
  2. (b) λ_max doubles; the star appears redder.
    B1 λ_max ∝ 1/T, so ×2; B1 shifts towards red/infrared (redder)
  3. (c) The large increase in surface area (r² × 10 000) outweighs the decrease in T⁴ (÷16).

  4. (d) Explain how a standard candle can be used to find the distance to a galaxy containing it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The total power of electromagnetic radiation emitted by the star.
    B1
  2. (b) The radiant power passing normally through unit area at the Earth (W m⁻²).
    B1

    B1

Q24Standard candles, luminosity & radiant flux · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

The luminosity of the Sun is 3.83 × 10²⁶ W. Mars is 2.28 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun.

  1. (a) Calculate the radiant flux intensity of the Sun's radiation at Mars. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the radiant flux intensity decreases with the square of the distance from the Sun. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 586 W m⁻²
    F = L/(4πd²) (C1) = 3.83 × 10²⁶/(4π × (2.28 × 10¹¹)²) = 586 W m⁻² (A1)
  2. (b) The same power spreads out over the surface of a sphere of area 4πd², which increases as d²; so power per unit area ∝ 1/d².
    B1 power spread over sphere of area 4πd²; B1 hence F ∝ 1/d² (no absorption)

  3. (c) State which type is used to measure the greater distances, and why. [1]

  1. (a) Cepheid variable stars; Type Ia supernovae
    B1 each
  2. (b) Cepheids: luminosity is found from the period of their brightness variation (period–luminosity relation). Type Ia supernovae: all reach (almost) the same peak luminosity.
    B1 Cepheid period–luminosity; B1 Type Ia same peak luminosity
  3. (c) Type Ia supernovae, because they are much more luminous and visible from much further away.

Q26Standard candles, luminosity & radiant flux · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A Cepheid variable star in a nearby galaxy has a period which shows that its luminosity is 1.2 × 10³⁰ W. Its radiant flux intensity at the Earth is 3.5 × 10⁻¹³ W m⁻².

  1. (a) Explain why the period of a Cepheid variable star is measured. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the distance to the Cepheid, in m and in light-years. (1 light-year = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m) [3]
  3. (c) Interstellar dust between the Cepheid and the Earth absorbs some of its light. State and explain the effect on the calculated distance. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The period is related to its luminosity (period–luminosity relation), so measuring the period gives L.
    B1
  2. (b) 5.22 × 10²⁰ m = 55200 light-years
    C1 F = L/(4πd²); A1 d = √(1.2 × 10³⁰/(4π × 3.5 × 10⁻¹³)) = 5.22 × 10²⁰ m; A1 55200 light-years
  3. (c) The distance is overestimated: the measured flux is smaller than it would be, and d ∝ 1/√F.
    B1 calculated distance too large; B1 F reduced and d ∝ F^(−1/2)

Two stars A and B are of the same type and have the same luminosity. The radiant flux intensity of star A at the Earth is 25 times that of star B.

  1. (a) Determine the ratio (distance of star B)/(distance of star A). [2]
  2. (b) Star A is 40 pc from the Earth. State the distance of star B in pc and in metres. (1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m) [2]
  3. (c) State the assumption that allows these two stars to be compared in this way. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.0
    M1 same L, so F ∝ 1/d², d ∝ 1/√F; A1 √25 = 5.0
  2. (b) 200 pc = 6.18 × 10¹⁸ m
    B1 200 pc; B1 200 × 3.09 × 10¹⁶ = 6.18 × 10¹⁸ m
  3. (c) They have the same luminosity (they are standard candles of the same type), and no light is absorbed on the way.
    B1

The radiant flux intensity of the Sun's radiation just above the Earth's atmosphere is 1.36 × 10³ W m⁻². The Earth is 1.50 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun.

  1. (a) Calculate the luminosity of the Sun. [2]
  2. (b) Neptune is 4.50 × 10¹² m from the Sun. Calculate the radiant flux intensity of sunlight at Neptune. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why a space probe sent to Neptune does not use solar panels as its main power source. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.85 × 10²⁶ W
    L = 4πd²F (C1) = 4π × (1.50 × 10¹¹)² × 1.36 × 10³ = 3.85 × 10²⁶ W (A1)
  2. (b) 1.51 W m⁻²

Q29Standard candles, luminosity & radiant flux · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A Type Ia supernova reaches a peak luminosity of 2.0 × 10³⁶ W. The peak radiant flux intensity measured at the Earth is 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ W m⁻².

  1. (a) Calculate the distance to the supernova in Mpc. (1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m) [3]
  2. (b) Later observations show that 20 % of the light was absorbed by dust in the host galaxy. Determine the true distance in Mpc, and the percentage by which your answer to (a) was in error. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why Type Ia supernovae are used rather than Cepheids for galaxies at this distance. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.15 × 10²⁴ m = 167 Mpc
    C1 d = √(L/(4πF)); A1 5.15 × 10²⁴ m; A1 167 Mpc
  2. (b) true distance = 149 Mpc; (a) was 12 % too large
    C1 true F = 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵/0.80 = 7.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ W m⁻²; A1 d = 149 Mpc; A1 ratio = 1/√0.80 = 1.12, so 12 % too large
  3. (c) Type Ia supernovae are far more luminous, so they can still be detected (and measured) at distances where Cepheids are too faint.
    B1
Q30Standard candles, luminosity & radiant flux · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A Cepheid in the Milky Way, at a known distance of 2.5 kpc, has the same period as a Cepheid in a distant galaxy. Their radiant flux intensities at the Earth are 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ W m⁻² and 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁷ W m⁻² respectively.

  1. (a) Explain why the two Cepheids can be assumed to have the same luminosity. [1]
  2. (b) Determine the distance to the galaxy, in kpc and in Mpc. [3]
  3. (c) The distance to the nearby Cepheid is uncertain by ±4 %. State the percentage uncertainty in the distance to the galaxy, ignoring other uncertainties, and explain why accurate calibration of nearby standard candles is so important. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cepheids with the same period have the same luminosity (period–luminosity relation).
    B1
  2. (b) 3160 kpc = 3.16 Mpc
    C1 same L so F ∝ 1/d²; C1 d_far = 2.5 × √(4.0 × 10⁻¹¹/2.5 × 10⁻¹⁷); A1 3160 kpc = 3.16 Mpc
  3. (c) ±4 %; every distance found using that type of standard candle (and hence H₀) depends on the calibration, so its error carries through to all of them.
    B1 ±4 % (d_far ∝ d_near); B1 calibration error propagates to all distances measured this way
