Q1Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In the α-particle scattering experiment, a thin gold foil is bombarded with α-particles. Which observation shows that most of the volume of an atom is empty space?A A very small fraction of the α-particles are deflected through more than 90°.B Most of the α-particles pass through the foil with little or no deflection.C Some α-particles are absorbed by the gold foil.D The α-particles that are deflected all move away from the nucleus. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B Most of the α-particles pass through the foil with little or no deflection.
    Almost all α-particles pass straight through, so most of the atom offers no obstruction: it is mostly empty space. A shows that the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus, not that the atom is mostly empty.
Q2Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A nucleus of uranium-238, ²³⁸₉₂U, emits one α-particle and then two β⁻ particles. Which nuclide is formed?A ²³⁴₉₂UB ²³⁴₉₀ThC ²³⁰₉₀ThD ²³⁴₉₄Pu [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A ²³⁴₉₂U
    α: A − 4, Z − 2 gives ²³⁴₉₀; each β⁻: Z + 1, A unchanged, so ²³⁴₉₂ (an isotope of uranium). ²³⁴₉₀Th is the nuclide after the α only.
Q3Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In the α-particle scattering experiment, α-particles from a source are directed at a very thin gold foil in a vacuum. A detector is moved around the foil to count the α-particles scattered through different angles.

  1. (a) State two observations from the experiment and, for each, the conclusion drawn about the structure of the atom. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the foil used must be very thin. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most α-particles pass straight through → the atom is mostly empty space. A very small number are deflected through large angles (> 90°) → there is a tiny, dense nucleus that is positively charged and contains most of the mass.
    B1 most undeflected; B1 mostly empty space / nucleus very small compared with the atom; B1 very few deflected through large angles; B1 nucleus is positive (repels α) and contains most of the mass (concentrated)
  2. (b) So that each α-particle is scattered by at most one nucleus (and is not absorbed).
    B1 α-particles have a short range / single scattering events only
Q4Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) For a neutral atom of the nuclide ⁵⁹₂₆Fe, state the number of (i) protons, (ii) neutrons, (iii) electrons. [2]
  2. (b) Iron-56 is another isotope of iron. Explain what is meant by isotopes. [1]
  3. (c) Iron-59 decays by β⁻ emission to form an isotope of cobalt (Co). Write a nuclear equation for the decay. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 26 (ii) 33 (iii) 26
    B1 26 protons and 26 electrons; B1 59 − 26 = 33 neutrons
  2. (b) Nuclei of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
    B1 same proton number, different nucleon (neutron) number
  3. (c) ⁵⁹₂₆Fe → ⁵⁹₂₇Co + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄
    B1 correct cobalt nuclide ⁵⁹₂₇Co; B1 β⁻ particle and antineutrino included with correct numbers
Q5Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A radioactive source emits α-particles, β⁻ particles and γ-radiation.

  1. (a) State the charge of each type of radiation in terms of e. [2]
  2. (b) State which type of radiation (i) is the most strongly ionising, (ii) travels at the speed of light, (iii) has a mass of about 4 u. [3]
  3. (c) State the type of radiation that is not deflected by an electric field and explain why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) α: +2e; β⁻: −e; γ: 0
    B2 all three correct (B1 for two)
  2. (b) (i) α (ii) γ (iii) α
    B1 each
  3. (c) γ-radiation, because it is uncharged (electromagnetic radiation).
    B1
Q6Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Write a nuclear equation for the β⁻ decay of cobalt-60 to nickel-60. Cobalt has proton number 27. [2]
  2. (b) The nickel nucleus formed is in an excited state and then emits a γ-ray photon. State the effect of this emission on the proton number and nucleon number of the nucleus, and give a reason. [2]
  3. (c) Cobalt-60 is used in hospitals to treat cancer. Suggest why the γ-radiation, not the β⁻ radiation, is used for treating a tumour deep inside the body. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) ⁶⁰₂₇Co → ⁶⁰₂₈Ni + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄
    B1 ⁶⁰₂₈Ni; B1 electron and antineutrino
  2. (b) Neither changes: a γ-photon has no charge and no mass; the nucleus only loses energy.
    B1 no change to A or Z; B1 γ has no charge/mass, only energy is lost
  3. (c) γ-radiation is much more penetrating and can reach the tumour; β⁻ is absorbed by a few mm of tissue.
    B1
Q7Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) The β⁻ particles emitted by a radioactive source have a continuous range of kinetic energies up to a maximum value. What is the reason for this?A The β⁻ particles lose different amounts of energy as they leave the source.B The nuclei emitting the β⁻ particles have different amounts of energy.C The energy released in each decay is shared between the β⁻ particle and an antineutrino.D The energy released in each decay is shared between the β⁻ particle and a γ-ray photon. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C The energy released in each decay is shared between the β⁻ particle and an antineutrino.
    Each decay releases the same energy, but it is shared in varying proportions with an (electron) antineutrino, so the β⁻ energy varies continuously. A γ-photon (D) has a fixed energy and would not produce a continuous spectrum.
Q8Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A nucleus decays by β⁺ emission. What happens to its proton number Z and its nucleon number A?A Z decreases by 1; A is unchangedB Z increases by 1; A is unchangedC Z decreases by 1; A decreases by 1D Z is unchanged; A decreases by 1 [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Z decreases by 1; A is unchanged
    In β⁺ decay a proton changes into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino: Z falls by 1, the number of nucleons stays the same. B describes β⁻ decay.

Radon-222 is a radioactive gas that can collect in poorly ventilated basements. It decays through a series of short-lived nuclides.

  1. (a) Identify, with its proton number and nucleon number, each of the nuclides X, Y and Z in the chain below. Polonium is Po (84), lead is Pb (82) and bismuth is Bi (83).²²²₈₆Rn → (α) → X → (α) → Y → (β⁻) → Z [3]
  2. (b) Z then decays by β⁻ emission. State the proton number and nucleon number of the product, and name the element. [2]
  3. (c) Write the equation for the β⁻ decay of Y, including all the particles emitted. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) X = ²¹⁸₈₄Po; Y = ²¹⁴₈₂Pb; Z = ²¹⁴₈₃Bi
    B1 each nuclide (A and Z both correct)
  2. (b) Z = 84, A = 214: polonium-214
    B1 84 and 214; B1 polonium
  3. (c) ²¹⁴₈₂Pb → ²¹⁴₈₃Bi + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄
    B1 nuclides; B1 electron and electron antineutrino
Q10Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Smoke detectors contain americium-241, which emits α-particles. Each α-particle is emitted with a kinetic energy of about 5.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Convert 5.5 MeV into joules. (1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) [1]
  2. (b) The mass of an α-particle is 4.0 u, where 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Calculate the initial speed of an α-particle. [3]
  3. (c) State how the α-particle energies from americium-241 differ from the β⁻ energies from a β⁻ source. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8.8 × 10⁻¹³ J
    5.5 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.8 × 10⁻¹³ J (B1)
  2. (b) 1.6 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
    m = 4.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (C1); ½mv² = 8.8 × 10⁻¹³ (C1); v = 1.6 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) α-particles from one decay have discrete (single) energies; β⁻ energies have a continuous range.
    B1
Q11Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A student places different absorbers between a radioactive source and a Geiger–Müller tube and measures the count rate. The background count rate is 30 counts per minute.absorber: none — count rate 520 min⁻¹absorber: sheet of paper — 516 min⁻¹absorber: 3 mm aluminium — 216 min⁻¹absorber: 2.5 cm lead — 94 min⁻¹

  1. (a) Calculate the corrected count rate for each absorber. [2]
  2. (b) Deduce, with reasons, which types of radiation the source emits. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) none: 490; paper: 486; aluminium: 186; lead: 64 counts per minute
    C1 subtract background 30 min⁻¹; A1 all four values correct
  2. (b) No α: paper causes no significant drop. β present: 3 mm aluminium causes a large drop. γ present: the count through 2.5 cm of lead is still well above background.
    B1 no α with reason; B1 β with reason; B1 γ with reason
Q12Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon (proton number 6). Carbon-14 is used to date ancient wooden objects such as those found at Mohenjo-daro. Take 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and treat the mass of each nucleus as A × u.

  1. (a) Calculate the specific charge (charge/mass) of a carbon-12 nucleus. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the specific charge of a carbon-14 nucleus. [1]
  3. (c) Explain, in terms of the particles in each nucleus, why the specific charges differ. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.82 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
    q = 6 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 9.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 12 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁶ kg (C1); q/m = 4.82 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹ (A1)
  2. (b) 4.13 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
    9.60 × 10⁻¹⁹/(14 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.13 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹ (B1)
  3. (c) Both have 6 protons (same charge) but carbon-14 has 8 neutrons rather than 6, so a greater mass and a smaller charge/mass.
    B1 same number of protons so same charge; B1 more neutrons so greater mass
Q13Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Thorium-232, ²³²₉₀Th, decays through a series of α and β⁻ emissions to the stable nuclide lead-208, ²⁰⁸₈₂Pb.

  1. (a) Determine the number of α-particles emitted in the series. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the number of β⁻ particles emitted in the series. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the order in which the decays happen does not affect your answers. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6
    M1 nucleon number falls by 232 − 208 = 24, only α changes A; A1 24/4 = 6
  2. (b) 4
    M1 6 α-decays reduce Z by 12, to 90 − 12 = 78; A1 Z must rise to 82, so 4 β⁻ decays
  3. (c) Changes in A and Z simply add up: each α always changes A by −4 and Z by −2, each β⁻ changes Z by +1.

A gold-197 nucleus has a radius of about 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. A gold atom has a radius of about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

  1. (a) Calculate the mass of a gold-197 nucleus in kg. (1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the density of the gold nucleus. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the ratio (volume of the atom)/(volume of the nucleus). [1]
  4. (d) The density of solid gold is about 1.9 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³. Use your answers to explain what this tells you about the structure of the atom. [2]
Show answer

    197 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg (B1)
  2. (b) 2.28 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³

Q15Atoms, nuclei & radiation (alpha/beta/gamma, decay equations) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Phosphorus-32 decays by β⁻ emission to sulfur-32. The total energy released in each decay is 1.71 MeV.

  1. (a) An electron emitted by one phosphorus-32 nucleus has a kinetic energy of 0.65 MeV. Determine the energy carried away by the antineutrino. Assume the recoil energy of the nucleus is negligible. [2]
  2. (b) Describe the shape of the energy spectrum of the β⁻ particles and explain why it has this shape. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why α-particles emitted by one nuclide all have the same energy (discrete energy). [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.06 MeV
    total energy released is the same for every decay = 1.71 MeV (C1); 1.71 − 0.65 = 1.06 MeV (A1)
  2. (b) A continuous range of energies from (almost) zero up to 1.71 MeV, with most electrons having an intermediate energy; the energy released is shared in different proportions between the electron and the antineutrino.
    B1 continuous range from 0 to maximum 1.71 MeV; B1 a fixed energy is released in each decay; B1 shared randomly with the antineutrino
  3. (c) Only two particles are produced (α and daughter nucleus), so momentum and energy conservation fix the share: no third particle.
    B1

  2. (b) A meson is one quark and one antiquark; the largest possible charge is +⅔ + ⅓ = +1 (e.g. u d̄), so +2e is impossible.

Q30Fundamental particles (quarks, leptons, hadrons) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Sodium-24 (proton number 11) decays by β⁻ emission to magnesium-24 (proton number 12). It is used to trace leaks in underground water pipes.

  1. (a) Determine the total numbers of up quarks and down quarks in a sodium-24 nucleus. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the numbers of up quarks and down quarks in the magnesium-24 nucleus formed. [1]
  3. (c) Use your answers to explain why the nucleon number does not change in β⁻ decay but the proton number increases by one. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 35 up quarks and 37 down quarks
    M1 11 protons (uud) and 13 neutrons (udd); A1 up = 2 × 11 + 13 = 35, down = 11 + 2 × 13 = 37
  2. (b) 36 up quarks and 36 down quarks
    B1 12 protons and 12 neutrons: 36 and 36
  3. (c) The total number of quarks stays 72 (so 24 nucleons); one down quark becomes an up quark, turning one neutron (udd) into a proton (uud).
    B1 total quarks unchanged → nucleon number unchanged; B1 one d → u, so one neutron becomes a proton
