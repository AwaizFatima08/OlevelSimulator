Q1Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Core · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe the scene at a busy railway station in a large city just before dawn and then at the height of the morning rush hour. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Structure: 1) the empty, quiet station before dawn; 2) first signs of life (stalls opening, first passengers); 3) the rush hour at its height; 4) a closing image that links back to the opening (e.g. the pigeons now scattered by the noise).Planning ideas: Before dawn (any three): a sweeper pushing a broom along an empty platform; a single tea stall hissing as the kettle boils; the hum of strip lights; a guard yawning; pigeons on the rafters; cold air and the smell of diesel. Rush hour (any three): crowds pressing towards the doors; porters in red shirts shouting; announcements echoing; the smell of fried snacks and sweat; trains screeching in; vendors calling prices.Example paragraph: 'At four o'clock the station belongs to the pigeons. They shuffle along the iron beams above platform three while, below them, a lone sweeper drags his broom in slow arcs, raising small clouds of dust that glitter under the strip lights. The only sound, apart from the buzz of the lamps, is the tea-seller's kettle beginning its thin whistle. The air smells of cold metal and yesterday's diesel.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear structure that moves through time or space; the two settings/moments are clearly contrasted; precise, focused detail rather than a list; a sense of atmosphere that changes; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = wide, precise vocabulary; varied sentence lengths and openings used for effect; imagery used sparingly and freshly (not clichés); accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; present or past tense kept consistent; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.Avoid turning the description into a story with a plot.
Q2Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Core · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe the scene in an examination hall in the ten minutes before an important exam begins and during the final ten minutes of the exam. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Structure in time: entering the hall; the paper handed out; (brief link through the middle of the exam); the final ten minutes; time called. Keep the focus on the scene and people, not on a story about one candidate's result.Planning ideas: Before: students whispering final facts; the scrape of chairs as candidates find their seats; an invigilator placing papers face down; nervous tapping of pens. Final ten minutes: frantic scribbling; a student staring at the clock; someone shaking a cramped hand; the invigilator's footsteps; a sigh as a pen is put down.Example paragraph: '"Stop writing, please." The words fall into the silence like a stone into a well. Pens freeze in mid-air. Somewhere behind me a chair creaks, and a long breath escapes from a hundred chests at once.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear structure that moves through time or space; the two settings/moments are clearly contrasted; precise, focused detail rather than a list; a sense of atmosphere that changes; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = wide, precise vocabulary; varied sentence lengths and openings used for effect; imagery used sparingly and freshly (not clichés); accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; present or past tense kept consistent; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.Avoid turning the description into a story with a plot.
Q3Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe a beach on a calm summer morning and then the same beach after a violent storm. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: calm morning (gentle waves, joggers, camel rides, warm sand, gulls); signs of change; the beach after the storm (debris, churned sand, darkened sky clearing, people returning to look). Ending could show the first sign of recovery.Planning ideas: Divide the piece into two halves of roughly equal length; use a linking paragraph (the storm arriving or its aftermath being discovered); repeat one feature (e.g. a fishing boat, a beach café, a line of footprints) in both halves to show how it has changed; change the sentence rhythm from long, smooth sentences to short, broken ones.Example paragraph: an upturned wooden boat with a split hull; a café sign snapped in half; seaweed draped over a lamp post; a child's sandal half-buried in grey sand. Model line: 'The ice-cream kiosk lies on its side like a tired animal, its striped awning ripped into ribbons that twitch in the wind.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear structure that moves through time or space; the two settings/moments are clearly contrasted; precise, focused detail rather than a list; a sense of atmosphere that changes; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = wide, precise vocabulary; varied sentence lengths and openings used for effect; imagery used sparingly and freshly (not clichés); accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; present or past tense kept consistent; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.Avoid turning the description into a story with a plot.
Q4Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe a night market in a foreign city you have visited or imagined, as it opens in the early evening and as it closes after midnight. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: preparation and opening (setting up, first customers, colours); peak crowds (briefly); closing (stalls folding, litter, cats, quiet). Sense of a foreign place: unfamiliar foods, languages, currency. Avoid copying a tourist brochure; select vivid particulars.Planning ideas: Sound: 'the clatter of metal shutters rolling up'. Smell: 'smoke from charcoal grills carrying the scent of lemongrass'. Light: 'strings of bulbs flickering on one by one above the stalls'.Example paragraph: 'The noodle seller wipes his last pan with a grey cloth, slowly, as if the movement belongs to someone else. His apron, crisp at seven, is now patterned with oil and chilli. He counts coins into a tin box, lips moving, then lowers the gas flame until the blue ring vanishes with a soft pop.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear structure that moves through time or space; the two settings/moments are clearly contrasted; precise, focused detail rather than a list; a sense of atmosphere that changes; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = wide, precise vocabulary; varied sentence lengths and openings used for effect; imagery used sparingly and freshly (not clichés); accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; present or past tense kept consistent; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.Avoid turning the description into a story with a plot.
Q5Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe a family home during the busy days of preparation for a wedding and then on the evening of the wedding itself. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: preparations (cooking, mehndi, tailors, relatives arriving, noise, lists, lights going up); the evening (music, colours, clothes, food, emotions of the bride's family); a final quieter moment. Challenge: keep it descriptive, convey mixed emotions (joy, tiredness, sadness at a daughter leaving) through detail rather than statement.Planning ideas: e.g. the front gate: first hung with a half-tied string of marigolds and blocked by a delivery van of chairs; on the evening, glowing with fairy lights and crowded with guests' shoes. Or a grandmother's charpai in the courtyard: buried under fabric samples, then empty and quiet as she sits watching the celebration.Example paragraph: 'Nobody in the house has sat down since Tuesday. The courtyard is a sea of folded chairs, cardboard boxes and rolls of golden ribbon, and my aunt stands in the middle of it, phone at one ear, giving orders to three people at once.'

Q6Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe the scene in a hospital waiting area late at night. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Structure could move around the room (entrance, rows of seats, reception desk, the doors to the wards) or through time (midnight to dawn). Show emotion through people's gestures. End on a quiet, resonant image such as the first grey light at the window.Planning ideas: Focus on small, telling details of different people waiting (a father clutching car keys, a child asleep across two chairs); use sound and light (the hum of a vending machine, a flickering tube light, the squeak of a nurse's shoes); slow the pace with long sentences broken by sudden short ones when a name is called.Example paragraph: 'The loudest thing in the room is the clock. Each tick lands on the silence like a drop of water on a tin roof. Beneath it runs the low murmur of the drinks machine, and every few minutes the double doors sigh open, letting through the rattle of a trolley and a voice calling a name that nobody here answers to.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear structure that moves through time or space; the two settings/moments are clearly contrasted; precise, focused detail rather than a list; a sense of atmosphere that changes; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = wide, precise vocabulary; varied sentence lengths and openings used for effect; imagery used sparingly and freshly (not clichés); accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; present or past tense kept consistent; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.Avoid turning the description into a story with a plot.
Q7Descriptive (scene, place or event) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Describe a mountain valley in two different seasons. (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your composition on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Structure: season one (wide view, then close detail); transition; season two (same features changed: the river, the fields, the people, the light); final image that unites both. Challenge: avoid clichés such as 'winter wonderland'; use precise colour and sound vocabulary.Planning ideas: Spring (e.g. in a northern valley): apricot blossom like pale smoke on the slopes; meltwater roaring in the river; farmers ploughing terraced fields; the smell of wet earth. Winter: snow muffling every sound; frozen waterfall; smoke rising from chimneys; villagers wrapped in woollen shawls; footprints on a white path.Example paragraph: 'By October the blossoms are a memory. The poplars turn gold, then bare, and one morning the first snow drifts down so quietly that the village wakes up to find the world has been rewritten in white.'

Q8Narrative (given sentence) · Core · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Write a story which includes the sentence: 'The door had been locked from the inside.' (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your story on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content points: the sentence must be included word for word; it should be the turning point, not dropped in randomly; a believable explanation or outcome; a satisfying ending.Planning ideas: Example: 1) A boy visits his grandfather's flat after school and gets no answer. 2) Neighbours gather; worry grows. 3) The caretaker tries the key and finds the door won't open: 'The door had been locked from the inside.' 4) They climb in through the balcony and find the grandfather asleep with headphones on, after a fall that has hurt his ankle. 5) Relief; the boy decides to visit every day.Example paragraph: 'Mr Akram turned the key once, twice. It moved, but the door stayed shut, as solid as a wall. He bent down and peered at the gap beneath it. "The bolt," he said quietly. The door had been locked from the inside.'
    Content and structure (10 marks): 9–10 = a clear, well-paced plot with a beginning that hooks, a developed middle with tension, and a controlled ending; a small cast of convincing characters; events that are believable inside the story; 7–8 = relevant and well organised, with mostly precise detail and some development; 5–6 = relevant but uneven: some detail is general or listed, structure is simple; 3–4 = thin or partly off the task, weak structure; 1–2 = little relevant content.Style and accuracy (15 marks): 13–15 = varied sentences used for pace (short ones at moments of tension); precise verbs and description; dialogue used sparingly and correctly punctuated; consistent tense and viewpoint; very few errors; 10–12 = fluent and mostly accurate, some range of vocabulary and sentence types; 7–9 = generally clear, plain vocabulary, errors that do not obscure meaning; 4–6 = frequent errors, limited vocabulary and sentence control; 1–3 = errors often obscure meaning.
Q9Narrative (given sentence) · Core · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Write a story which includes the sentence: 'It was only a small mistake, but it changed everything.' (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your story on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: ordinary situation; the mistake; growing consequences; the sentence used as a reflection (often near the start or the end); resolution showing how life changed.Planning ideas: e.g. sending a message to the wrong group chat; catching the wrong bus; writing down a phone number with two digits reversed; leaving a gate open on a farm. Reason: e.g. the wrong bus takes the character somewhere new where they meet someone important, giving room for development.Example paragraph: 'I was in such a hurry that I didn't look at the number on the front of the bus. Seventeen, forty-seven — what was the difference? I found a seat by the window, plugged in my earphones and closed my eyes.'

Q10Narrative (given sentence) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Write a story which includes the sentence: 'Nobody believed me until the lights went out.' (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your story on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: narrator notices a danger or strange event; is ignored; the lights going out proves them right; resolution (narrator helps, is thanked or learns something). Avoid an ending such as 'it was all a dream'.Planning ideas: Any reasoned choice. E.g. at the start as a hook that makes the reader ask what the narrator warned about; or at the climax, after the narrator's warnings are ignored, so that it marks the moment the danger becomes real.Example paragraph: 'Nobody believed me until the lights went out. For three days I had told them about the smell of burning near the fuse box in the school hall, and for three days they had laughed. Then, in the middle of the prize-giving, with three hundred parents in their best clothes, the hall went black.'

Q14Narrative (given sentence) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 25 marks

Composition title: Write a story which includes the sentence: 'For the first time in years, the old house was silent.' (350–450 words)

  1. (a) Write your story on the title above. Write 350 to 450 words. [25]
Show answer
  1. (a) Content: the house and family; changes over time (time-shifts handled clearly); a closing scene with the sentence. Challenge: managing a long time span in 450 words, using selected scenes rather than summary.Planning ideas: e.g. a large, noisy joint family lives in an old house; over the years the children move abroad or to other cities; the grandmother stays on alone; the story ends on the day the house is sold and she leaves: 'For the first time in years, the old house was silent.'
