Q1Continuous summary · Core · Paper 1 style · 20 marks

Read Text B, then answer the question.Text B: Gardens in the skyIn crowded cities such as Karachi, flat concrete roofs are usually used for nothing more than drying laundry and storing old furniture. A growing number of families, however, are turning them into gardens, and the results are surprising.The most obvious reward is food. A few large pots and old plastic drums can produce tomatoes, chillies, mint and spinach for much of the year, which saves families money at a time when vegetable prices keep rising. Because the vegetables travel only a few metres from soil to kitchen, they are fresher than anything sold in the market, and families know exactly what has – and has not – been sprayed on them.Rooftop plants also make homes more comfortable. A layer of soil and leaves shades the concrete, so the rooms underneath stay noticeably cooler in the fierce summer months, and families run their fans and air-conditioners less. Across a whole neighbourhood, green roofs can help reduce the 'heat island' effect that makes cities hotter than the countryside around them.There are benefits for wildlife too. Butterflies, bees and small birds, which have almost disappeared from many built-up areas, quickly find rooftop flowers. Some gardeners collect rainwater in tanks, which reduces the amount of water rushing into the overloaded drains during monsoon storms.Finally, many rooftop gardeners talk about how the work makes them feel. Spending half an hour watering and weeding in the cool of the evening, away from the noise of the street, is calming. Children who help learn where food comes from and are more willing to eat vegetables they have grown themselves. Neighbours swap seeds and advice, and friendships form across the low walls between roofs.

  1. (a) Summarise the benefits of rooftop gardens, according to Text B.You must use continuous writing (not note form). Use your own words as far as possible.Your summary should be no more than 150 words. [20]

Children learn where food comes from / eat more vegetables12. Builds friendships among neighbours

Read Text B, then answer the question.Text B: Never too late to learnMany adults believe that learning a new language is something only children can do. It is true that young children pick up the sounds of a language easily, but research and experience show that adults gain a great deal from learning a second or third language – and often learn faster at first, because they know how to study.The most practical advantage is at work. Employers in tourism, trade, health care and technology value staff who can speak to customers in their own language, and bilingual employees are often considered for promotion or work abroad. Travel becomes richer too: instead of relying on guides and signs, a traveller who speaks even a little of the local language can ask for directions, bargain in markets and make friends.Learning a language also exercises the brain. Remembering new words and switching between grammar systems improves concentration and memory, and some studies suggest that people who speak more than one language may keep their minds sharper as they grow older. Adult learners often say that studying a new language helps them understand their own language better, as they notice how sentences are built.There are social and emotional rewards as well. Families separated by migration find that learning a relative's language brings them closer, especially to grandparents and cousins abroad. Reading books or watching films in the original language opens a window on another culture and its way of thinking, which can make people more tolerant. Finally, there is the simple pleasure of achievement: the first time a learner holds a full conversation in a new language is a moment of real pride.

Read Text B, then answer the question.Text B: The city that switched to bicyclesFifty years ago, the streets of many northern European cities were as jammed with cars as those of any city today. Now, in some of them, more people travel to work by bicycle than by car. How did this change happen?Part of the answer is that city governments made cycling safe. They built wide cycle lanes separated from traffic by kerbs, so that riders no longer had to compete with lorries and buses. Traffic lights were timed to give cyclists a head start at junctions, and speed limits for cars in residential streets were lowered. Parents who once feared for their children now let them cycle to school.Cycling also became convenient. Huge bicycle parks were built beside railway stations, allowing people to cycle to the train and continue their journey. Shops, offices and schools were required to provide racks. In winter, cycle paths are cleared of snow before the roads are.Money played a part too. Petrol prices rose sharply in the 1970s, and parking in the city centre became expensive and difficult, while a bicycle costs almost nothing to run. Many companies give employees an allowance for every kilometre they cycle to work.Finally, attitudes changed. Cycling came to be seen as healthy and modern rather than old-fashioned. Doctors pointed out that regular cycling strengthens the heart and helps people control their weight. Commuters discovered that they arrived at work more alert, and that in the rush hour a bicycle was often faster than a car. Today, government ministers and business leaders are photographed cycling to work, and children grow up thinking of the bicycle as the normal way to get around.

Read Text B, then answer the question.Text B: Waste not, want notAround a third of all the food produced in the world is never eaten. Some is lost on farms and during transport, but a surprising amount is thrown away in our own kitchens. The good news is that households can make a big difference with a few simple habits.The first step happens before shopping. Checking the fridge and cupboards and writing a list helps families buy only what they need. Planning meals for the week makes it less likely that vegetables will be forgotten at the back of the fridge until they rot. Shoppers can also resist special offers such as 'buy two, get one free' unless they are sure they will use the extra item.Storage matters too. Keeping the fridge at the right temperature, storing bread in a cool dry place and putting older food at the front where it will be seen all help food last longer. Many fruits and cooked dishes can be frozen before they spoil.In the kitchen, cooks can measure rice and lentils carefully rather than guessing, so that less food is left over. Leftovers can become tomorrow's lunch: yesterday's rice can be fried with vegetables, and stale bread can be turned into a sweet dish. Peels and scraps that cannot be eaten can be composted for the garden.Finally, families should understand date labels. A 'best before' date is about quality, not safety; food is often perfectly good after it. Food that is still good but will not be eaten can be shared with neighbours or given to organisations that pass it on to people in need.
