Q1Radioactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the nature of (i) an alpha particle, (ii) a beta particle, (iii) gamma radiation. [3]
  2. (b) State which of the three radiations is (i) the most ionising, (ii) the most penetrating. [2]
  3. (c) State what is needed to stop most beta particles. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) a helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons) (ii) a high-speed electron (from the nucleus) (iii) an electromagnetic wave
    B1 each
  2. (b) (i) alpha (ii) gamma
    B1 each
  3. (c) A few millimetres of aluminium
    B1
Q2Radioactivity · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which type of radiation is stopped by a single sheet of paper?A alpha onlyB beta onlyC gamma onlyD beta and gamma [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A alpha only
    Alpha particles are highly ionising and have a very short range; paper absorbs them. Beta (B) needs a few mm of aluminium.
Q3Radioactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by background radiation. [1]
  2. (b) Give two natural sources and one man-made source of background radiation. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why the background count is higher in some regions than in others. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ionising radiation that is always present in the environment, from natural and artificial sources
    B1
  2. (b) Natural: radon gas from rocks/soil; rocks and building materials; cosmic rays; food and drink. Man-made: medical X-rays / treatments; nuclear power / fallout from weapons tests
    B1 each, two natural and one man-made
  3. (c) Different rocks (e.g. granite) contain more radioactive isotopes / more radon; higher altitude has more cosmic rays
    B1
Q4Radioactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State three precautions a technician should take when handling a radioactive source in a school laboratory. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why increasing the distance from a source reduces the risk. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why ionising radiation is harmful to living cells. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Use long-handled tongs; keep the source pointed away from people; minimise the time it is out; store it in a lead-lined box; wash hands after
    1 mark each for any three
  2. (b) Radiation spreads out and is absorbed by air, so less reaches the person (alpha and beta have short ranges in air)
    B1
  3. (c) It ionises atoms in cells, damaging molecules such as DNA; this can kill cells or cause mutations / cancer
    B1 ionisation of molecules in cells; B1 cell death or mutation / cancer
Q5Radioactivity · Core · Paper 3 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) A student uses a Geiger-Müller tube and counter with no source present. The counter records 30 counts in 2.0 minutes. Calculate the background count rate in counts/min. [1]
  2. (b) A source is placed in front of the tube. The count rate is now 465 counts/min. Calculate the count rate due to the source alone. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why the student measures the background count for several minutes rather than a few seconds. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15 counts/min
    30 ÷ 2.0 = 15 B1
  2. (b) 450 counts/min
    465 − 15 = 450 B1
  3. (c) Decay is random, so a longer count gives a more reliable average
    B1
Q6Radioactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Spent fuel from a nuclear power station contains radioactive isotopes. One of them, caesium-137, has a half-life of 30 years. Calculate how long it takes for its activity to fall to 1/32 of its starting value. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two precautions taken when storing such waste. [2]
  3. (c) Give one advantage of nuclear power stations compared with gas-fired power stations. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 150 years
    1/32 = (½)⁵, so 5 half-lives M1; 5 × 30 = 150 years A1
  2. (b) Sealed in thick steel/concrete or glass containers; stored deep underground or in shielded, secure sites away from water supplies; monitored
    1 mark each for any two
  3. (c) No carbon dioxide emitted while generating / large output from small fuel mass / reliable
    B1
Q7Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define the half-life of a radioactive isotope. [1]
  2. (b) A sample has an activity of 1600 Bq. Its half-life is 6 hours. Calculate its activity after 24 hours. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate how long it takes for the activity to fall to 200 Bq. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The time taken for half the nuclei of the isotope in a sample to decay (or for the activity to halve)
    B1
  2. (b) 100 Bq
    24 h = 4 half-lives M1; 1600 → 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 Bq A1
  3. (c) 18 hours
    3 half-lives × 6 = 18 h B1
Q8Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Radium-226, ²²⁶₈₈Ra, decays by emitting an alpha particle to form radon (Rn). Write the decay equation, showing the nucleon and proton numbers of the radon nucleus. [3]
  2. (b) Radon is a gas. Explain why breathing in radon is more dangerous than being near a sealed source of radium outside the body. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He (or α)
    B1 alpha as ⁴₂He / ⁴₂α; B1 222; B1 86
  2. (b) Alpha particles cannot penetrate skin, but inside the lungs they are emitted next to living cells; they are strongly ionising and damage cells / DNA
    B1 alpha absorbed by dead skin layer outside body; B1 inside, strong ionisation of nearby cells
Q9Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Carbon-14, ¹⁴₆C, decays by beta emission to nitrogen. Write the decay equation. [3]
  2. (b) Describe the change that takes place inside the nucleus during beta decay. [2]
  3. (c) State why the nucleon number does not change in beta decay. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e (or β)
    B1 beta as ⁰₋₁e; B1 nucleon number 14; B1 proton number 7
  2. (b) A neutron changes into a proton and an electron; the electron is emitted at high speed as the beta particle
    B1 neutron → proton; B1 + electron emitted
  3. (c) A neutron is replaced by a proton, so the total number of nucleons stays the same
    B1
Q10Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A nucleus emits an alpha particle. What happens to its proton number and its nucleon number?A proton number −2, nucleon number −4B proton number −4, nucleon number −2C proton number +1, nucleon number 0D proton number 0, nucleon number −4 [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A proton number −2, nucleon number −4
    An alpha particle contains 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so Z falls by 2 and A falls by 4. C describes beta decay.
Q11Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Technetium-99m is used as a medical tracer. It emits gamma radiation and has a half-life of 6.0 hours. Explain why a gamma emitter is chosen for a tracer that is injected into a patient. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a half-life of a few hours is suitable. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the fraction of the technetium-99m remaining 24 hours after injection. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Gamma passes out of the body so it can be detected outside; it is weakly ionising so causes less damage
    B1 penetrates tissue to reach the detector; B1 least ionising
  2. (b) Long enough for the scan to be done; short enough that the activity soon falls, limiting the patient's dose
    B1 long enough to detect; B1 decays quickly afterwards
  3. (c) 1/16
    24 ÷ 6.0 = 4 half-lives M1; (½)⁴ = 1/16 A1
Q12Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which statement about radioactive decay is correct?A The decay of a nucleus is random and cannot be predicted.B Heating a sample makes its nuclei decay faster.C It is possible to predict exactly when a particular nucleus will decay.D Increasing the pressure on a sample increases its half-life. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A The decay of a nucleus is random and cannot be predicted.
    Decay is random and spontaneous: it is not affected by temperature or pressure, and we can only predict what fraction of a large sample will decay. B is wrong: half-life is unaffected by physical conditions.
Q13Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A smoke detector contains a small source of americium-241, an alpha emitter. Alpha particles ionise the air between two plates, so a small current flows. Explain how smoke entering the detector sets off the alarm. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why an alpha source is suitable for use in homes. [2]
  3. (c) Americium-241 has a half-life of about 430 years. Explain why this is an advantage. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Smoke particles absorb the alpha particles, so less air is ionised; the current falls and the circuit triggers the alarm
    B1 smoke absorbs alpha / reduces ionisation; B1 current falls, which triggers the alarm
  2. (b) Alpha is strongly ionising, so a small source gives a measurable current; alpha has a very short range and is stopped by the casing, so it is not a hazard outside the detector
    B1 strongly ionising; B1 short range / stopped by casing
  3. (c) The activity stays almost constant for the life of the detector, so it does not need replacing
    B1
Q14Radioactivity · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A Geiger-Müller tube is placed a short distance from a radioactive source. Different absorbers are placed between them. The background count rate is 20 counts/min.No absorber: 520 counts/minThin paper: 515 counts/min3 mm aluminium: 21 counts/min

  1. (a) State the evidence that the source does not emit alpha particles. [1]
  2. (b) State the evidence that the source emits beta particles. [1]
  3. (c) State and explain whether the source emits gamma radiation. [2]
  4. (d) Calculate the count rate due to the source when there is no absorber. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Paper hardly reduces the count rate (520 → 515)
    B1
  2. (b) 3 mm of aluminium reduces the count rate greatly, but paper does not (515 → 21)
    B1
  3. (c) No: with 3 mm of aluminium the count rate (21 counts/min) is the same as the background (20 counts/min), so no radiation from the source passes through the aluminium
    B1 no gamma; B1 aluminium reading equals background, but gamma would pass through 3 mm of aluminium almost unabsorbed
  4. (d) 500 counts/min
    520 − 20 = 500 B1

Q16Radioactivity · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A student measures the count rate from a radioactive source with a Geiger-Müller tube and counter. The background count rate is 20 counts/min.Time / min: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10Measured count rate / counts per min: 820, 586, 420, 303, 220, 161

  1. (a) Explain why the background count rate must be subtracted from each reading. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the corrected count rate at 4.0 min and at 8.0 min. [2]
  3. (c) Use your answers to find the half-life of the source. [1]
  4. (d) Predict the count rate the student would measure at 12 minutes. [2]
  5. (e) Explain why the readings do not follow a perfectly smooth curve when repeated. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Background radiation is always present, so the measured rate includes counts not from the source
    B1
  2. (b) 400 counts/min; 200 counts/min
    420 − 20 = 400 B1; 220 − 20 = 200 B1
  3. (c) 4.0 minutes
    corrected rate halves from 800 to 400 in 4.0 min (and 400 to 200 in the next 4.0 min) B1
  4. (d) 120 counts/min
    corrected = 800 ÷ 8 = 100 (3 half-lives) M1; measured = 100 + 20 = 120 counts/min A1
  5. (e) Radioactive decay is random (counts fluctuate)
    B1
Q17Radioactivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A factory makes thin aluminium foil. A radioactive source is placed on one side of the foil and a detector on the other, to monitor the thickness. State which type of radiation should be used. Explain why the other two types are unsuitable. [3]
  2. (b) The count rate at the detector suddenly increases. State what has happened to the foil and what the control system should do. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest whether the source should have a long or short half-life. Explain. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Beta
    B1 beta; B1 alpha would be completely stopped by the foil whatever its thickness; B1 gamma would pass through almost unchanged, so small thickness changes would not affect the count
  2. (b) The foil has become thinner; the rollers should be moved apart (less pressure)
    B1 thinner; B1 adjust rollers to increase thickness
  3. (c) Long, so that the count rate does not fall noticeably during use (and the source need not be replaced often)
    B1
Q18Radioactivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Living wood contains carbon-14, which has a half-life of 5700 years. When a tree dies, the carbon-14 in it decays and is not replaced. A wooden bowl found at an archaeological site has a carbon-14 activity that is 1/8 of the activity of the same mass of living wood. Calculate the age of the bowl. [3]
  2. (b) Another sample has 25 % of the activity of living wood. Calculate its age. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why carbon dating cannot be used for an object thought to be about 100 years old. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 17100 years
    1/8 = (½)³ M1; 3 half-lives A1; 3 × 5700 = 17 100 years A1
  2. (b) 11400 years
    25 % = 2 half-lives; 2 × 5700 = 11 400 years B1
  3. (c) Very little of the carbon-14 has decayed in 100 years (much less than one half-life); the change in activity is too small to measure accurately against random fluctuations
    B1 100 years is tiny compared with the half-life; B1 activity change too small to detect
Q19Radioactivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A hospital receives 80 mg of iodine-131, which has a half-life of 8 days. Calculate the mass of iodine-131 remaining after 40 days. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the mass of iodine-131 that has decayed in this time. [1]
  3. (c) The iodine-131 emits beta particles. State one reason why used waste from the hospital must be stored safely before disposal. [1]
  4. (d) Find the smallest whole number of half-lives after which the mass of iodine-131 is less than 1.0 mg, and state this time in days. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.5 mg
    40 ÷ 8 = 5 half-lives M1; 80 ÷ 2⁵ = 2.5 mg A1
  2. (b) 77.5 mg
    80 − 2.5 = 77.5 mg B1
  3. (c) It is still radioactive and its emissions can ionise and damage living cells; it must be stored until the activity is very low
    B1
  4. (d) 56 days
    80 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 2.5 → 1.25 → 0.625 mg: first below 1.0 mg after 7 half-lives M1; 7 × 8 = 56 days A1
Q20Radioactivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Gamma radiation is used to sterilise sealed packs of surgical instruments. Explain why gamma radiation is used rather than alpha or beta. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the radiation sterilises the instruments. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the instruments do not become radioactive. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest one reason why the source used has a long half-life. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Gamma penetrates the sealed packaging to reach the instruments; alpha and beta would be absorbed by the packaging
    B1 gamma penetrates packaging; B1 alpha/beta absorbed
  2. (b) It kills bacteria / microorganisms by damaging their cells (ionisation)
    B1
  3. (c) Gamma rays do not change the nuclei of the atoms in the instruments; the instruments are irradiated, not contaminated (no radioactive material is left on them)
    B1 nuclei unchanged; B1 difference between irradiation and contamination
  4. (d) So the activity remains roughly constant and the source does not need frequent replacement
    B1

Q22Atom & nucleus · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks

Q24Atom & nucleus · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A neutral atom loses one electron. What does it become?A a positive ionB a negative ionC an atom of a different elementD an isotope of the same element [1]

  1. (a) A a positive ion

Q25Atom & nucleus · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) In an experiment, a beam of alpha particles is directed at a very thin sheet of gold foil in a vacuum. Describe what is observed about the paths of the alpha particles. [3]
  2. (b) Explain what each observation shows about the structure of the atom. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why the experiment is carried out in a vacuum. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most pass straight through undeflected; a few are deflected through small angles; a very small number are deflected through large angles (some bounce back)
    B1 most go straight through; B1 some deflected; B1 very few deflected through more than 90°
  2. (b) Most of the atom is empty space; the nucleus is positively charged (repels the positive alpha particles); the nucleus is very small and contains most of the mass (large deflections are rare)
    B1 mostly empty space; B1 nucleus positive; B1 nucleus tiny and dense / contains most of the mass
  3. (c) Alpha particles have a very short range in air; air molecules would absorb / deflect them
    B1
Q26Atom & nucleus · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) How many neutrons are in a nucleus of ²³⁵₉₂U?A 92B 143C 235D 327 [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 143

Q27Atom & nucleus · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In a fusion reactor two isotopes of hydrogen join:²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + XUse the conservation of nucleon number and proton number to identify particle X. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why fusion only happens at extremely high temperatures, such as in the core of the Sun. [2]
  3. (c) State one difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) X is a neutron, ¹₀n
    nucleon number 2 + 3 − 4 = 1, proton number 1 + 1 − 2 = 0 M1; neutron A1
  2. (b) Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other; they need very high kinetic energy (speed) to get close enough to fuse
    B1 electrostatic repulsion between positive nuclei; B1 high temperature gives high enough kinetic energy
  3. (c) Fission splits a large nucleus into smaller ones; fusion joins small nuclei to form a larger one
    B1
Q28Atom & nucleus · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Carbon-14 is ¹⁴₆C and nitrogen-14 is ¹⁴₇N. Calculate the number of neutrons in each nucleus. [2]
  2. (b) A student says these are isotopes because they have the same nucleon number. Explain why the student is wrong. [2]
  3. (c) State which of the two nuclei has the greater positive charge. Explain your answer. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) carbon-14: 8; nitrogen-14: 7

  2. (b) Isotopes must have the same proton number (same element); these have different proton numbers (6 and 7), so they are different elements

    B1 nitrogen-14 with reason: more protons (7 v 6); neutrons carry no charge
