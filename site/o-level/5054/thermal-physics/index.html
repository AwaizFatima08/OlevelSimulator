Q1Kinetic particle model · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which statement describes the particles in a liquid?A They are far apart and move freely at high speed.B They are close together and vibrate about fixed positions only.C They are close together and move around each other at random.D They are far apart and vibrate about fixed positions. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C They are close together and move around each other at random.
    Liquid particles are close together (liquids are hard to compress) but are not in fixed positions, so they slide past each other at random. B describes a solid; A describes a gas.
Q2Kinetic particle model · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A student in a school laboratory in Peshawar fills a small glass cell with smoke and lights it from the side. She looks at the cell through a microscope.

  1. (a) Describe the motion of the bright specks of smoke seen through the microscope. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what causes this motion. [3]
  3. (c) State what this observation suggests about the speed of the air molecules compared with the speed of the smoke particles. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Random / zig-zag / jerky movement in all directions, constantly changing direction.
    B1 random or zig-zag; B1 continual changes of direction (not just 'they move').
  2. (b) Air molecules (too small to be seen) move fast and randomly; they collide with the much larger smoke particles; the collisions are uneven from different sides, giving an unbalanced force that changes at random.
    B1 air molecules move randomly at high speed; B1 they collide with / bombard the smoke particles; B1 unequal numbers/forces of collisions on different sides at any instant.
  3. (c) The air molecules move much faster (they are much lighter than the smoke particles).
    B1
Q3Kinetic particle model · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A delivery rider in Lahore checks the air in his motorcycle tyre before a long ride.

  1. (a) Explain, in terms of the air molecules, how the air inside the tyre exerts a pressure on the tyre wall. [3]
  2. (b) The rider pumps more air into the tyre. Its volume and temperature do not change. Explain why the pressure increases. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Molecules move randomly at high speed; they collide with the wall and rebound; each collision changes the molecule's momentum, so a force acts on the wall; force per unit area is the pressure.
    B1 molecules hit the walls; B1 change of momentum / rebound gives a force on the wall; B1 pressure = force ÷ area (many collisions over the area).
  2. (b) More molecules in the same volume, so more collisions with each unit area of wall per second, so a larger force per unit area.
    B1 more molecules (per unit volume); B1 more frequent collisions with the wall → greater force per unit area.
Q4Kinetic particle model · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State two differences between evaporation and boiling. [2]
  2. (b) On a hot day a cricketer sweats. Explain, in terms of molecules, why the evaporation of sweat cools his skin. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Evaporation happens at any temperature; boiling only at the boiling point. Evaporation happens only at the surface; boiling happens throughout the liquid (bubbles form).
    1 mark each for any two of: any temperature vs fixed temperature (boiling point); surface only vs throughout the liquid; no bubbles vs bubbles; evaporation causes cooling of the liquid while boiling needs continuous heating at constant temperature.
  2. (b) The most energetic (fastest) water molecules escape from the surface; the molecules left behind have a lower average kinetic energy; so the temperature of the remaining sweat falls and it takes thermal energy from the skin.
    B1 more energetic / faster molecules escape from the surface; B1 average kinetic energy of remaining molecules decreases; B1 so temperature of the liquid (and skin) falls / energy drawn from the skin.
Q5Kinetic particle model · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A sealed plastic syringe contains 60 cm³ of air at a pressure of 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly until the volume of the air is 24 cm³.

  1. (a) Calculate the new pressure of the air. [3]
  2. (b) State one assumption you made in (a). [1]
  3. (c) Explain, in terms of molecules, why the pressure increases when the volume is reduced. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 250 kPa
    C1 p₁V₁ = p₂V₂; C1 100 × 60 = p₂ × 24; A1 p₂ = 250 kPa.
  2. (b) The temperature of the air stays constant (and no air escapes).
    B1 constant temperature or fixed mass of gas.
  3. (c) The same number of molecules are in a smaller volume, so they hit each unit area of the walls more often; greater force per unit area.
    B1 molecules closer together / more per unit volume; B1 more frequent collisions with the walls (per unit area).
Q6Kinetic particle model · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A sealed metal flask of fixed volume contains air. The flask is placed in a water bath and heated slowly.

  1. (a) Explain, in terms of the gas molecules, why the pressure in the flask increases as it is heated. [3]
  2. (b) The room temperature is 20 °C. Convert this temperature to kelvin. [1]
  3. (c) State what is meant by absolute zero and give its value in °C. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster; they hit the walls more often and with greater force (greater change of momentum); so the force per unit area on the walls increases.
    B1 molecules move faster / more kinetic energy; B1 more frequent collisions with walls; B1 harder collisions / greater change of momentum, so greater force per unit area.
  2. (b) 293 K
    B1 θ + 273.
  3. (c) The lowest possible temperature, at which the particles have the least possible kinetic energy; −273 °C (0 K).
    B1 lowest possible temperature / particles have least kinetic energy; B1 −273 °C.
Q7Kinetic particle model · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A fixed mass of gas has a volume of 3.0 m³ at a pressure of 120 kPa. The pressure is increased to 180 kPa at constant temperature. What is the new volume?A 1.5 m³B 2.0 m³C 4.5 m³D 7.2 m³ [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B 2.0 m³
    pV = constant: 120 × 3.0 = 180 × V, so V = 2.0 m³. C (4.5 m³) comes from multiplying by 180/120 instead of dividing.
Q8Kinetic particle model · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In many homes in Sindh, drinking water is stored in an unglazed clay pot called a matka. The clay has tiny pores, so the outside of the pot is always slightly damp.

  1. (a) Explain why the water inside the matka becomes cooler than the surrounding air. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest two ways the family could make the water cool faster. Give a reason for each. [4]
  3. (c) The matka works less well during the humid monsoon season. Suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water seeps through the pores and evaporates from the outer surface; the most energetic molecules escape; the average kinetic energy of the remaining water falls, so it cools and draws thermal energy from the water inside.
    B1 water evaporates from outer surface; B1 faster/more energetic molecules escape; B1 average KE (temperature) of remaining water decreases / energy taken from water inside.
  2. (b) Place the matka in a breeze / near a fan – moving air carries away water vapour so more molecules escape; place it where the air is dry (low humidity) – vapour does not return to the surface; use a matka with a larger surface area – more molecules at the surface can escape.
    For each of two ways: B1 way, B1 matching reason. Accept draught/wind, larger surface area, drier air, higher outside temperature (with reason).
  3. (c) The air already contains a lot of water vapour, so the rate of evaporation is lower.
    B1
Q9Kinetic particle model · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A student investigates how the pressure p of a fixed mass of air depends on its volume V. The air is trapped in a sealed tube connected to a pressure gauge. She changes the volume, waits one minute, then reads the gauge. Results:V / cm³: 50.0, 40.0, 30.0, 25.0, 20.0p / kPa: 102, 128, 150, 204, 255

  1. (a) Calculate p × V for each reading. [2]
  2. (b) Identify the anomalous reading and suggest one cause. [2]
  3. (c) State whether the results support the relationship p ∝ 1/V. Explain your answer. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest why the student waited one minute after each change of volume before reading the pressure. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5100, 5120, 4500, 5100, 5100 (kPa cm³)
    B2 all five correct; B1 for three or four correct.
  2. (b) The reading at 30.0 cm³ (150 kPa) – pV is about 4500 instead of about 5100. Cause: the gauge was read before the gas returned to room temperature, or the gauge/volume was misread (it should be about 170 kPa).
    B1 identifies 30.0 cm³ reading; B1 sensible cause (misreading scale, gas temperature not constant / reading too soon, parallax at scale).
  3. (c) Yes – apart from the anomalous point, p × V is constant (5100–5120, within 1 %), so p is inversely proportional to V.
    B1 yes; B1 reason: pV constant for the other readings (within experimental error).
  4. (d) Compressing the gas warms it; waiting lets it return to room temperature so the temperature is constant.
    B1
Q10Kinetic particle model · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A diver near Astola Island breathes out an air bubble at a depth where the total pressure is 250 kPa. The bubble has a volume of 1.2 cm³. At the surface the pressure is 100 kPa.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of the bubble at the surface. Assume its temperature does not change. [3]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of molecules, why the bubble expands as it rises. [3]
  3. (c) Divers are trained never to hold their breath while rising quickly to the surface. Use your answer to (a) to suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.0 cm³
    C1 p₁V₁ = p₂V₂; C1 250 × 1.2 = 100 × V; A1 3.0 cm³.
  2. (b) The water pressure outside the bubble decreases as depth decreases; the gas molecules inside now exert a greater pressure than the water pushing in, so the bubble grows; as the volume increases, collisions per unit area become less frequent until the gas pressure equals the outside pressure.
    B1 outside pressure falls as it rises; B1 molecules' collisions push the bubble wall outwards (gas pressure > outside); B1 larger volume → fewer collisions per unit area until pressures balance.
  3. (c) The air in the lungs would expand (about 2.5 times here) and could damage the lungs.
    B1
Q11Kinetic particle model · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

An aerosol can of air freshener carries the warning: 'Do not expose to temperatures above 50 °C.' On a summer afternoon in Jacobabad the inside of a parked car rises from 25 °C to 60 °C.

  1. (a) Convert 25 °C and 60 °C to kelvin. [1]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of the molecules of gas inside the can, why the can could burst if it is left in a hot car. [3]
  3. (c) A student says: 'The kelvin temperature has nearly doubled, so the pressure has nearly doubled.' Explain why the student is wrong. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why there is a lowest possible temperature. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 298 K and 333 K
    B1 both.
  2. (b) Temperature rise increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules; they hit the walls more often and harder; volume is fixed, so the pressure rises until it may exceed the strength of the can.
    B1 molecules move faster / greater average KE; B1 more frequent and harder collisions (greater change of momentum per collision); B1 volume fixed so pressure increases (can bursts).
  3. (c) The kelvin temperature only rises from 298 K to 333 K, about 12 %; it is the Celsius value that has more than doubled, so the pressure rises by a much smaller fraction.
    B1 kelvin values 298 → 333 K (not doubled); B1 so increase in pressure is small (about 12 %) / Celsius doubling is not meaningful because 0 °C is not absolute zero.
  4. (d) Temperature depends on the kinetic energy of particles; at absolute zero the particles have the least possible kinetic energy and cannot lose any more.
    B1
Q12Thermal properties & temperature · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Water boils at 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure. What is this temperature in kelvin?A −173 KB 100 KC 273 KD 373 K [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) D 373 K

Q13Thermal properties & temperature · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Overhead power cables between pylons near Multan are hung so that they sag slightly. In summer the afternoon temperature can reach 48 °C.

  1. (a) Explain, in terms of particles, why the cables sag more on a hot afternoon. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why engineers do not hang the cables tight in winter. [1]
  3. (c) Equal volumes of a solid, a liquid and a gas are heated through the same temperature rise. State which expands the most and which expands the least. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The particles in the metal gain energy and vibrate more (with larger amplitude); they push further apart, so the cable expands / gets longer and sags more.
    B1 particles vibrate more / faster; B1 average separation increases so the cable gets longer.
  2. (b) In cold weather the cable contracts; if it were tight it could snap or pull the pylons together.
    B1
  3. (c) Gas expands most; solid expands least.
    B1 gas most; B1 solid least.
Q14Thermal properties & temperature · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define specific heat capacity. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 2.0 kg of water by 35 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The energy needed per unit mass to raise the temperature by one degree (1 °C or 1 K).
    B1 energy per unit mass (per kg); B1 per degree rise in temperature.
  2. (b) 294000 J (294 kJ)
    C1 ΔE = mcΔθ; A1 294 000 J.
Q15Thermal properties & temperature · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A block of ice taken from a freezer at −18 °C is heated steadily until it has all melted and the water is at 30 °C.

  1. (a) Describe what happens to the temperature of the substance while it is melting. [1]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of particles, why energy must still be supplied while the substance melts. [2]
  3. (c) Define specific latent heat of fusion. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It stays constant (at the melting point).
    B1
  2. (b) The energy is used to break/weaken the forces (bonds) between particles so they can move past each other; it does not increase their kinetic energy, so temperature does not rise.
    B1 energy breaks / overcomes forces of attraction between particles; B1 kinetic energy (temperature) does not increase.
  3. (c) The energy needed to change unit mass of a substance from solid to liquid without any change in temperature.
    B1 energy per unit mass to change solid to liquid; B1 at constant temperature / no temperature change.
Q16Thermal properties & temperature · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

An electric kettle has a power of 2200 W. It contains 1.5 kg of water at 25 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C).

  1. (a) Calculate the energy needed to heat the water to 100 °C. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the minimum time this takes. [2]
  3. (c) The actual time is longer. Suggest two reasons. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 472500 J
    C1 ΔE = mcΔθ = 1.5 × 4200 × 75; A1 472 500 J.
  2. (b) 215 s (about 3.6 minutes)
    C1 t = E ÷ P; A1 215 s (ecf from a).
  3. (c) Thermal energy is lost from the kettle to the surroundings; energy is also needed to heat the kettle itself (and the element).
    1 mark each for any two of: energy lost to the air/surroundings; energy heats the kettle body and element; some water evaporates; supply voltage lower than rated.

  1. (a) Blocks of aluminium and copper have the same mass. Each receives the same quantity of thermal energy. The specific heat capacity of aluminium is 900 J/(kg °C) and that of copper is 390 J/(kg °C). Which statement is correct?A Both blocks have the same temperature rise.B The aluminium block has about 2.3 times the temperature rise of the copper block.C The copper block has about 2.3 times the temperature rise of the aluminium block.D The copper block has about 0.43 times the temperature rise of the aluminium block. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C The copper block has about 2.3 times the temperature rise of the aluminium block.
    Δθ = E ÷ (mc); with E and m equal, Δθ ∝ 1/c, so Δθ(Cu)/Δθ(Al) = 900/390 ≈ 2.3. B reverses the ratio.
Q18Thermal properties & temperature · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Water in a kettle is already boiling. The kettle stands on a balance. The 2000 W heater is left on for 100 s and the reading on the balance falls by 0.090 kg.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy supplied by the heater in 100 s. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate a value for the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the temperature of the water stays at 100 °C even though the heater is still switched on. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest whether your answer to (b) is likely to be greater or smaller than the true value. Explain. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 200000 J
    B1 E = Pt = 200 000 J.
  2. (b) 2.22 × 10⁶ J/kg
    C1 L = E ÷ m = 200 000 ÷ 0.090; A1 2.22 × 10⁶ J/kg.
  3. (c) The energy supplied is used to separate the molecules / overcome the forces between them (change of state to steam), not to increase their kinetic energy.
    B1 energy used to overcome intermolecular forces / change state; B1 average kinetic energy of molecules does not increase.
  4. (d) Greater – some of the heater's energy is lost to the surroundings, so less than 200 000 J actually boils the 0.090 kg of water.
    B1 greater; B1 energy lost to surroundings so the energy used for vaporisation is overestimated.
Q19Thermal properties & temperature · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A cook in a café in Islamabad mixes 0.3 kg of water at 80 °C with 0.2 kg of water at 20 °C in an insulated jug. She then adds 0.05 kg of ice at 0 °C.Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/(kg °C); specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg.

  1. (a) Show that the temperature of the mixture is 56 °C. Assume no energy is lost to the surroundings or the jug. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the final temperature after the ice has melted and the water has mixed. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [4]
  3. (c) Explain why a cube of ice at 0 °C cools a drink more than the same mass of water at 0 °C. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 56 °C
    M1 energy lost by hot water = energy gained by cold: 0.30 × c × (80 − T) = 0.20 × c × (T − 20); A1 T = 56 °C.
  2. (b) 43.7 °C
    C1 energy to melt ice = 0.050 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 16 700 J; C1 energy from warm water = 0.50 × 4200 × (56 − T); C1 energy to warm melted ice = 0.050 × 4200 × (T − 0); A1 T = 43.7 °C.
  3. (c) The ice must first absorb latent heat to melt (without changing temperature); this extra energy is taken from the drink before the melted water then warms up.
    B1 ice absorbs latent heat of fusion to melt; B1 so more energy is removed from the drink.
Q20Thermal properties & temperature · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A student finds the specific heat capacity of aluminium. A 1.00 kg aluminium block has two holes: one for a 12 V heater and one for a thermometer. The heater current is 4.0 A. The heater is switched on for 300 s.Temperature at start = 22.0 °C; highest temperature = 37.5 °C.

  1. (a) Calculate the electrical energy supplied by the heater. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium given by these results. [2]
  3. (c) The accepted value is 900 J/(kg °C). State whether the student's value agrees with it within 10 %. Show your working. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest two improvements to the experiment, explaining how each improves the result. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 14400 J
    C1 E = VIt; A1 14 400 J.
  2. (b) 929 J/(kg °C)
    C1 c = E ÷ (mΔθ) = 14 400 ÷ (1.00 × 15.5); A1 929 J/(kg °C).
  3. (c) Yes – it is about 3.2 % larger, which is within 10 %.
    M1 (929 − 900) ÷ 900 × 100 ≈ 3 %; A1 within 10 % so agrees.
  4. (d) Wrap the block in insulation (e.g. cotton wool / foam) to reduce energy losses to the surroundings; put a few drops of oil in the thermometer hole for good thermal contact; start from a block cooled a few degrees below room temperature and heat to the same amount above it, so gains and losses balance; use a thermometer with a finer scale (or a digital one) for more precise readings.
    1 mark each for any two improvements with reasons, tied to this apparatus.
Q21Thermal properties & temperature · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A boiling tube of a waxy substance X is melted in hot water, then removed and allowed to cool in air. A student records its temperature every minute:time / min: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8temperature / °C: 80.0, 74.0, 69.5, 69.0, 69.0, 69.0, 68.5, 62.0, 56.0

  1. (a) State the freezing point of substance X shown by these results. [1]
  2. (b) Describe how the results show that X is changing state between 3 min and 6 min. [1]
  3. (c) Explain, in terms of particles, why the temperature stays constant while X freezes even though it is losing energy to the surroundings. [2]
  4. (d) Calculate the average rate of cooling, in °C/min, of the liquid between 0 and 2 min and of the solid between 7 and 8 min. [2]
  5. (e) Suggest why taking a reading every 30 s, instead of every minute, would give a more reliable freezing point. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 69 °C
    B1 accept 68.5–69.0 °C.
  2. (b) The temperature stays (almost) constant while the substance is still losing energy.
    B1
  3. (c) As particles form bonds / become fixed in a lattice, energy (latent heat) is released; this replaces the energy lost to the surroundings so the average kinetic energy does not fall.
    B1 bonds form between particles / latent heat released; B1 balances energy lost, so kinetic energy / temperature unchanged.
  4. (d) Liquid: (80 − 69.5) ÷ 2 = 5.25 °C/min; solid: (62 − 56) ÷ 1 = 6.0 °C/min.
    B1 5.25 °C/min (accept 5.3); B1 6.0 °C/min.
  5. (e) More readings during the flat part, so the constant temperature is identified more clearly.
    B1
Q22Thermal energy transfer · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Thermal energy reaches the Earth from the Sun through space. Which method of transfer is this?A conductionB convectionC evaporationD infrared radiation [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) D infrared radiation
    Space is (almost) a vacuum, and only electromagnetic radiation travels without a medium. Conduction and convection need particles.
Q23Thermal energy transfer · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A karahi (cooking wok) is made of steel, but its two handles are made of wood.

  1. (a) Explain why the metal body of the karahi is a good choice for cooking. [1]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of particles, how thermal energy is conducted through the metal. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why the handles are made of wood. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Metal is a good thermal conductor, so energy passes quickly from the flame to the food.
    B1
  2. (b) Particles (atoms/ions) at the hot side vibrate more and pass energy to neighbouring particles through collisions/bonds; metals also have free (delocalised) electrons that gain kinetic energy and move quickly through the metal, carrying energy to cooler parts.
    B1 atoms/lattice vibrations passed to neighbours; B1 free/delocalised electrons; B1 electrons move through the metal and transfer energy by collisions (faster than lattice alone).
  3. (c) Wood is a poor conductor (insulator), so the handles stay cool enough to hold.
    B1
Q24Thermal energy transfer · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a flat in Karachi, a split air conditioner is fixed high up on the wall near the ceiling.

  1. (a) Explain why the air conditioner is mounted high on the wall. [3]
  2. (b) In winter the family uses a gas heater. State where in the room the heater should be placed and explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The cold air from the unit is denser than the warm air in the room; it sinks; warm, less dense air rises to the top to be cooled, setting up a convection current that cools the whole room.
    B1 cold air is more dense; B1 cold air sinks / warm air rises; B1 convection current circulates air through the whole room.
  2. (b) Near the floor – the warm air it produces is less dense and rises, so a convection current carries warm air around the room.
    B1 low down / at floor level; B1 hot air rises (less dense) to set up a convection current.

Q26Thermal energy transfer · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A student tests how the colour of a surface affects the rate of cooling. Two identical aluminium cans with lids are used: one painted dull black and one polished shiny silver. Each is filled with 250 cm³ of water at 80.0 °C and left on a wooden bench. After 15 minutes:dull black can: 61.5 °Cshiny silver can: 70.0 °C

  1. (a) Calculate the temperature drop of each can. [1]
  2. (b) State what the results show about the two surfaces. [1]
  3. (c) State three variables that must be kept the same for this to be a fair test. [3]
  4. (d) Suggest why both cans were fitted with lids made of the same material. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Black: 18.5 °C; silver: 10.0 °C
    B1 both.
  2. (b) The dull black surface is the better emitter of infrared radiation (it cooled more).
    B1
  3. (c) Volume/mass of water; starting temperature; size/shape/material of can; time of cooling; room temperature / draughts; both with lids (or both without).
    1 mark each for any three.
  4. (d) To reduce (and keep equal) energy loss by evaporation and convection from the water surface, so the difference is due to the can surfaces.
    B1
Q27Thermal energy transfer · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A vacuum flask keeps tea hot during a family trip to Murree. It has a double-walled glass container with a vacuum between the walls. The glass surfaces facing the vacuum are silvered. The flask is closed with a hollow plastic stopper.

  1. (a) Explain how the vacuum between the two glass walls reduces thermal energy transfer. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the silvered surfaces reduce thermal energy transfer. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the stopper is made of hollow plastic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Conduction and convection need particles; a vacuum has no particles, so neither can occur across the gap.
    B1 no particles / medium in vacuum; B1 so no conduction or convection.
  2. (b) Shiny surfaces are poor emitters of infrared radiation and good reflectors, so radiation from the hot tea is reflected back and little is emitted across the gap.
    B1 poor emitter; B1 reflects infrared back towards the liquid.
  3. (c) Plastic is a poor conductor, and the trapped air in the hollow is also a poor conductor; the stopper also stops convection currents and evaporation from the top.
    B1 poor conductor (plastic/trapped air); B1 prevents convection / evaporation losses from the top.
Q28Thermal energy transfer · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the type of radiation that the Earth's surface mainly emits. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the average temperature of the Earth stays roughly constant over many years. [2]
  3. (c) Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide absorb some of the infrared radiation emitted by the Earth. Explain why an increase in these gases causes the Earth's average temperature to rise. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Infrared radiation
    B1
  2. (b) The Earth absorbs radiation from the Sun and emits infrared radiation into space; when the rate of absorbing equals the rate of emitting, the temperature stays constant.
    B1 absorbs from Sun and emits infrared; B1 rates of absorption and emission are equal (balance).
  3. (c) Less infrared escapes into space, so the rate of energy loss falls below the rate of absorption; the Earth warms; as it warms it emits more until a new balance is reached at a higher temperature.
    B1 less radiation escapes; B1 energy in > energy out so temperature rises; B1 new balance at higher temperature (hotter body emits more).
Q29Thermal energy transfer · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 9 marks

A solar water heater on a roof in Quetta has a dull black absorbing panel of area 2.0 m², with copper pipes fixed to its front and a layer of foam behind it. On a sunny day the Sun delivers 600 W to each square metre of panel. Only 40 % of this power reaches the 80 kg of water in the tank. Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/(kg °C).

  1. (a) Explain why the absorbing panel is painted dull black. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the copper pipes are fixed to the panel and why the panel is backed with foam. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the time needed to heat the water by 20 °C. Give your answer in hours to 2 significant figures. [4]
  4. (d) The tank is placed above the panel. Explain how the water circulates without a pump. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Dull black is the best absorber of infrared (and visible) radiation.
    B1
  2. (b) Copper is a good conductor, so energy passes quickly from the panel to the water; foam is a poor conductor (trapped air), reducing losses from the back.
    B1 copper good conductor; B1 foam insulates / reduces conduction loss.
  3. (c) 3.9 hours (14000 s)
    C1 power to water = 600 × 2.0 × 0.40 = 480 W; C1 E = mcΔθ = 80 × 4200 × 20 = 6.72 × 10⁶ J; C1 t = E ÷ P = 14 000 s; A1 3.9 h.
  4. (d) Water heated in the panel expands, becomes less dense and rises into the tank; colder, denser water from the tank sinks down to the panel – a convection current.
    B1 heated water less dense and rises; B1 cooler denser water sinks to replace it (convection).
Q30Thermal energy transfer · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

On summer days at Clifton Beach, Karachi, a steady breeze blows in from the Arabian Sea during the afternoon.

  1. (a) The specific heat capacity of sand is much lower than that of water. Explain why, on a sunny morning, the land becomes warmer than the sea. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a breeze blows from the sea towards the land during the afternoon. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest and explain the direction of the breeze late at night. [2]
  4. (d) Car radiators have many thin metal fins. Explain how the fins increase the rate of energy loss by radiation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each kilogram of land needs less energy for each degree rise, so with similar energy received the land's temperature rises faster than the sea's (the sea also mixes the energy through a greater depth).
    B1 lower specific heat capacity → larger temperature rise for same energy; B1 land heats faster than sea.
  2. (b) Air above the hot land is heated, expands and becomes less dense, so it rises; cooler, denser air from above the sea moves in to replace it; this flow at ground level is the sea breeze (part of a convection current).
    B1 air over land heated, less dense; B1 warm air rises; B1 cooler air from over the sea flows in to replace it.
  3. (c) From the land towards the sea: the land cools faster than the sea, so the air above the warmer sea rises and air from the land replaces it.
    B1 land to sea; B1 sea now warmer than land so convection current is reversed.
  4. (d) They increase the surface area, and the rate of emission of infrared radiation increases with surface area.
    B1
