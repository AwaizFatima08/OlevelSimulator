Q1Conservation & sustainability · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which measure helps to keep a fish population sustainable?A using nets with a smaller mesh sizeB fishing all year roundC setting a maximum catch (quota) for each boatD catching the largest number of young fish [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C setting a maximum catch (quota) for each boat
    A quota limits how many fish are removed so enough remain to breed. A smaller mesh (A) would catch more young fish before they breed, which harms the stock.
Q2Conservation & sustainability · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Give three reasons why it is important to conserve endangered species. [3]
  2. (b) Describe two methods used to conserve endangered species. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) To maintain biodiversity; plants or animals may provide future medicines or foods; to keep food chains / webs stable; for ecotourism and income; for future generations / moral reasons; to preserve genetic variety for breeding.
    B1 each for any three.
  2. (b) Protecting habitats in national parks or reserves; captive breeding programmes and release; laws against hunting / trade; seed banks for plants; education.
    B1 each for any two.
Q3Conservation & sustainability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The Indus river dolphin is an endangered species found mainly in the Indus river in Pakistan. In one stretch of the river, surveys counted 1250 dolphins in an early survey and 1820 in a later survey (figures for this question).

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in the number of dolphins counted. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest three threats to the Indus river dolphin. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest two actions that may have helped the population to increase. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 45.6 %
    M1 (1820 − 1250) ÷ 1250 × 100; A1 45.6.
  2. (b) Barrages divide the river into separate sections, isolating populations; less water in the river when it is taken for irrigation; accidental capture in fishing nets; pollution from farms, factories and sewage; dolphins trapped in irrigation canals.
    B1 each for any three.
  3. (c) Hunting banned / legal protection; a protected reserve; rescuing dolphins trapped in canals; educating fishing communities; monitoring by surveys.
    B1 each for any two.
Q4Conservation & sustainability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The catch of one species of fish by boats along a stretch of coast (thousand tonnes):2000: 52; 2005: 61; 2010: 70; 2015: 48; 2020: 31

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage decrease in catch between 2010 and 2020. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why the catch fell after 2010 even though more boats were fishing. [2]
  3. (c) Describe three ways to make fishing in this area sustainable. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 55.7 %
    M1 (70 − 31) ÷ 70 × 100; A1 55.7.
  2. (b) Overfishing: fish were removed faster than they could reproduce; too few adult fish were left to breed.
    B1 overfishing; B1 breeding stock reduced.
  3. (c) Quotas limiting catch; larger mesh size so young fish escape; closed seasons during breeding; no-fishing protected areas; limit the number of boats / licences; fish farming.
    B1 each for any three.
Q5Conservation & sustainability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a sustainable resource. [1]
  2. (b) Describe how forests can be managed so that timber is a sustainable resource. [3]
  3. (c) Explain how recycling paper helps to conserve forests. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A resource that is produced as fast as it is used, so it does not run out.
    B1.
  2. (b) Replant trees to replace those cut down; selective felling (only some trees cut); leave areas protected; limit felling to the rate of regrowth; education / certification schemes.
    B1 each for any three.
  3. (c) Fewer new trees need to be cut down to make paper.
    B1.
Q6Conservation & sustainability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) A zoo runs a captive breeding programme for a rare species of leopard, starting with only eight animals. Explain why a population that has become very small is at risk even if it is protected. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest how zoos can reduce this risk. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a seed bank is a useful way of conserving plant species. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Little genetic variation remains; inbreeding increases the chance of harmful recessive conditions; the population may be unable to adapt to new diseases or environmental change; a single disaster could wipe it out.
    B1 each for any three.
  2. (b) Exchange animals between zoos in different countries; keep breeding records (studbooks) to avoid breeding close relatives; use frozen sperm / artificial insemination.
    B1 each for any two.
  3. (c) Seeds can be stored dry and cold for many years in a small space; they keep a wide range of genetic variety and can be grown if the species dies out in the wild.
    B1 long-term storage in small space; B1 keeps genetic variety / allows replanting.
Q7Conservation & sustainability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Markhor are wild goats living in the mountains of northern Pakistan. Their numbers fell because of hunting. In some areas, a small number of permits are now sold each year to foreign hunters to shoot an old male, and most of the fee goes to the local community. Suggest how this scheme can help the markhor population to increase. [3]
  2. (b) Give one argument against this scheme. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest how scientists could check whether the scheme is working. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Local people earn money from living markhor, so they protect them and stop illegal hunting; only a few old males are shot, which have already bred; money is used for wildlife guards / habitat protection / community projects.
    B1 each for any three.
  2. (b) Killing animals is ethically wrong; risk of corruption or too many permits; the strongest males may be removed from the gene pool.
    B1.
  3. (c) Carry out regular surveys / counts of markhor in the same areas; compare numbers over years and with areas that do not have the scheme.
    B1 repeated population surveys; B1 comparison over time / with control area.
Q8Water pollution & eutrophication · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Fertiliser washes from farmland into a lake. Which sequence of events follows?A algae grow rapidly → plants below die from lack of light → bacteria decompose dead plants → oxygen falls → fish dieB fish die → bacteria decompose fish → oxygen rises → algae grow rapidly → plants dieC algae grow rapidly → oxygen rises → bacteria die → fish die from too much oxygenD plants die from lack of nitrate → algae die → fish die from lack of food → bacteria increase [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A algae grow rapidly → plants below die from lack of light → bacteria decompose dead plants → oxygen falls → fish die
    Extra nitrate and phosphate cause an algal bloom; algae block light so plants die; decomposing bacteria increase and use up oxygen in aerobic respiration, so fish die. B gets the order wrong, starting with fish death.
Q9Water pollution & eutrophication · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Much of the litter on beaches near Karachi is plastic. State what is meant by saying that plastic is non-biodegradable. [1]
  2. (b) Describe two ways in which plastic waste in the sea can harm marine animals. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two ways to reduce plastic pollution of the sea. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It cannot be broken down by decomposers (bacteria and fungi), so it stays in the environment for a very long time.
    B1.
  2. (b) Animals such as turtles swallow plastic bags, which block the gut and they starve; animals get tangled in nets and plastic, and drown or are injured; small pieces of plastic (microplastics) enter food chains.
    B1 each for any two.
  3. (c) Use reusable cloth bags; recycle plastic; ban single-use plastic bags; proper waste collection; beach clean-ups.
    B1 each for any two.
Q10Water pollution & eutrophication · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how untreated sewage entering a river can cause the death of fish several hundred metres downstream. [5]
  2. (b) Untreated sewage is also a danger to people who use the river water. Explain why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sewage contains organic matter and nutrients (nitrate / phosphate); nutrients cause rapid growth of algae; algae block light so water plants cannot photosynthesise and die; bacteria (decomposers) multiply and break down sewage and dead plants; bacteria use oxygen in aerobic respiration; oxygen concentration falls so fish cannot respire and die.
    1 mark each for any five.
  2. (b) It contains pathogens (e.g. bacteria causing cholera or typhoid) that can cause disease if the water is drunk.
    B1.
Q11Water pollution & eutrophication · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A housing estate releases untreated sewage into a river at an outlet (0 m). Samples were taken upstream (−100 m) and downstream.distance / m: -100, 0, 200, 500, 1000, 2000oxygen / mg/dm³: 9.2, 8.8, 4.1, 2.3, 5.0, 8.5bacteria (relative numbers): 10, 120, 300, 260, 90, 20

  1. (a) Describe how the oxygen concentration changes downstream of the outlet. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage decrease in oxygen concentration from the upstream site (−100 m) to the lowest value. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [2]
  3. (c) Use the data on bacteria to explain the change in oxygen concentration between 0 and 500 m. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest why the oxygen concentration recovers further downstream. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) It falls from 8.8 mg/dm³ at the outlet to a lowest value of 2.3 mg/dm³ at 500 m; then rises again to 8.5 mg/dm³ at 2000 m.
    B1 falls to minimum at 500 m; B1 recovers further downstream (data quoted).
  2. (b) 75 %
    M1 (9.2 − 2.3) ÷ 9.2 × 100; A1 75.
  3. (c) Bacteria numbers increase greatly as they feed on the sewage; their aerobic respiration uses up oxygen from the water.
    B1 more bacteria feeding on sewage; B1 respiration uses oxygen.
  4. (d) The sewage has been broken down so bacteria numbers fall; oxygen dissolves from the air / is made by photosynthesis.
    B1.
Q12Water pollution & eutrophication · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Students counted two kinds of animal in kick samples from four sites on a stream below a farm. Stonefly nymphs need water with a high oxygen concentration. Bloodworms (midge larvae) contain haemoglobin.site: A (upstream), B, C, D (furthest downstream)stonefly nymphs: 24, 2, 0, 11bloodworms: 1, 30, 52, 12

  1. (a) Describe the differences in the numbers of the two animals between site A and site C. [2]
  2. (b) Use the information to explain these differences. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest which site, B or D, is cleaner. Give a reason. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest one advantage of using living organisms rather than a single chemical test to monitor pollution. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Stonefly nymphs fall from 24 at A to 0 at C; bloodworms increase from 1 at A to 52 at C.
    B1 stonefly decrease; B1 bloodworm increase (data).
  2. (b) Site C has the least oxygen because of pollution (bacteria decomposing waste); stonefly nymphs need high oxygen so cannot survive there; bloodworms have haemoglobin, so they can obtain enough oxygen and survive with less competition.
    B1 low oxygen at polluted site; B1 stonefly need high oxygen; B1 bloodworm haemoglobin allows survival in low oxygen.
  3. (c) D: it has more stonefly nymphs (11 v 2) and fewer bloodworms (12 v 30).
    B1 D with reason.
  4. (d) Organisms show the effect of pollution over a long period, not just at the moment of sampling.
    B1.
Q13Water pollution & eutrophication · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Farmers around a lake increased the amount of nitrate fertiliser used each year. The mean nitrate concentration in the lake water was recorded.fertiliser / kg per hectare: 50, 100, 150, 200nitrate in lake / mg/dm³: 2.0, 4.5, 8.0, 13.0

  1. (a) Calculate how many times greater the nitrate concentration was in the year with 200 kg/ha than in the year with 50 kg/ha. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest why nitrate in the lake increased more than in proportion to the fertiliser applied. [2]
  3. (c) Describe three ways in which farmers and local councils could reduce eutrophication of the lake. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6.5 times
    A1 13.0 ÷ 2.0 = 6.5.
  2. (b) Crops can absorb only a limited amount; once their need is met, extra fertiliser is not taken up and more of it is washed (leached) into the lake.
    B1 crops take up limited amount; B1 excess leached / runs off.
  3. (c) Apply only the amount of fertiliser crops need; avoid spreading before heavy rain; leave a strip of uncultivated land / trees along the lake edge; use organic manure that releases nutrients slowly; treat sewage before release.
    B1 each for any three.
Q14Air pollution & climate change · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which gas released by burning coal is the main cause of acid rain?A carbon dioxideB methaneC oxygenD sulfur dioxide [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) D sulfur dioxide

Q16Air pollution & climate change · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which farming activity releases large amounts of methane?A growing rice in flooded paddy fieldsB planting trees along field edgesC spraying insecticide on cottonD harvesting wheat with a combine harvester [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A growing rice in flooded paddy fields
    Bacteria in the waterlogged, anaerobic soil of flooded paddy fields produce methane (cattle are another major source). Planting trees (B) removes carbon dioxide and does not release methane.
Q17Air pollution & climate change · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how increasing amounts of carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere lead to global warming. [3]
  2. (b) Describe three possible effects of climate change. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Heat (infrared) radiation from the Earth's surface is absorbed by greenhouse gases; more greenhouse gases trap more heat; the mean temperature of the Earth rises (enhanced greenhouse effect).
    B1 Earth radiates heat / infrared; B1 absorbed / trapped by greenhouse gases; B1 more gas → more heat retained → temperature rises.
  2. (b) Melting glaciers and ice caps; sea level rise and flooding of coastal areas; more extreme weather (heatwaves, floods, droughts); changes in rainfall affecting crops; species extinction or migration; spread of insect-borne diseases.
    B1 each for any three.
Q18Air pollution & climate change · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Carbon dioxide concentration in the atmosphere measured at a remote mountain observatory (values rounded to the nearest ppm):year: 1960, 1980, 2000, 2020carbon dioxide / ppm: 317, 339, 370, 414

  1. (a) Calculate the mean rate of increase in carbon dioxide concentration, in ppm per year, between 1960 and 1980, and between 2000 and 2020. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in carbon dioxide concentration from 1960 to 2020. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two human activities that have caused this increase. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1960–1980: 1.1 ppm per year; 2000–2020: 2.2 ppm per year
    A1 22 ÷ 20 = 1.1; A1 44 ÷ 20 = 2.2.
  2. (b) 30.6 %
    M1 (414 − 317) ÷ 317 × 100; A1 30.6.
  3. (c) Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) in vehicles, power stations and factories; deforestation / burning forests, so less carbon dioxide is absorbed by photosynthesis.

Q19Air pollution & climate change · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Every winter, cities in Punjab such as Lahore suffer from heavy smog. Sources include vehicle exhaust, burning of crop stubble and coal-burning factories and brick kilns. Explain how sulfur dioxide from these sources can damage lakes and forests far from the city. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest three ways to reduce sulfur dioxide pollution. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sulfur dioxide is carried by wind; it dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain; acid rain lowers the pH of lakes, killing fish and other aquatic organisms (aluminium released from soil); it damages leaves and roots of trees / leaches minerals from soil, so trees die.
    B1 carried by wind; B1 forms acid rain; B1 effect on lakes; B1 effect on trees / soil.
  2. (b) Use low-sulfur fuels; remove sulfur dioxide from chimney gases (scrubbers / flue-gas desulfurisation); use renewable energy (solar, wind, hydroelectric); improve kiln technology; ban burning of crop stubble.
    B1 each for any three.

  2. (b) Explain why destroying a habitat can cause a species to become extinct. [2]

Q21Habitat destruction & deforestation · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Large areas of forest on a hillside are cut down. Which effect is most likely in the following years?A more transpiration from the hillsideB less soil erosion during heavy rainC more flooding in valleys belowD more carbon dioxide absorbed from the air [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C more flooding in valleys below
    Without trees, less rain is intercepted and absorbed by roots, so water runs off quickly and floods valleys (with soil erosion). B is wrong: erosion increases because roots no longer hold the soil.
Q22Habitat destruction & deforestation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hillside forests in northern Pakistan have been cut for timber and firewood. Explain how deforestation can increase the risk of floods and landslides. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how deforestation contributes to an increase in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fewer trees intercept rainfall; fewer roots to absorb water, so more runs off the surface quickly into rivers; roots no longer hold soil, so soil is washed away (erosion) and slopes become unstable; eroded soil fills river beds, so rivers overflow more easily.
    1 mark each for any four.
  2. (b) Fewer trees to remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis; burning or decay of cut trees releases carbon dioxide.
    B1 less photosynthesis; B1 burning / decomposition releases CO₂.
Q23Habitat destruction & deforestation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Mangrove forests grow along the coast of the Indus delta. Large areas have been cut down for firewood and to make space for shrimp farms. Suggest two effects of this on fish populations in the area. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why losing mangroves can lead to more damage to coastal villages during storms. [2]
  3. (c) Large mangrove replanting projects have taken place on the Sindh coast. Give one benefit of replanting other than for fish. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Young fish and shrimps lose the sheltered nursery areas among mangrove roots; less food (leaf litter, small animals); so fish populations and catches fall.
    B1 loss of breeding / nursery habitat; B1 loss of food / fewer fish.
  2. (b) Mangrove roots trap sediment and slow down waves; without them the coast erodes and storm waves reach further inland.
    B1 roots hold soil / reduce erosion; B1 waves no longer slowed.
  3. (c) Mangroves absorb carbon dioxide; protect the coastline; provide habitats for birds and other wildlife; sustainable wood supply.
    B1.

Q27Food production & agriculture · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Trials on wheat fields in Punjab measured grain yield with different amounts of nitrate fertiliser.fertiliser / kg per hectare: 0, 50, 100, 150, 200yield / tonnes per hectare: 2.1, 3.4, 4.2, 4.5, 4.5

  2. (b) No further increase in yield (it stays at 4.5 t/ha).

  1. (a) A country has a population of 240 million, growing by 2 % each year. Calculate the population after 10 years. Give your answer in millions to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss the problems a country faces in producing enough food for a growing population. Include reasons why famine may occur. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) 292.6 million
    M1 240 × 1.02¹⁰; A1 292.6.
  2. (b) Rising demand for food; less farmland as cities expand; soil erosion / desertification reduces fertile land; droughts and floods (climate change) destroy crops; water shortage for irrigation; pests and diseases; poverty means people cannot buy food; war or poor transport stops distribution; high cost of fertilisers and machinery.

Q30Food production & agriculture (option) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

One hectare of land produces wheat containing 20 000 MJ of energy per year. A person needs about 3650 MJ of food energy per year. When cattle eat wheat, about 10 % of the energy in the wheat ends up in the beef eaten by people.

  1. (a) Calculate how many people could be fed for one year from one hectare (i) if they eat the wheat, (ii) if the wheat is fed to cattle and they eat the beef. Give answers to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why so much energy is lost when the wheat is fed to cattle. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why people still keep animals for food in areas where crops grow poorly. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 5.5 people (ii) 0.5 people
    A1 20 000 ÷ 3650 = 5.5; M1 20 000 × 0.10 = 2000 MJ; A1 2000 ÷ 3650 = 0.5.
  2. (b) Cattle use energy for respiration (movement, keeping warm); some of the food is not digested and is lost in faeces; energy lost in urine / parts not eaten (bones, hide); so only about 10 % passes to the next trophic level.
    B1 respiration / heat loss; B1 egestion / undigested food; B1 not all parts eaten / energy in excretion.
  3. (c) Animals can graze on land too dry, steep or poor to grow crops, turning grass people cannot eat into food.
    B1.
