Q2Workers · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a football factory in Sialkot, one group of workers cuts the panels, another stitches them together and a third group prints the logos.

  1. (a) Define specialisation of workers (division of labour). [2]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages to the factory of its workers specialising. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Workers concentrating on a particular task or a narrow range of tasks, with production split into separate stages.
    1 mark: concentrating on one task / particular job. 1 mark: production broken down into stages / tasks.
  2. (b) Any two, developed: workers become more skilled and faster through repetition, so productivity rises; less time is wasted moving between tasks; training is quicker and cheaper because each worker learns only one task; it is easier to use specialist machinery; output rises and average cost falls.
    For each of two advantages: 1 mark for the advantage, 1 mark for the effect on the firm (output/costs/quality).
Q3Workers · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Demand for software developers in Pakistan has risen as more firms sell services to customers abroad.

  1. (a) Identify two factors that influence the demand for a type of worker. [2]
  2. (b) Explain, using demand and supply, how the wage of software developers is likely to change. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: demand for the product the workers make (derived demand); productivity of the workers; price of the workers (wage); price of capital that could replace them.
    1 mark each for any two valid factors.
  2. (b) Demand for developers increases (shifts right); with supply of skilled developers slow to rise, there is a shortage at the old wage, so the wage rises until a new equilibrium.
    Demand for developers increases/shifts right (1); supply limited in the short run because training takes time (1); shortage at old wage, firms compete for workers (1); equilibrium wage and employment rise (1).
Q4Workers · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a car assembly plant in Karachi each worker fits only one component, such as a door or a seat, as cars move along a production line.

  1. (a) Identify two disadvantages of specialisation for the workers. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two disadvantages of specialisation for the firm. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: boredom/repetitive work; low job satisfaction; narrow skills, making it hard to change jobs; risk of unemployment if the task is automated; repetitive strain injuries.
    1 mark each for any two valid disadvantages.
  2. (b) If one worker is absent the whole line stops; bored workers make mistakes and leave, raising costs.
    2 marks each for two developed points: interdependence – absence or a strike at one stage stops the whole line; boredom lowers quality/raises errors; higher labour turnover raises recruitment and training costs; inflexible workforce when demand changes.
Q5Workers · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A garment worker in Lahore is paid a piece rate of Rs 35 for each shirt she stitches. She stitches 40 shirts a day and works 26 days a month.

  1. (a) Calculate her monthly earnings. [2]
  2. (b) The piece rate rises to Rs 38 a shirt. Calculate the percentage increase in her monthly earnings if her output does not change. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one disadvantage to the worker of being paid by piece rate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 36 400
    35 × 40 = Rs 1 400 a day (M1); × 26 = Rs 36 400 (A1).
  2. (b) 8.6%
    New earnings 38 × 40 × 26 = Rs 39 520 (M1); (39 520 − 36 400) ÷ 36 400 × 100 = 8.6% (A1).
  3. (c) Earnings vary and fall if she is ill, the machine breaks or there are power cuts; pressure to work fast.
    Disadvantage (1) explained (1), e.g. income is uncertain because it depends on output, which falls through illness or power cuts.

Q8Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A factory worker in Faisalabad is paid Rs 400 an hour for a basic week of 40 hours. Any extra hours are paid at time-and-a-half (1.5 times the basic rate). In one week he works 48 hours.

  1. (a) Calculate his gross earnings for the week. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the employer pays a higher rate for overtime hours. [2]

Q10Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Define derived demand. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse how a large increase in the number of engineering graduates is likely to affect the wages of engineers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Demand for a factor of production, such as labour, that depends on the demand for the product it helps to make.
    Demand for a factor/labour (1) that comes from demand for the good or service it produces (1).
  2. (b) Supply of engineers increases (shifts right); at the old wage there is a surplus of engineers, so the equilibrium wage falls and employment rises; the effect is smaller if demand for engineers is also rising.
    More graduates increases the supply of engineers (1), supply curve shifts right (1); surplus of engineers at the original wage (1); competition for jobs pushes the wage down (1) while the number employed rises (1); the wage may not fall if demand for engineers is rising at the same time (e.g. construction boom) or graduates emigrate (1).
Q11Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

An electrician in Karachi earns more than an electrician doing the same job in a small town in rural Sindh.

  1. (a) Identify two reasons why wages for the same job may differ between a large city and a small town. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse why skilled workers usually earn more than unskilled workers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. higher cost of living in the city; greater demand for electricians in the city; more firms competing for workers; workers unwilling to move.
    1 mark each for any two valid reasons.
  2. (b) Supply of skilled workers is limited because training takes time and money; demand is high because they are more productive; so the equilibrium wage is higher. Unskilled workers are in plentiful supply and easier to replace, so wages are lower.
    Skilled: supply is low/inelastic because of long, costly training and ability needed (1+1); demand is high because their productivity and value of output are high (1+1). Unskilled: large supply of workers, easily replaced, so wage low (1). Diagram/comparison of two labour markets with higher equilibrium wage for skilled (1).
Q12Workers · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A government raises the national minimum wage by 20%. Many workers in shops, restaurants and small factories are paid at the minimum wage.

  1. (a) Define a national minimum wage. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not raising the minimum wage benefits low-paid workers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) The lowest wage rate per hour (or per month) that employers are legally allowed to pay workers, set by the government.
    1 mark: lowest wage that can be paid. 1 mark: set by law/government.
  2. (b) Benefits those who keep their jobs, but may cost some jobs.
    Up to 3 marks for 'benefits': higher income for those employed; reduces poverty and inequality; may motivate workers and raise productivity; encourages people into work. Up to 3 marks for 'may not': higher labour costs may make firms employ fewer workers, raising unemployment among the low-skilled; firms may replace workers with machines; prices may rise, reducing real gains; firms may cut hours or move into the informal sector, where the law is not enforced. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion (e.g. depends on the size of the rise and the elasticity of demand for labour).
Q13Workers · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks

In a country the average monthly wage of men is Rs 80 000 and the average monthly wage of women is Rs 62 400.

  1. (a) Calculate the gap between men's and women's average wages as a percentage of men's average wage. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the difference between men's and women's average wages is caused only by discrimination. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) 22%
    (80 000 − 62 400) ÷ 80 000 × 100 (M1) = 22% (A1).
  2. (b) Discrimination can explain part of it (lower pay for the same work, fewer promotions). Other causes: women may work in lower-paid occupations or sectors, work part-time, have career breaks for childcare, have less access to training or education, or be less geographically mobile. Conclusion: discrimination is one cause, but not the only one, though some other causes may themselves reflect social barriers.
    Discrimination (up to 4): employers pay women less for the same job; fewer promotions to senior posts; barriers to entering some well-paid occupations. Other causes (up to 4): occupational differences (more women in lower-paid sectors such as teaching assistants, garment work); part-time work/career breaks reduce experience; less education/training in some regions; lower geographical mobility; less membership of strong trade unions. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.
Q14Workers · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Identify two parts of a worker's pay other than the basic wage. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a worker's earnings are likely to rise throughout their working life. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. overtime pay, bonuses, commission, allowances, fringe benefits such as free transport.
    1 mark each for any two valid items.
  2. (b) Likely to rise: experience and skills increase productivity; promotion; pay scales based on length of service; further qualifications. Not always: earnings may fall in later years in physical jobs; skills can become out of date with new technology; the industry may decline; ill-health, part-time work or career breaks; real earnings fall if inflation is higher than pay rises. Conclusion: earnings usually rise in early and middle working life, then level off or fall.
    Rise (up to 4): experience and training raise productivity; promotion to more senior posts; annual increments in pay scales (e.g. government jobs); more qualifications. Not always (up to 4): physical strength declines in manual jobs; technology makes skills obsolete; structural decline of the industry; illness, part-time work or career breaks; pay rises below inflation cut real earnings. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.
Q15Workers · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain one reason why specialisation by workers increases output. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not specialisation benefits an economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Workers become skilled and quick at one task through repetition, so more is produced per hour.
    Reason (1) explained (1): e.g. repetition increases skill and speed; no time lost moving between tasks; workers can do the task they are best at.
  2. (b) For: higher productivity and output, lower average costs and prices, use of machinery, more trade. Against: boredom and lower quality, dependence on others (a breakdown at one stage stops production), narrow skills cause structural unemployment when demand changes, regions that specialise are vulnerable. Conclusion: benefits usually outweigh costs if workers can retrain.

Against (up to 4): repetitive work lowers motivation; interdependence — strikes or absence at one stage halt others; workers with narrow skills face occupational immobility and unemployment when industries decline; over-dependence on a few industries. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.

Q19Firms · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

  1. (a) Any two of: number of workers; sales revenue; output; capital employed; market share; number of branches/shops.

  2. (b) Explain two ways in which a firm can grow internally. [4]

  1. (a) When a firm grows by increasing its own output/size, e.g. opening new branches or buying more capital, rather than by joining with another firm.

Q21Firms · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A dairy company in Punjab closes several small milk-processing units and builds one large, highly automated plant.

  1. (a) Define economies of scale. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two internal economies of scale that the company might gain. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The fall in average (unit) cost of production that occurs as a firm increases its scale of production / grows larger.
    1 mark: fall in average cost. 1 mark: as the firm increases its scale/size of output (long run).

(b) Any two, developed: technical – large automated machines produce much more output per worker, lowering cost per litre; purchasing (bulk-buying) – buying milk, packaging and fuel in large quantities gets discounts; financial – a large firm can borrow more cheaply from banks; marketing – advertising costs are spread over more output; managerial – specialist managers can be employed.

Q22Firms · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the average cost per litre before and after the expansion. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one diseconomy of scale that might occur if the company grows much larger. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before Rs 150 per litre; after Rs 120 per litre

    1 mark: identifies a diseconomy. 1 mark: explains why it raises average cost.

  2. (b) Explain two reasons why the cement company might make this merger. [4]

  2. (b) Explain two ways in which a trade union may benefit the firm that employs its members. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A fall of 12%
    New labour cost per unit = 1.10 ÷ 1.25 = 0.88 of the old cost (M1); a fall of 12% (A1).
  2. (b) e.g. one organisation to negotiate with saves time; agreements on productivity/new technology; communicates workers' views and reduces disputes; helps training and safety.
    2 marks each for two developed ways: employer bargains with one body rather than each worker; helps introduce change (new machines) with less resistance; channels complaints, reducing labour turnover and unofficial action; supports training and health and safety, raising productivity.

After a fire in a garment factory, a trade union campaigns for better fire exits and safety training.

  1. (a) Identify two aims of a trade union other than higher pay. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why an employer might agree to spend more on safety after the union's campaign. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. better health and safety; job security; shorter hours; better holidays/pensions; training; equal opportunities.
    1 mark each for any two valid aims.
  2. (b) e.g. to avoid a strike and lost output; to avoid fines or legal claims; to protect its reputation with buyers; fewer accidents mean less lost output and lower labour turnover.
    2 marks each for two developed reasons: avoid industrial action and the lost output/revenue it causes; avoid fines, closure or compensation claims under safety laws; foreign buyers/brands may stop orders from unsafe factories; fewer accidents reduce lost working time and costs; better morale lowers labour turnover and recruitment costs.
Q115Trade unions · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

2 000 workers at a fertiliser plant go on strike for 5 days. Each worker normally produces output worth Rs 12 000 a day.

  1. (a) Calculate the value of output lost during the strike. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two possible effects of the strike on the workers who take part. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 120 million (Rs 120 000 000)
    2 000 × 12 000 = Rs 24 million a day (M1); × 5 = Rs 120 million (A1).
  2. (b) Lose wages for the strike days; may win higher pay or better conditions; risk of losing their jobs if the firm loses customers; relations with managers may worsen.
    2 marks each for two developed effects: lose pay for 5 days, reducing household income; may gain a pay rise or better conditions afterwards; job losses if customers switch to imports/rivals; stress and poorer relations with employer; (credit) farmers face fertiliser shortages, harming public support.
Q116Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A teachers' union asks for an increase in its members' average monthly pay from Rs 90 000 to Rs 103 500. Prices in the country rose by 12% over the past year.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage pay rise that the union is asking for. [1]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a trade union is always beneficial for its members. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15%
    (103 500 − 90 000) ÷ 90 000 × 100 = 15% (B1).
  2. (b) Usually beneficial, but not always.
    Up to 3 marks for 'beneficial': collective bargaining wins higher pay (e.g. protecting real wages when prices rise 12%); better working conditions, safety and job security; legal advice and representation; training. Up to 3 marks for 'not always': members pay subscriptions; strikes mean lost pay; high wage rises may cause employers to cut jobs or replace workers with machines; a weak union may achieve little; members may disagree with the union's decisions. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q117Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Identify two ways in which trade unions might reduce the efficiency of firms. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not trade unions benefit an economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. strikes disrupt output; resisting new technology; restrictive practices/overmanning; wage rises above productivity raise costs.
    1 mark each for any two valid ways.
  2. (b) Benefits: fairer pay, better conditions and safety, more skilled and motivated workers, smoother introduction of change, reduced inequality. Costs: strikes lose output and exports, higher wage costs can raise prices and unemployment, resistance to new technology, may protect members at the expense of non-members. Conclusion depends on how unions behave and on the economic situation.
    Benefit (up to 4): protect workers from exploitation; higher wages raise spending; health and safety gains; training and productivity agreements; easier collective negotiation; less inequality. Not benefit (up to 4): strikes lose output and harm exporters; cost-push inflation; unemployment if wages exceed productivity; resistance to change reduces competitiveness; foreign investors may be deterred. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.
Q118Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks

Junior doctors in government hospitals in a country have gone on strike several times over pay. Some politicians want to ban strikes in essential services.

  1. (a) Identify two essential services other than health care. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the government should ban strikes by workers in essential services such as hospitals. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. fire service, police, electricity supply, water supply, ambulance services.
    1 mark each for any two valid examples.
  2. (b) For a ban: strikes put lives at risk, harm the poor who cannot pay for private care, and hit the whole economy. Against: workers lose their main bargaining power, pay and conditions may fall, staff may leave the profession or emigrate, causing shortages; a ban may be ignored. Alternatives: independent pay review bodies, arbitration. Conclusion: a ban could be justified only with fair alternative ways of setting pay.
    For (up to 4): risk to patients' lives; poor households most affected; public services have no alternative supplier; disruption to the wider economy. Against (up to 4): removes workers' strongest bargaining tool, so pay may fall behind; low morale, staff shortages, brain drain; may lead to unofficial action; basic right of workers; better to use arbitration/minimum service rules. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.
Q119Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain why trade union membership may fall during a recession. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a trade union is likely to win a pay rise for its members during a recession. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Many workers lose their jobs, so they leave the union or stop paying; others cannot afford the subscription or feel the union cannot protect them.
    Unemployment rises, so fewer workers are employed to be members (1); unemployed or low-income workers stop paying subscriptions / believe unions are weak during a recession (1).
  2. (b) Less likely: demand and profits are falling, firms are cutting jobs, unemployment means workers are easy to replace, and members fear losing jobs so are less willing to strike. More likely if: the union is large and well-organised, workers are skilled and hard to replace, the firm or sector is still profitable (e.g. essential services), or inflation is high and the case for a rise is strong. Conclusion: possible but usually small.
    Unlikely (up to 4): falling demand and profits limit the firm's ability to pay; unemployment is high, so firms can find replacement workers; members fear redundancy; government may freeze public-sector pay. Likely (up to 4): strong, large membership; skilled workers in short supply; some firms still profitable; good negotiating skills or public support; high inflation erodes real wages, strengthening the claim. Levels: 1–3 limited/one-sided; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides developed plus a justified conclusion.
Q120Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 9 marks

A car plant employs 3 000 union members. The union asks for a pay rise of Rs 2 000 a month for every member. The firm says it can afford to increase its total yearly wage bill by only Rs 54 million.

  1. (a) Calculate the yearly cost of the union's claim and the monthly pay rise per worker that the firm can afford. [3]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the union should accept the firm's offer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Claim costs Rs 72 million a year; the firm can afford Rs 1 500 a month per worker.
    3 000 × 2 000 × 12 = Rs 72 million (M1 A1); 54 000 000 ÷ 3 000 ÷ 12 = Rs 1 500 a month (B1).
  2. (b) Accept: avoids strike and lost wages, protects jobs if the firm really cannot afford more, and a Rs 1 500 rise is three-quarters of the claim. Reject: if profits are high or inflation is high, members' real pay may fall; holding out may win more. Conclusion depends on the firm's finances, inflation and members' views.
    Accept (up to 3): 75% of the claim; avoids loss of pay during a strike; higher costs could lead to job cuts or automation; keeps good relations. Reject (up to 3): firm may be able to pay more (check profits); rise may not keep up with inflation; union has strong bargaining power if workers are skilled; could negotiate other benefits. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
