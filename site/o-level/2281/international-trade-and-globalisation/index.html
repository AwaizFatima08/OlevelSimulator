Q1Globalisation, free trade and protection · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which of the following is a protectionist measure? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A an import quota
    A quota is a physical limit on the quantity of imports, so it protects domestic producers. B and C reduce trade barriers; D is a cause of globalisation, not a policy.
Q2Globalisation, free trade and protection · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define globalisation. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of globalisation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The process by which the world's economies become more closely connected through trade, investment, movement of people and information.
    1 mark: increasing integration/interdependence of economies; 1 mark: through trade, capital flows, MNCs, migration or technology.
  2. (b) Improvements in transport (containers, air freight) lowered costs of trade; the internet and mobile communications make it easier to do business across borders
    1 mark each for two causes + 1 mark each for explanation: cheaper/faster transport; better communications/internet; reduced trade barriers and trade agreements; growth of MNCs; more freedom of capital movements.
Q3Globalisation, free trade and protection · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain two reasons why a government may protect its domestic industries. [4]
  2. (b) State one method of protection other than a tariff. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) To protect infant industries until they can compete; to protect jobs in domestic industries
    1 mark each for two reasons + 1 mark each for explanation: infant industry argument; protect employment; prevent dumping; protect strategic industries (food, defence); reduce a current account deficit; raise tariff revenue.
  2. (b) e.g. a quota
    B1 any one: quota; embargo; subsidy to domestic producers; strict product standards/regulations; exchange controls.

Q5Globalisation, free trade and protection · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Before a tariff, the world price of steel is $600 per tonne. A country demands 10 million tonnes a year and domestic firms supply 4 million tonnes, so 6 million tonnes are imported. The government imposes a tariff of $100 per tonne. Domestic supply rises to 5 million tonnes and domestic demand falls to 9 million tonnes.

  1. (a) Calculate the quantity of steel imported after the tariff. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the government's revenue from the tariff. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two disadvantages of the tariff for the country. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4 million tonnes
    B1 9 − 5 = 4 million tonnes.
  2. (b) $400 million
    M1 4 million × $100; A1 $400 million.
  3. (c) Steel users pay higher prices, raising costs for construction and car firms; other countries may retaliate with tariffs on the country's exports
    1 mark each for two disadvantages + 1 mark each for explanation: higher prices for consumers/firms using steel; less choice; protected domestic firms may become inefficient; retaliation reduces exports; resources move to less efficient domestic producers.
Q6Globalisation, free trade and protection · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a multinational company (MNC). [2]
  2. (b) A foreign car manufacturer opens a factory in Pakistan. Explain two benefits to Pakistan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A firm that has its headquarters in one country and production or operations in more than one country.
    1 mark: firm operating/producing in more than one country; 1 mark: headquarters in one (home) country.
  2. (b) Creates jobs and incomes; brings new technology and skills; may increase exports and tax revenue
    1 mark each for two benefits + 1 mark each for explanation: employment and higher incomes; training and transfer of technology/management skills; tax revenue (corporation tax); exports improve the current account; more choice for consumers; local suppliers (parts makers) gain orders.
Q7Globalisation, free trade and protection · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define dumping. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a government might respond to dumping by imposing a tariff. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Selling a product in a foreign market at a price below its cost of production (or below the price in the home market).
    1 mark: selling exports abroad; 1 mark: at below cost/below home-market price.
  2. (b) Dumped imports undercut domestic firms, threatening output and jobs; a tariff raises the import price to a fair level so domestic firms can compete.
    1 mark: dumped goods unfairly cheap; 1 mark: domestic firms lose sales/close, jobs lost; 1 mark: tariff raises price of imports and restores competition (anti-dumping duty).
Q8Globalisation, free trade and protection · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a country should remove all its barriers to trade. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Free trade gives lower prices, more choice, competition and export opportunities through specialisation; but infant and strategic industries may need protection, jobs may be lost, dumping may occur and the country may become over-dependent on imports.
    Up to 5 marks for removing barriers: lower prices/more choice for consumers; cheaper imported raw materials for firms; competition improves efficiency; specialisation raises world output; other countries more likely to lower their barriers. Up to 5 marks against: infant industries cannot compete; structural unemployment in declining industries; dumping; loss of tariff revenue; strategic industries (food, energy) at risk; current account deficit. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q9Globalisation, free trade and protection · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

After joining a regional free trade area, a country's exports rose from $25 billion to $31 billion a year and its imports rose from $30 billion to $39 billion a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in the country's exports. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in the country's imports. [1]
  3. (c) Analyse why joining the free trade area might increase the country's imports by more than its exports. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 24 %
    B1 (31 − 25) ÷ 25 × 100 = 24 %.
  2. (b) 30 %
    B1 (39 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = 30 %.
  3. (c) Removing its own tariffs makes imports cheaper so demand rises; partner countries' firms may be more efficient; domestic firms may lack the capacity or quality to expand exports quickly; higher incomes also raise demand for imports.
    1 mark: tariffs on imports removed, lowering import prices; 1 mark: consumers/firms switch from domestic goods to cheaper imports; 1 mark: domestic exporters face strong competition/slow to raise capacity or quality; 1 mark: other reason, e.g. imported capital goods needed by exporters, rising incomes, partners already had low tariffs.
Q10Globalisation, free trade and protection · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a quota and an embargo. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether a quota on imported cars will protect jobs in a country's car industry. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) A quota limits the quantity of a product that can be imported; an embargo is a complete ban on trade in a product or with a country.
    1 mark: quota – limit on quantity; 1 mark: embargo – total ban.
  2. (b) Restricting imports shifts demand to domestic producers, protecting jobs in the short run; but higher car prices reduce total sales, protected firms may stay inefficient, retaliation can cut exports and jobs, and car-dealing and service jobs linked to imports may be lost.
    Up to 4 marks for protection: fewer imports, consumers buy domestic cars; domestic output and employment rise/are maintained; time for industry to become competitive. Up to 4 marks against: car prices rise so fewer cars sold overall; lack of competition → inefficiency, jobs at risk later; retaliation hurts exporters; jobs lost in import/sales businesses; domestic firms may use imported parts or automate. Max 6; judgement needed for top marks.
Q11Current account of the balance of payments · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which of the following is recorded as a credit (inflow) in the trade in services section of a country's current account? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A spending by foreign tourists visiting the country
    Foreign tourists' spending is an export of services, so it is a credit. B is a debit in trade in goods; C is a primary income debit; D is an import of services (debit).
Q12Current account of the balance of payments · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State the four components of the current account of the balance of payments. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by a current account deficit. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Trade in goods; trade in services; primary income; secondary income
    1 mark for any two; 2 marks for all four.
  2. (b) When the value of money flowing out of the country on the current account (imports of goods and services, income paid abroad) is greater than the value flowing in.
    1 mark: debits/outflows exceed credits/inflows; 1 mark: on trade in goods and services plus primary and secondary income.
Q13Current account of the balance of payments · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) For each item, state whether it is recorded in primary income or secondary income, and whether it is a credit or a debit for Pakistan:(i) profits sent home by a foreign-owned company operating in Pakistan(ii) money sent home by Pakistani workers in the Gulf(iii) aid given by Pakistan's government to a country hit by an earthquake(iv) interest received by Pakistani residents on savings held abroad [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) primary income, debit; (ii) secondary income, credit; (iii) secondary income, debit; (iv) primary income, credit
    1 mark for each item correctly classified (both section and credit/debit).

Q15Current account of the balance of payments · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Current account data for a country in one year ($ billion):exports of goods 28; imports of goods 45exports of services 7; imports of services 6primary income credits 1; primary income debits 5secondary income (net, mainly workers' remittances) +30

  1. (a) Calculate the balance of trade in goods. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the current account balance. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why workers' remittances are important for this country's current account. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) −$17 billion
    B1 28 − 45 = −17.
  2. (b) +$10 billion (surplus)
    M1 (28 − 45) + (7 − 6) + (1 − 5) + 30; A1 −17 + 1 − 4 + 30 = +$10 bn.
  3. (c) Remittances (secondary income) of $30 bn more than offset the large deficit on trade in goods, turning the current account into a surplus and providing foreign currency to pay for imports.
    1 mark: remittances recorded as secondary income credit; 1 mark: they cover the trade in goods deficit (30 > 17)/without them the current account would be in deficit (−$20 bn); 1 mark: provide foreign currency/support the exchange rate/raise household incomes.
Q16Current account of the balance of payments · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two causes of a current account deficit. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one possible consequence of a long-term current account deficit. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) High domestic incomes raise spending on imports; high inflation or an appreciated currency makes exports uncompetitive
    1 mark each for two causes + 1 mark each for explanation: rising incomes → more imports; high relative inflation → exports dearer; exchange rate appreciation; low productivity/poor quality of exports; dependence on imported fuel or capital goods; large profit outflows to foreign firms (primary income).
  2. (b) e.g. pressure for the currency to depreciate, raising import prices and inflation
    1 mark for consequence + 1 mark for explanation: currency depreciation/reserves fall; borrowing from abroad and growing foreign debt; loss of jobs in industries competing with imports; lower aggregate demand.
Q17Current account of the balance of payments · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a current account deficit is always a problem for a country. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) It can be a problem if long-lasting, financed by debt, or caused by uncompetitive industries; but it may be short-term, reflect imports of capital goods that raise future output, or be offset by financial inflows, and consumers gain from more imports.
    Up to 5 marks for problem: debt and interest payments; reserves run down; downward pressure on currency → imported inflation; lost output and jobs; sign of uncompetitive industries. Up to 5 marks for not always: short-term or small relative to GDP; imports of machinery boost future growth and exports; financed by FDI; higher living standards from imported goods; may correct automatically under floating exchange rate. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q18Current account of the balance of payments · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a contractionary (deflationary) fiscal policy could reduce a current account deficit. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one disadvantage of using this policy to reduce the deficit. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Higher taxes or lower government spending reduce disposable income and aggregate demand, so spending on imports falls; lower inflation also makes exports more competitive and firms may sell more abroad.
    1 mark: raise taxes/cut government spending; 1 mark: lower disposable income/aggregate demand; 1 mark: less spending on imports; 1 mark: reduced inflationary pressure improves price competitiveness of exports/domestic firms seek export markets.
  2. (b) It reduces spending on domestic goods too, causing lower economic growth and higher unemployment.
    1 mark: disadvantage (lower growth, unemployment, cuts to public services, unpopular); 1 mark: explanation.
Q19Current account of the balance of payments · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A country has GDP of $360 billion and a current account deficit of $14.4 billion. Its exports of goods are $40 billion and its imports of goods are $52 billion.

  1. (a) Calculate the current account deficit as a percentage of GDP. [2]
  2. (b) The value of exports of goods rises by 10 % while imports of goods are unchanged. Calculate the new balance of trade in goods. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a country's export revenue might fall even though the volume of its exports rises. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4 %
    M1 14.4 ÷ 360 × 100; A1 4 %.
  2. (b) −$8 billion
    M1 new exports 40 × 1.1 = $44 bn; A1 44 − 52 = −$8 bn.
  3. (c) If world prices of its exports fall by a larger percentage than the rise in volume, revenue (price × quantity) falls.
    1 mark: export prices fall (e.g. commodity prices or depreciation in foreign-currency terms); 1 mark: price fall proportionally larger than volume rise, so price × quantity falls.

Q21Foreign exchange rates · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define an exchange rate. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of a depreciation of a currency in a floating exchange rate system. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The price of one currency in terms of another currency.
    1 mark: price/value of a currency; 1 mark: expressed in terms of another currency.
  2. (b) A fall in demand for the currency, e.g. because exports fall; a rise in supply of the currency, e.g. because imports rise or interest rates are cut and savers move money abroad
    1 mark each for two causes + 1 mark each for explanation (via demand/supply of the currency): fall in exports/foreign demand; rise in imports; lower interest rates; higher relative inflation; fall in foreign direct investment; speculation.
Q22Foreign exchange rates · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The exchange rate of a country's currency, the rupee, changes from Rs 280 = $1 to Rs 300 = $1. An imported machine part costs $50. Which statement is correct? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A the rupee has depreciated and the part now costs Rs 15 000
    More rupees are needed to buy $1, so the rupee has depreciated. Cost = 50 × 300 = Rs 15 000 (up from 50 × 280 = Rs 14 000). B gets the price right but the direction wrong.
Q23Foreign exchange rates · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A machine made in Germany is priced at €20 000. The exchange rate changes from €1 = $1.10 to €1 = $1.25.

  1. (a) Calculate the cost of the machine in US dollars before and after the change in the exchange rate. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in the dollar price of the machine. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [1]
  3. (c) Explain the likely effect of this exchange rate change on the quantity of European machinery bought by US firms. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $22 000 before; $25 000 after
    B1 20 000 × 1.10 = $22 000; B1 20 000 × 1.25 = $25 000.
  2. (b) 13.6 %
    B1 (25 000 − 22 000) ÷ 22 000 × 100 = 13.6 %.
  3. (c) The machines are dearer in dollars, so US firms buy fewer (or switch to other suppliers), depending on price elasticity of demand.
    1 mark: higher dollar price; 1 mark: quantity demanded falls/switch to US or other suppliers (more so if demand is elastic).
Q24Foreign exchange rates · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a floating exchange rate system and a fixed exchange rate system. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages of a fixed exchange rate for a country. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Floating: the rate is set by market demand for and supply of the currency. Fixed: the government or central bank sets the rate at a chosen level and maintains it.
    1 mark: floating determined by market forces; 1 mark: fixed set and maintained by the government/central bank (by buying or selling currency).
  2. (b) Certainty for exporters and importers, encouraging trade and investment; discipline to keep inflation low
    1 mark each for two advantages + 1 mark each for explanation: certainty/less risk for traders and foreign investors; easier planning of prices and costs; prevents speculation; forces government to control inflation to stay competitive; stable import prices.
Q25Foreign exchange rates · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a depreciation of its currency will benefit an economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Exports become cheaper and imports dearer, which may raise output, jobs and improve the current account; but imported raw materials and fuel cost more, causing cost-push inflation, real incomes fall, foreign-currency debts rise, and benefits depend on price elasticity of demand for exports and imports.
    Up to 5 marks for benefits: exports cheaper in foreign currency → more sold; imports dearer → consumers switch to domestic products; higher aggregate demand, output and employment; current account improves; more tourism. Up to 5 marks for costs: imported inflation (fuel, raw materials); lower living standards; foreign debts more expensive to repay; little benefit if demand for exports/imports is price inelastic or other countries retaliate. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q26Foreign exchange rates · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hotel in Hunza charges Rs 18 000 per night. The exchange rate changes from 1 Malaysian ringgit (RM) = Rs 60 to RM 1 = Rs 66.

  1. (a) Calculate the cost per night of the hotel, in ringgit, before and after the exchange rate change. Give the second answer to the nearest ringgit. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the likely effect of this exchange rate change on tourism to Pakistan and on Pakistan's current account. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) RM 300 before; RM 273 after
    B1 18 000 ÷ 60 = RM 300; B1 18 000 ÷ 66 = RM 272.7 ≈ RM 273.
  2. (b) Holidays in Pakistan become cheaper for Malaysian tourists, so more visit; tourist spending is an export of services, so the trade in services balance and current account improve (if demand is price elastic).
    1 mark: rupee has depreciated against the ringgit/stay cheaper in ringgit; 1 mark: more foreign tourists; 1 mark: tourism spending is a services export/credit; 1 mark: current account improves, depending on elasticity or other changes (e.g. Pakistani travel to Malaysia becomes dearer).
Q27Specialisation and free trade · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is an advantage to a country of specialising in the products it can make most efficiently and trading with other countries? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A it can achieve a higher total output from its resources
    Specialisation allows resources to be used where they are most productive, increasing output; the country trades for other goods. B and C are the opposite – specialisation increases dependence and narrows the range of domestic industries.
Q28Specialisation and free trade · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define specialisation at a national level. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two disadvantages for a country of specialising in a narrow range of products. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) When a country concentrates on producing the goods and services it is best at producing (and trades for others).
    1 mark: country concentrates on a narrow range of products; 1 mark: those it produces most efficiently/trades for the rest.
  2. (b) If world demand or prices for its product fall, incomes and jobs fall sharply; it becomes dependent on imports of other goods, which may be disrupted
    1 mark each for two disadvantages + 1 mark each for explanation: risk from fall in world demand/price; structural unemployment if the industry declines; dependence on imports (supply disruption, war); depletion of resources; competition from new low-cost producers.
Q29Specialisation and free trade · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Garments make up 82 % of the exports of a South Asian country whose total exports are worth $55 billion a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the value of the country's garment exports. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two benefits to the country of specialising in garment production. [4]
  3. (c) Explain one risk of this degree of specialisation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $45.1 billion
    B1 0.82 × 55 = $45.1 bn.
  2. (b) Economies of scale lower average costs, making garments competitive; large export earnings and employment, especially for women workers
    1 mark each for two benefits + 1 mark each for explanation: economies of scale; workers become skilled/productive; export revenue/foreign currency; employment and income; attracts foreign investment.
  3. (c) Exports depend on one industry, so a fall in world demand or a rise in competition from lower-cost countries would cut export earnings and jobs.
    1 mark: risk identified (fall in world demand, new competitors, trade barriers abroad, rising wages); 1 mark: explanation of effect on export earnings/jobs.
Q30Specialisation and free trade · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether consumers benefit when their country specialises and trades freely with other countries. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Consumers gain from lower prices, wider choice and better quality through competition and economies of scale; but may lose if jobs and incomes fall in declining industries, if supply is disrupted, if quality or safety standards are lower, or if foreign firms gain monopoly power.
    Up to 5 marks for benefits: lower prices from efficient production/economies of scale; wider choice including goods not produced at home; competition raises quality; higher incomes from export industries. Up to 5 marks against: consumers are also workers – job losses in industries facing imports; vulnerability to supply disruptions and price shocks; unsafe imports; dumping may eventually reduce competition; depreciation may make imports expensive. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
