Q1Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) State three problems faced by small farmers in Pakistan. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why crop yields per hectare in Pakistan are lower than in many other countries. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any three: small and fragmented holdings; shortage of irrigation water; waterlogging and salinity; lack of credit / dependence on money-lenders; poor-quality seed; high cost of fertiliser and pesticides; pests and diseases; low prices from middlemen; floods and droughts; lack of storage; low literacy / lack of advice.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Many farmers use uncertified seed, so plants are weaker and give less grain; irrigation water is wasted by flood irrigation and unlined canals, so crops at the tail end get too little; fertiliser is expensive, so poor farmers apply too little; waterlogging and salinity reduce the fertility of the soil; lack of training means modern methods are not used.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
Q2Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) The Green Revolution began in Pakistan in the 1960s. State three inputs that it introduced or increased. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the Green Revolution benefited large landowners more than small farmers. You should develop your answer. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any three: high-yielding varieties of seed (e.g. of wheat and rice); chemical fertilisers; pesticides; tube wells for irrigation; tractors and machinery.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) The new seeds only gave high yields with plenty of fertiliser, water and pesticides, which cost money; large landowners could afford these inputs and tube wells, or get bank loans, so their yields and profits rose most; small farmers often could not afford the full package.
    1 mark per point; 3 marks for a developed explanation.
Q3Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A farmer owns 12 acres. Under inheritance, the land is divided equally among his four sons. Later each son's share is divided equally among his three children.

  1. (a) Calculate the area of land each grandchild receives. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two problems caused by the division of land into small, scattered plots. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest one way of reducing the fragmentation of land holdings. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1 acre
    12 ÷ 4 = 3 acres per son; 3 ÷ 3 = 1 acre per grandchild.
  2. (b) Plots are too small to use tractors or other machines efficiently, so costs per acre are high; time and land are wasted walking between scattered plots and on boundary ridges and paths; small plots give too little output to feed a family or produce a surplus to sell, keeping families poor; disputes over boundaries and water turns.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
  3. (c) Consolidation of holdings (exchanging plots so each farmer has one compact block) / co-operative farming.
    1 mark.
Q4Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Complete the following information about land reforms: in 1959 the ceiling on individual holdings was set at ____ acres of irrigated land; in 1972 it was reduced to ____ acres of irrigated land. [2]
  2. (b) By what percentage was the irrigated-land ceiling reduced between 1959 and 1972? [1]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why the land reforms had only a limited effect on the distribution of land. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 500 acres (1959); 150 acres (1972).
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) 70%
    (500 − 150) ÷ 500 × 100 = 70%.
  3. (c) Landlords transferred land into the names of relatives and servants before the reforms, so they kept control; ceilings were per individual, so families kept large estates; much of the land taken was poor, barren land; exemptions (e.g. for orchards, stud farms) and weak enforcement by officials linked to landlords.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
Q5Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Two wheat farms in the same district in one season (hypothetical data):Farm X: 1.5 hectares, produced 4.2 tonnesFarm Y: 3.0 hectares, produced 9.9 tonnes

  1. (a) Calculate the yield of wheat per hectare on each farm. Give your answers in tonnes per hectare. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest three reasons why Farm Y has a higher yield than Farm X. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Farm X 2.8 t/ha; Farm Y 3.3 t/ha
    4.2 ÷ 1.5 = 2.8; 9.9 ÷ 3.0 = 3.3. 1 mark each.
  2. (b) Any three: Farm Y uses certified / high-yielding seed; applies more or better-timed fertiliser; has a tube well, so a reliable water supply; uses laser land levelling; has less waterlogged or saline soil; uses machinery to sow on time; farmer has more training and credit.
    1 mark each.
Q6Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A farmer needs to borrow Rs 150,000 for six months to buy seed and fertiliser. A village money-lender charges simple interest of 3% per month. An agricultural bank such as ZTBL charges simple interest of 12% per year (hypothetical rates).

  1. (a) Calculate the interest the farmer would pay to the money-lender. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the interest the farmer would pay on a bank loan, and how much he would save by borrowing from the bank. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why many small farmers still borrow from money-lenders or middlemen (arthis) rather than banks. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 27,000
    150,000 × 0.03 × 6 = Rs 27,000.
  2. (b) Bank interest Rs 9,000; saving Rs 18,000
    150,000 × 0.12 × 6 ÷ 12 = Rs 9,000 (M1); 27,000 − 9,000 = Rs 18,000 (A1).
  3. (c) Banks require land titles or other collateral, which tenants and small farmers often lack; bank procedures need paperwork, which is hard for farmers with little education; banks are far away in towns, while the money-lender is in the village and lends quickly; the arthi also buys the crop, so the loan is repaid at harvest without forms.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
Q7Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Wheat area and production in a country over three years (hypothetical data):Year 1: area 8.8 million hectares, production 24.0 million tonnesYear 2: area 9.0 million hectares, production 26.2 million tonnesYear 3: area 9.2 million hectares, production 28.0 million tonnes

  1. (a) Calculate the yield (tonnes per hectare) for each year. Give your answers to two decimal places. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in production from Year 1 to Year 3. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Use the data to explain whether the increase in production was mainly due to a larger area or to higher yields. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1 2.73, Year 2 2.91, Year 3 3.04 t/ha
    Production ÷ area for each year; 1 mark each.
  2. (b) 16.7%
    (28.0 − 24.0) ÷ 24.0 × 100 (M1) = 16.7% (A1).
  3. (c) Area rose by only about 4.5% (8.8 → 9.2 million ha) but production rose by 16.7%, and yield rose from 2.73 to 3.04 t/ha, so higher yields were the main cause.
    1 mark for comparing area change with production change; 1 mark for using yield figures.
Q8Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain two advantages of mechanisation (using machines such as tractors and combine harvesters) for farmers. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) Read these two views:View A: 'Mechanisation is the best way to increase agricultural output in Pakistan.'View B: 'Mechanisation does more harm than good in rural Pakistan.'Which view do you agree with more? Give reasons for your answer, referring to examples you have studied. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ploughing and sowing are faster, so crops are sown at the best time and yields rise; harvesting by combine reduces losses from rain and shattering; fewer labourers are needed, cutting labour costs; laser levelling saves irrigation water.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
  2. (b) View A: timely sowing and harvesting; higher yields and less waste; allows double cropping (e.g. rice then wheat); saves water with laser levelling. View B: machines are too expensive for most small farmers, and holdings are too small and fragmented to use them; landless labourers lose jobs and migrate to cities; burning of rice stubble left by combines adds to smog; dependence on imported fuel and spare parts. Judgement required.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points, one side or generic. L2 (3–4): developed points on one side, or simple points on both sides. L3 (5–6): developed points on both sides with a named Pakistani example and a reasoned judgement.
Q9Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain how waterlogging and salinity reduce crop output. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) Evaluate the extent to which solving water problems would increase agricultural output in Pakistan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Seepage from unlined canals and over-irrigation raises the water table, so roots are surrounded by water and cannot get air, and plants die; in hot weather water rises by capillary action and evaporates, leaving salt on the surface; salt makes it hard for plants to take in water and is toxic, so yields fall and land may be abandoned.
    Up to 4 marks for a developed explanation of both waterlogging and salinity (max 2 if only one).
  2. (b) Water problems are central: most crops depend on irrigation; water shortages at canal tails; waterlogging and salinity have damaged large areas; lining canals, drainage tube wells and drip/sprinkler irrigation would raise yields. Other limits on output: small fragmented holdings, poor seed, lack of credit, pests (e.g. locusts in 2019–20), low prices and middlemen, lack of storage, floods. Judgement required.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points, one side or generic. L2 (3–4): developed points on one side, or simple points on both sides. L3 (5–6): developed points on both sides with a named Pakistani example and a reasoned judgement.
Q10Agricultural problems, land reform and output · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain two ways in which the floods of 2022 affected agriculture in Sindh. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) Read these two views:View A: 'Building more dams and flood embankments is the best way to protect farming from floods.'View B: 'Farmers must adapt to climate change, for example with new crop varieties, better forecasts and insurance.'Which view do you agree with more? Give reasons for your answer, referring to examples you have studied. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Standing crops such as cotton and rice were destroyed, so farmers lost their income for the season; floodwater stayed on fields for months, delaying the sowing of the next wheat crop; livestock drowned and fodder was lost; canals, roads and storage were damaged, so inputs and crops could not be moved.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
  2. (b) View A: dams store floodwater for irrigation and electricity; embankments protect towns and fields; reduce peak flows. View B: dams are very expensive and slow to build and silt up; extreme rainfall (as in 2022) can overwhelm structures; drought- and flood-tolerant seed, changed sowing dates, early warning systems, crop insurance and keeping floodplains clear reduce losses cheaply. Judgement, possibly that both are needed.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points, one side or generic. L2 (3–4): developed points on one side, or simple points on both sides. L3 (5–6): developed points on both sides with a named Pakistani example and a reasoned judgement.

Q23Types of farming and farming systems · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how terraces are made and used in the northern mountains. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages of terrace farming in mountain areas. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Steep hillsides are cut into a series of flat steps; each step is held up by a stone or earth wall; water from streams or glacial meltwater is led along small channels to the terraces; crops such as wheat, maize, potatoes and fruit trees are grown on them.
    1 mark per point up to 3.
  2. (b) Flat steps slow down run-off, so soil erosion is reduced; water stays on the land longer, so crops get more moisture; they create flat land where there would otherwise be none, increasing the area that can be farmed.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
Q24Types of farming and farming systems · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A farmer grows wheat on 5 acres. Each acre gives 1,600 kg of wheat, sold at Rs 95 per kg. Costs per acre are (hypothetical data): seed Rs 9,000; fertiliser Rs 24,000; irrigation water Rs 6,000; ploughing, harvesting and labour Rs 28,000; pesticide Rs 5,000.

  1. (a) Calculate the income from selling the wheat grown on one acre. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total cost per acre and the profit per acre. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the profit from the whole 5-acre farm. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest two ways the farmer could increase this profit. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 152,000
    1,600 × 95 = Rs 152,000.
  2. (b) Cost Rs 72,000; profit Rs 80,000
    9,000 + 24,000 + 6,000 + 28,000 + 5,000 = Rs 72,000 (M1); 152,000 − 72,000 = Rs 80,000 (A1).

    1 mark each.

    1 mark each.
  3. (c) Milk and meat improve the family diet; animals can be sold in an emergency, acting as savings.
    1 mark point + 1 mark development.

A broiler farmer near Rawalpindi buys 5,000 day-old chicks. 4% of them die before they are sold. The birds that survive weigh an average of 2.1 kg when sold (hypothetical data).

  1. (a) Calculate the number of chicks that survive to be sold. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total mass of the birds sold. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why commercial poultry farming has grown quickly in Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4,800
    5,000 × 0.96 = 4,800.
  2. (b) 10,080 kg
    4,800 × 2.1 = 10,080 kg.
  3. (c) Broilers are ready for market in about six weeks, so farmers get a quick return on their money; chicken is cheaper than beef or mutton, so demand from a growing urban population is high; sheds need little land, so farms can be set up near cities; feed mills and hatcheries supply chicks and feed.

Q28Livestock farming · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) What is meant by 'transhumance'? [2]
  2. (b) Explain why herders in the mountains of Gilgit-Baltistan and northern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa move their animals during the year. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) State one problem faced by nomadic herders today. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The seasonal movement of herders and their animals between summer pastures in the mountains and winter pastures in the lowlands.
    1 mark seasonal movement; 1 mark between high and low pastures.
  2. (b) In winter high pastures are covered in snow and very cold, so animals are taken down to valleys and plains where there is grazing; in summer lowland grass dries up in the heat while melting snow leaves fresh grass in high alpine pastures, so herds move up; moving prevents overgrazing in one place.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
  3. (c) Loss of grazing land to farming or forests being protected; overgrazing; long distances from schools and health care; disputes over grazing rights; droughts.
    1 mark.
Q29Livestock farming · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A dairy farm near Lahore keeps 40 buffaloes that give an average of 7 litres of milk a day each, and 20 crossbred cows that give an average of 15 litres a day each. Milk is sold at Rs 160 per litre (hypothetical data).

  1. (a) Calculate the farm's total daily milk production. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the average daily milk yield per animal. Give your answer to two decimal places. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the farm's income from milk in a 30-day month. [2]
  4. (d) Explain two reasons why milk yields per animal in Pakistan are generally low. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 580 litres
    40 × 7 + 20 × 15 = 280 + 300 = 580 L.
  2. (b) 9.67 litres
    580 ÷ 60 = 9.67 L.
  3. (c) Rs 2,784,000
    580 × 30 = 17,400 L (M1); × Rs 160 = Rs 2,784,000 (A1).
  4. (d) Animals are often underfed with poor-quality fodder, so they produce less milk; little veterinary care, so diseases such as foot-and-mouth reduce yields; most animals are of low-yielding local stock, with little use of improved breeding; heat stress in summer without shade or cooling.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
Q30Livestock farming · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain two problems in the marketing of milk in Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) Read these two views:View A: 'Large commercial dairy farms are the best way to increase milk production in Pakistan.'View B: 'Helping small farmers with one or two animals would do more for the country.'Which view do you agree with more? Give reasons for your answer, referring to examples you have studied. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most milk is sold loose through middlemen (gawalas), who pay farmers low prices; there is little cold storage or chilling, so milk spoils quickly in summer; milk is often adulterated with water or chemicals, causing health risks; long distances from villages to cities.
    1 mark for each of two reasons + 1 mark each for development (a consequence or 'which leads to').
  2. (b) View A: commercial farms use improved breeds, balanced feed, veterinary care and machines, giving much higher yields; they can supply processors with chilled, safe milk; they attract investment. View B: millions of rural families keep a few animals, so support (vaccination, training, collection centres, chilling units, microloans) would raise incomes widely and reduce poverty, especially for women; commercial farms need capital, imported breeds, and may suffer heat stress. Judgement required.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points, one side or generic. L2 (3–4): developed points on one side, or simple points on both sides. L3 (5–6): developed points on both sides with a named Pakistani example and a reasoned judgement.
