Q1Water supply, shortage and pollution · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

In an illustrative example, a country has about 230 billion m³ of renewable water a year and a population of about 250 million. Countries with less than 1,000 m³ per person per year are said to face water scarcity.

  1. (a) Calculate the water available per person per year. [2]
  2. (b) State whether this country faces water scarcity, giving a reason. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why water available per person is falling in Pakistan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 230 billion ÷ 250 million = 920 m³ per person.
    M1 correct division with units; A1 920 m³.
  2. (b) Yes: 920 m³ is below 1,000 m³ per person.
    1 mark.
  3. (c) The population is growing rapidly, so the same water is shared among more people. Reservoirs are losing storage to silt and little new storage has been built, while groundwater is being over-pumped faster than it is recharged.
    1 + 1 development each: population growth; silting of reservoirs; limited new dams; over-pumping of groundwater; wastage; climate change and reduced glacier flows.
Q2Water supply, shortage and pollution · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State two sources of drinking water for people in Pakistan's cities. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of water pollution in rivers near large cities. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two: rivers/canals (e.g. Indus via Keenjhar Lake for Karachi); reservoirs/dams (Simly, Khanpur, Hub); groundwater from tube wells; bottled or tanker water.
    1 each.
  2. (b) Untreated sewage from homes is emptied straight into rivers and drains because there are few treatment plants. Factories such as tanneries and textile mills discharge chemicals and dyes without treatment, poisoning the water.
    1 + 1 development each: raw sewage; industrial effluent; solid waste dumping; agricultural run-off (fertilisers, pesticides).

Q5Water supply, shortage and pollution · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Name two diseases that can be caught from drinking contaminated water. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which households can make their drinking water safer. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cholera; typhoid.
    1 mark each: diarrhoea; cholera; typhoid; hepatitis A or E; dysentery; polio.

Q6Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A survey of 400 households in a village in southern Punjab found that 136 had piped water at home, 212 used a hand pump or tube well and the rest collected water from a canal or pond.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of households with piped water. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate how many households collected water from a canal or pond. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two health risks for the households that use canal or pond water. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 136 ÷ 400 × 100 = 34%.
    B1 34%.
  2. (b) 400 − 136 − 212 = 52.
    B1 52.
  3. (c) The water may carry bacteria and viruses from sewage and animals, causing diarrhoea, cholera and typhoid, especially in children. Standing water also breeds mosquitoes, spreading malaria and dengue.
    1 + 1 development each: water-borne diseases; mosquito-borne disease; chemical contamination from farm run-off.
Q7Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

  2. (b) Suggest two ways to improve water supply to a large city like Karachi. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The city's population has grown faster than its water supply, which depends mainly on the Indus (via Keenjhar Lake) and Hub Dam. Much water is lost through leaking pipes and illegal connections, and a 'tanker mafia' sells water at high prices.

Q10Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In an illustrative example, a city uses 500 million litres of water a day. Industry produces waste water equal to 30% of this, and 90% of the industrial waste water is released without treatment.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of industrial waste water produced each day, and the volume that is not treated. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two effects of untreated waste water from tanneries, such as those around Kasur, on the local area. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest two ways of reducing this pollution. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Industrial: 500 × 30% = 150 million litres; untreated: 150 × 90% = 135 million litres
    B1 150 million litres; B1 135 million litres.
  2. (b) Tannery waste contains chromium and other chemicals, which seep into groundwater, so hand-pump water becomes unsafe and causes skin and stomach diseases; waste channels carry polluted water onto fields, so crops and soil are contaminated; drains and rivers become dark and smelly, killing fish.
    1 mark for each effect + 1 for development. Effects: groundwater contamination (chromium); illness (skin, kidney, stomach); polluted crops/soils; fish deaths/river pollution; bad smell, lower quality of life.
  3. (c) Build a combined effluent treatment plant for the tanneries; fine factories that release untreated waste.
    1 mark each: treatment plants (individual or combined); enforce environmental laws/fines; cleaner tanning methods/chrome recovery; relocate tanneries to an industrial estate with treatment; monitoring.
Q11Water supply, shortage and pollution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

In an illustrative example, a coastal town in Balochistan needs 12 million gallons of water a day. A desalination plant supplies 5 million gallons a day.

  1. (a) What is meant by desalination? [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of the town's daily needs met by the plant. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why desalination provides only a small part of Pakistan's water supply. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Removing salt from seawater (or brackish water) to make fresh water.
    B1 removing salt from sea/brackish water.
  2. (b) 5 ÷ 12 × 100 = 41.7%
    M1 5 ÷ 12 × 100; A1 41.7%.
  3. (c) Desalination plants use large amounts of energy, and electricity in Pakistan is expensive and often in short supply, so the water is costly; the plants are expensive to build and maintain; they can only serve places near the sea, such as Karachi and Gwadar, and the very salty waste (brine) must be disposed of without harming marine life.
    1 mark for each reason + 1 for development. Reasons: high energy use/cost; high building and maintenance costs; only practical on the coast; brine disposal; breakdowns/lack of skilled staff.
Q12Water supply, shortage and pollution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) affects water supply in Pakistan. [4]
  2. (b) 'Pakistan's water shortage is caused more by poor management than by a lack of water.' Do you agree? Give reasons to support your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The treaty gave the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan. To replace the eastern river water, Pakistan built link canals, Mangla and Tarbela dams and barrages under the Indus Basin Project; the Ravi and Sutlej now carry little water through Pakistan except in floods.
    1 + 1 development each, max 4.
  2. (b) Management: large losses from unlined canals; flood irrigation wastes water; little storage (only about a month of river flow); low water charges encourage waste; pollution makes water unusable. Natural/limited supply: arid climate, low rainfall in most of the country, dependence on one river system, variable monsoon and glacier melt, rising population. Judgement required.
    Levels: L1 (1–2) simple points; L2 (3–4) both sides developed with judgement.
Q13Water supply, shortage and pollution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain why rural women and children are most affected by water shortages. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which climate change may affect Pakistan's future water supply. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) In many villages it is their task to fetch water, so they walk long distances carrying heavy loads. This takes time from school and paid work, and lack of clean water at home increases illness among children.

  2. (b) Higher temperatures are melting glaciers faster, giving more water at first but less in the long term as glaciers shrink. Rainfall is becoming more variable, with more droughts and more intense floods, making supply less reliable.
    1 + 1 development each: glacier melt; changes to monsoon; higher evaporation; more floods/droughts.

Q15Water supply, shortage and pollution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In an illustrative example, a town of 250,000 people receives 30 million litres of piped water a day.

  1. (a) Calculate the average amount of water supplied per person per day. [2]
  2. (b) 'Reducing water pollution is more important than increasing the amount of water supplied for improving health in Pakistan's cities.' Evaluate this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 30,000,000 ÷ 250,000 = 120 litres per person per day
    M1 30,000,000 ÷ 250,000; A1 120 litres.
  2. (b) For: much illness in cities comes from contaminated water – sewage leaking into supply pipes, untreated industrial waste and polluted groundwater – so cleaning water and treating sewage would directly reduce diarrhoea, typhoid and hepatitis; more water that is still dirty would not help. Against: many poor areas get too little water, so people buy from tankers or use unsafe sources; enough water is needed for hygiene (washing hands, sanitation) which also prevents disease; supply and quality must be improved together. Q16Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a karez works. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages of the karez system in Balochistan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A karez is an underground channel dug into the foot of a hill or alluvial fan. It carries groundwater by gravity to the surface at a lower level. Vertical shafts at intervals are used for digging and cleaning; water is used in fields and villages, for example in Balochistan.
    1 per point, max 3.
  2. (b) Because the water flows underground, little is lost by evaporation in the hot, dry climate. It works by gravity, so no fuel or electricity is needed and it can supply water all year.
    1 + 1 development each: low evaporation; no pumping cost; reliable supply; community-managed.
Q17Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) What is the difference between a perennial canal and an inundation canal? [2]
  2. (b) Explain two disadvantages of inundation canals. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A perennial canal takes water from a barrage or dam and flows all year; an inundation canal flows only when the river is high in summer floods.
    1 each.
  2. (b) They have no water in winter when the river is low, so rabi crops cannot be irrigated. Their supply depends on the size of the flood: a poor flood brings too little water and a big flood can damage fields.
    1 + 1 development each.
Q18Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Name two traditional methods of lifting water from wells in Pakistan. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why tube wells have become a common method of irrigation. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two: Persian wheel; shaduf; charsa (leather bag pulled by animals); hand pump.

  2. (b) They can supply water whenever the farmer needs it, including when canals are closed. They supplement canal water in areas at the tail end of canals and also help lower high water tables. Cheap diesel and electric pumps and government schemes encouraged them.

Q20Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) What is meant by barani farming? [1]
  2. (b) Describe how rod kohi (hill-torrent) irrigation works in areas such as Dera Ismail Khan and Dera Ghazi Khan. [3]
  3. (c) State one disadvantage of rod kohi irrigation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Farming that depends on rainfall, without irrigation.
    B1 rain-fed farming.
  2. (b) When heavy rain falls in the hills, floodwater rushes down the torrent beds; farmers build earth embankments and channels to divert this water onto fields surrounded by bunds; the water soaks into the soil, which then stores enough moisture to grow a crop such as wheat or gram.
    1 mark each for three of: depends on flash floods from the hills; water diverted by earth embankments/channels; fields enclosed by bunds; water soaks in/stored in the soil; crops sown afterwards; communal sharing of water.
  3. (c) The supply is unreliable because it depends on irregular rainstorms.
    B1 unreliable/irregular floods; embankments washed away; too much or too little water; no control over timing.
Q21Irrigation methods · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Put these parts of the canal irrigation system in order, starting from the river: watercourse (khal), barrage, distributary, main canal, branch canal. [2]
  2. (b) What is meant by warabandi? [2]
  3. (c) Explain why farmers at the tail end of a watercourse often receive less water than they are entitled to. You should develop your answer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Barrage → main canal → branch canal → distributary → watercourse (khal)
    2 marks all correct; 1 mark with one pair reversed.
  2. (b) A rotation system in which each farmer on a watercourse receives canal water for a fixed time on a fixed day, in proportion to the size of the land.
    1 mark: rotation/turn system for sharing water; 1 mark: fixed time per farmer based on land area.
  3. (c) Water seeps into the ground and evaporates along the earth channel, and farmers near the head may take extra water, so less is left by the time it reaches the tail.
    1 mark reason + 1 development: seepage/evaporation losses; water theft/illegal outlets at the head; poorly maintained channels.

Q24Irrigation methods · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe sprinkler irrigation. [2]
  2. (b) Compare sprinkler irrigation with flood irrigation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water is pumped through pipes and sprayed over crops through nozzles, like rain.

Q25Irrigation methods · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

In an illustrative example, a wheat field in Punjab used 1,050 mm of irrigation water in a season before it was laser levelled, and 800 mm after.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of water saved by laser levelling. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) 1 mm of water over 1 hectare is 10 m³. Calculate the volume of water saved per hectare. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two benefits to farmers of laser levelling their fields. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) (1,050 − 800) ÷ 1,050 × 100 = 23.8%
    M1 250 ÷ 1,050 × 100; A1 23.8%.
  2. (b) 250 mm × 10 = 2,500 m³ per hectare
    B1 2,500 m³.
  3. (c) Water spreads evenly across a level field, so less is needed and the tube-well pumping bill falls; all plants receive the same amount of water, so the crop grows evenly and yields rise; low patches no longer stay waterlogged, reducing salinity.
    1 mark for each benefit + 1 for development. Benefits: water saving; lower fuel/electricity costs; even crop growth/higher yields; less waterlogging and salinity; faster irrigation; better fertiliser use.
Q26Irrigation methods · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In an illustrative example, a diesel tube well uses 4 litres of diesel per hour. Diesel costs Rs 280 per litre and the pump runs for 600 hours in a season.

  1. (a) Calculate the cost of diesel for one season. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages to farmers of changing to a solar-powered tube well. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest why the spread of solar tube wells could make groundwater problems worse. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4 × 280 × 600 = Rs 672,000
    M1 4 × 280 × 600; A1 Rs 672,000.
  2. (b) There is no fuel to buy after the panels are installed, so running costs are very low; the pump works during load-shedding because it does not depend on the grid; there is no diesel smoke and fewer engine repairs.

Q27Irrigation methods · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A tube well pumps water at 0.03 m³ per second and runs for 8 hours a day.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of water pumped in one day. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two problems caused by the rapid growth in the number of private tube wells. [4]

Q28Irrigation methods · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the Indus Basin irrigation system is important to Pakistan's economy. [4]
  2. (b) 'Modern irrigation methods should replace traditional methods everywhere in Pakistan.' Do you agree? Give reasons to support your answer. [4]

  1. (a) Most of Pakistan's crops, including wheat, rice, cotton and sugarcane, are grown on irrigated land because rainfall is too low. These crops feed the population and supply raw materials to industries such as textiles and sugar, which earn much of Pakistan's export income and provide many jobs.

Judgement required.

Q29Irrigation methods · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Illustrative figures for one hectare of land in central Punjab:Rice: water used 12,000 m³, yield 3,000 kgWheat: water used 4,500 m³, yield 3,000 kg(1 m³ = 1,000 litres)

  1. (a) Calculate the litres of water used to produce 1 kg of each crop. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two problems of growing rice in an area where the water table is falling. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest two ways in which farmers could produce rice using less water. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rice: 12,000 m³ ÷ 3,000 kg = 4 m³ = 4,000 litres per kg; wheat: 4,500 ÷ 3,000 = 1.5 m³ = 1,500 litres per kg.
    B1 4,000 litres; B1 1,500 litres.
  2. (b) Rice needs standing water for months, so farmers pump large amounts of groundwater, making the water table fall faster; as it falls, wells must be deepened and pumping costs rise, so poorer farmers cannot afford to irrigate; deeper pumping may draw up saline water that damages soils.
    1 mark for each problem + 1 for development. Problems: heavy groundwater use; rising pumping costs/deeper wells; small farmers lose access; saline water intrusion; less water for other crops or drinking.
  3. (c) Sow rice directly into the field instead of transplanting into flooded fields; alternate wetting and drying instead of keeping fields flooded all the time.
    1 mark each: direct-seeded rice; alternate wetting and drying; laser levelling; growing rice on beds; shorter-duration varieties; switching some land to less thirsty crops.

Against: the main causes – seepage from the huge canal network, high regional water tables and flat land with no outlet – need large drains (such as LBOD), canal lining and SCARP-type pumping that only the government can fund; small farmers cannot afford gypsum or machinery. Judgement on the extent: farmers can manage the symptoms, but lasting solutions need government drainage and canal works.
    Level 3 (5–6): developed points for both sides/views, Pakistani examples and a clear, supported judgement. Level 2 (3–4): developed point(s) for one side or simple points for both. Level 1 (1–2): simple points only.
Q60Waterlogging and salinity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Yields (tonnes per hectare) of three crops on normal and on saline land on a farm in Punjab (illustrative figures):Wheat: normal 3.2, saline 2.0Cotton: normal 2.4, saline 1.8Barley: normal 2.8, saline 2.5

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage loss in yield on saline land for each crop. Give your answers to one decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Using your answers, state which crop is most tolerant of salt. [1]
  3. (c) A farmer with saline land decides to keep growing wheat rather than barley. Explain two reasons why he might make this choice, even though barley is more salt-tolerant. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Wheat: (3.2 − 2.0) ÷ 3.2 × 100 = 37.5%; cotton: (2.4 − 1.8) ÷ 2.4 × 100 = 25.0%; barley: (2.8 − 2.5) ÷ 2.8 × 100 = 10.7%
    B1 each: 37.5%; 25.0%; 10.7%.
  2. (b) Barley, because its yield falls by the smallest percentage.
    B1 barley with reason.
  3. (c) Wheat is the family's staple food, so growing it means they do not have to buy flour; there is a guaranteed government procurement price and a large market for wheat, while barley sells for less and has fewer buyers; the farmer has the seed, machinery and experience for wheat.
    1 mark for each reason + 1 for development. Reasons: wheat is the staple food; higher/guaranteed price; larger market; tradition and experience; even reduced wheat yield may earn more than barley.
