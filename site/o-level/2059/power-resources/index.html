Q1Solar, wind and other renewables · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define the term 'renewable energy resource'. [1]
  2. (b) Name two renewable sources of energy, other than hydel, that are used to generate electricity in Pakistan. [2]
  3. (c) Name the area in Sindh where most of Pakistan's wind farms are located. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A source of energy that is naturally replaced and will not run out when used, such as sunlight, wind or flowing water.
    1 mark: replenished naturally / will not be used up.
  2. (b) Solar and wind (also accept bagasse/biomass).
    1 mark each for any two: solar; wind; bagasse/biomass; biogas.
  3. (c) The Gharo–Keti Bandar wind corridor (around Jhimpir/Thatta)
    1 mark Gharo–Keti Bandar corridor / Jhimpir / Thatta district.
Q2Solar, wind and other renewables · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a solar panel system can provide electricity for a house in a village not connected to the national grid. [3]
  2. (b) Give two reasons why much of Pakistan is suitable for solar power. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Photovoltaic panels on the roof turn sunlight directly into electricity (direct current); it charges batteries so power can be used at night; an inverter changes it to alternating current for lights, fans and appliances.
    1 mark per point, max 3: PV panels/cells; convert sunlight to electricity; battery storage for night; inverter; panels facing south/in sun; no grid connection needed.
  2. (b) Most of the country has many hours of strong sunshine for most of the year; large areas of flat, unused desert land (e.g. Cholistan, Balochistan) are available for solar parks.
    1 mark each for any two: high sunshine hours/clear skies; high intensity in low latitudes; deserts with little cloud; cheap flat land; dry climate.
Q3Solar, wind and other renewables · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two advantages of using biogas in rural areas of Pakistan. [4]
  2. (b) State two reasons why biogas plants are not more widely used. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Biogas from animal dung provides free fuel for cooking and lighting, so families save money and need not cut trees for firewood, reducing deforestation. The slurry left after gas production is a good fertiliser, improving crop yields.
    Two developed points: cheap/free fuel → savings; reduces firewood use → less deforestation; cleaner than burning dung/wood → less indoor smoke, better health; slurry as fertiliser; uses waste/hygiene; saves women's time collecting fuel.
  2. (b) Initial cost of building the digester is too high for poor farmers; a family needs several animals to supply enough dung every day.
    1 mark each for any two: initial cost; need enough cattle/dung; lack of awareness/technical skill; maintenance; gas output falls in cold weather; water needed.
Q4Solar, wind and other renewables · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Name the body that regulates electricity in Pakistan. [1]
  2. (b) Define the term 'net metering'. [1]
  3. (c) Describe what happens to the electricity from rooftop panels when the household uses less than the panels generate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) NEPRA (National Electric Power Regulatory Authority)
    1 mark.
  2. (b) A system in which a household with solar panels sends surplus electricity to the grid and receives credit for it on its bill.
    1 mark for a definition that includes sending surplus electricity to the grid and receiving credit.
  3. (c) The surplus flows into the grid; the meter records it; the household receives credit against its bill.
    1 mark each, up to 2: surplus fed into the grid; meter records the export; credit set against future bills.
Q5Solar, wind and other renewables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A house in Multan has a rooftop solar system rated at 3 kW. On average it produces its full rated power for the equivalent of 4 hours a day (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Calculate the electricity generated by the system in one 30-day month. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the money saved in one month if grid electricity costs Rs 50 per kWh. [1]
  3. (c) The system cost Rs 540,000. Calculate how many months it would take for the savings to repay this cost. [1]
  4. (d) Explain why many households and businesses in Pakistani cities have installed rooftop solar panels in recent years. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 360 kWh
    M1 3 × 4 × 30; A1 360 kWh.
  2. (b) Rs 18,000
    360 × 50 = Rs 18,000.
  3. (c) 30 months
    540,000 ÷ 18,000 = 30.
  4. (d) Grid electricity prices have risen sharply, so solar quickly pays for itself; load-shedding makes grid supply unreliable; panel prices have fallen greatly, and net metering lets owners sell extra power to the grid.
    1 mark each, max 3: rising tariffs; load-shedding/unreliable supply; cheaper (imported) panels; net metering; abundant sunshine; reduces bills for businesses.
Q6Solar, wind and other renewables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two reasons why the coastal area of southern Sindh is suitable for wind farms. [4]
  2. (b) State two disadvantages of wind power. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strong and steady winds blow from the sea, especially during the summer monsoon months, so turbines turn for many hours. The land is flat and thinly populated, so large turbines can be spaced out cheaply, and it is close to Karachi, a large market for electricity.
    Two developed points: strong/consistent sea winds (esp. April–September); flat open land; low population/cheap land; near Karachi/national grid; few obstacles to wind.
  2. (b) Output varies because wind speed changes, so it cannot provide a steady supply; turbines are expensive to build and the best sites may be far from the grid.
    1 mark each for any two: unreliable/variable; high initial cost; noise; visual impact; harm to birds; needs large area; transmission lines needed.
Q7Solar, wind and other renewables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'bagasse' and explain how it is used to produce electricity. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why bagasse power can only be produced for part of the year. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bagasse is the dry fibrous waste left after juice is crushed out of sugar cane. Sugar mills burn it in boilers to make steam, which turns turbines to generate electricity for the mill, with surplus sold to the grid.
    1 mark definition (sugar-cane waste after crushing); 1 mark burnt to heat water/steam; 1 mark steam turns turbine/generator, surplus to grid.
  2. (b) Sugar cane is crushed only during the harvest/crushing season (roughly November to March), so bagasse is not available all year unless it is stored.
    1 mark seasonal harvest/crushing season; 1 mark storage difficult/bulky so supply stops.
Q8Solar, wind and other renewables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by tidal power. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest why the creeks of the Indus delta have been considered for tidal power. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why Pakistan has not yet developed tidal power. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Electricity generated using the rise and fall of the sea's tides to drive turbines.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) The delta coast has many narrow creeks where tidal water flows in and out strongly; it is close to Karachi and the coastal industries that need electricity.
    1 mark each for any two: many creeks/inlets channel tides; reasonable tidal range; near Karachi demand; unused coastal land.
  3. (c) The technology is very expensive and unproven at large scale, so investors prefer cheaper solar, wind and fossil fuels. Barriers and turbines could harm mangrove forests, fish breeding grounds and the livelihoods of fishing communities in the delta.
    Two developed points: high cost/lack of technology; low tidal range in places; environmental damage to mangroves/fishing; silt from the Indus; cyclones/storms; other options cheaper.
Q9Solar, wind and other renewables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A wind turbine has a rated capacity of 1.5 MW. Over one year it runs at an average of 35% of its capacity (capacity factor). Take a year as 8,760 hours (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Calculate the energy the turbine generates in one year, in MWh. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a wind turbine's output varies from hour to hour. [3]
  3. (c) State one advantage and one disadvantage of wind power for the electricity grid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4,599 MWh
    1 mark: 1.5 MW × 8,760 h = 13,140 MWh at full output. 1 mark: 13,140 × 0.35 = 4,599 MWh.
  2. (b) Wind speed changes from moment to moment; a turbine needs a minimum wind speed to start and shuts down in very strong winds; gusts and calm periods make output uneven.
    1 mark each, up to 3: wind speed varies; minimum speed needed to start; shut-down in very high winds; gusts and calm periods.
  3. (c) Advantage: no fuel is burned and no CO₂ is emitted while generating. Disadvantage: output is intermittent, so backup generation is needed.
    1 mark for a valid advantage; 1 mark for a valid disadvantage.

  2. (b) Explain two advantages to farmers of solar tube wells over diesel-powered pumps. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A pump for irrigation powered by solar panels; the panels generate electricity that drives the pump to lift groundwater onto fields; no fuel is needed.

  2. (b) Solar tube wells have no diesel cost, so running costs are lower; there is no fuel supply problem; they are quieter and produce no exhaust; they can work in areas without grid power.

Q12Solar, wind and other renewables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by geothermal energy. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest why parts of northern Pakistan and western Balochistan have potential for geothermal energy. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two difficulties of developing geothermal power in these areas. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Heat from inside the Earth, carried by hot water or steam, used to generate electricity or heat buildings.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) These areas lie in active tectonic zones where plates meet, with hot springs (e.g. in Gilgit-Baltistan) and old volcanic areas (e.g. Chagai), showing heat close to the surface.
    1 mark tectonic/plate boundary/earthquake zone; 1 mark evidence: hot springs/volcanic features.
  3. (c) The areas are remote and mountainous, so building plants and long transmission lines to the cities is very costly. Detailed surveys and deep drilling are needed and are expensive and risky, and Pakistan lacks experience and funding for this technology.
    Two developed points: remoteness/rugged terrain; distance from demand/transmission cost; high exploration/drilling cost; lack of technology/expertise; earthquake risk; security in Balochistan.
Q13Solar, wind and other renewables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Read the two views:View A: Pakistan should rely mainly on solar and wind power for its future electricity.View B: Pakistan should continue to rely mainly on hydel and thermal power.Which view do you agree with more? Give reasons for your answer, referring to both views. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) A: abundant sunshine and coastal wind, no fuel import bill, falling costs, quick to install, clean, rooftop solar reduces load on grid. B: solar and wind are intermittent and need storage or back-up; hydel and thermal give steady base-load; existing plants and Thar coal reserves; grid not designed for many variable sources. Q14Solar, wind and other renewables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the extent to which renewable energy can solve the energy problems of remote rural areas of Pakistan. Give reasons for your answer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Yes: off-grid solar home systems, micro-hydel on mountain streams in the north, biogas from dung and small wind turbines on the coast can give power without costly grid extension. Limits: high upfront cost for poor families, batteries expensive, small systems cannot run heavy machinery or tube wells easily, need for maintenance skills, seasonal limits (frozen streams, cloudy monsoon days). Judgement on extent.
    Levels: L1 (1–2) simple points; L2 (3–4) developed points one side; L3 (5–6) developed points on both sides with judgement. Credit named types matched to areas (micro-hydel in Gilgit-Baltistan/Chitral, solar in Balochistan/Thar).
Q15Solar, wind and other renewables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A house uses 10 kWh of electricity each evening after sunset. A battery bank stores 5 kWh of usable energy. Solar panels charge the battery during the day (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Explain the problem of intermittency for solar power and how battery storage can help. [6]
  2. (b) Calculate how many 5 kWh batteries are needed to supply one evening's 10 kWh use with no other source. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Solar panels produce no electricity at night and produce less on cloudy days or in winter; batteries store daytime electricity for use after sunset; storage is costly and limited in capacity, so grid or other backup is still needed.
    1 mark each, up to 6: no output at night; output falls with cloud and in winter; batteries store daytime energy; batteries supply power after sunset; storage is costly and limited; backup generation still needed.
  2. (b) 2 batteries
    1 mark: 10 kWh ÷ 5 kWh per battery = 2. 1 mark: 2 batteries.

Q17Coal, oil and natural gas · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the quality of most coal found in Pakistan. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two problems of using Thar coal to generate electricity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mostly low-grade lignite or sub-bituminous coal with a high content of moisture, ash and sulphur, so it gives less heat per tonne.
    1 mark low grade/lignite; 1 mark high ash/sulphur/moisture or low heating value.
  2. (b) The coal lies deep under thick sand, so huge amounts of overburden must be removed and water pumped out, making mining expensive. Burning lignite releases a lot of carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and ash, causing air pollution and health problems, and power plants need water in a desert area.
    Two developed points: deep/under sand → costly mining; low quality → much coal per unit electricity; air pollution/greenhouse gases; water shortage in desert; remote → transmission lines needed; displacement of local people.

Q20Coal, oil and natural gas · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two reasons why Pakistan imports most of the oil it uses. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest two effects on Pakistan's economy of a large rise in world oil prices. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pakistan's own oil fields in the Potwar Plateau and Sindh are small and produce only a small fraction of national demand. Q27Coal, oil and natural gas · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

  3. (c) State one disadvantage of a run-of-river scheme. [1]

Q66Hydel power · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Illustrative figures (invented for this question): a reservoir had a storage capacity of 9,000 million m³ when it was built. After 40 years of silt deposits its capacity was 7,650 million m³.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of the capacity lost to silt. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two effects of silt build-up on a hydel dam. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15.0%
    1 mark: (9,000 − 7,650) ÷ 9,000 × 100 = 15.0%.
  2. (b) Less water is stored, so less power is available in dry periods; silt can damage turbines and intakes; the reservoir has less room to store flood water; the life of the dam is shortened.
    1 mark each, up to 4: less water stored and less power; damage to turbines and intakes; less flood-control space; shorter life of dam.

  1. (a) Any two of: Indus; Jhelum; Chenab.
    1 mark each for any two.
  2. (b) Dams such as Tarbela and Mangla depend on steady river flows; snow and glacier melt gives summer water that fills the reservoirs; reliable flow supports power generation through the year; uncertain flows make planning difficult; disputes could affect the flow.

  1. (a) Describe how water stored in a dam is used for both irrigation and electricity in Pakistan. [4]
  2. (b) State one conflict that can arise between power generation and irrigation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water released from the reservoir passes through turbines to generate power, then flows into canals for irrigation; releases are planned so that power and irrigation both get water; storage helps supply water in the dry season.

Q70Hydel power · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A 1,000 MW hydel station operates for 3,000 hours in a year (illustrative figures).

  1. (a) Calculate the energy generated, in TWh. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why a hydel station may generate less than this figure in practice. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3 TWh
    1 mark: 1,000 MW × 3,000 h = 3,000,000 MWh = 3 TWh.
  2. (b) Low river flows in drought reduce water in the reservoir; maintenance outages take turbines offline; silt can damage turbines; floods may force the gates to close; the head of water may be lower than design.
    1 mark each, up to 4: low river flows and reservoir levels; maintenance outages; silt damage; gates closed in floods; lower head than design.

Q73Hydel power · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the risks that earthquakes and floods pose to hydel dams in Pakistan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Risks: earthquakes can crack dam walls and damage gates; floods may overtop spillways and erode foundations; silt and landslides into the reservoir reduce capacity and can cause waves; a dam failure would threaten people downstream. Q74Hydel power · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Illustrative figures (invented for this question): a set of small hydel schemes would give 200 MW at a total cost of Rs 60 billion. A large dam would give 4,500 MW at a total cost of Rs 900 billion.

  1. (a) Calculate the cost per MW for the small schemes, in Rs billion per MW. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the cost per MW for the large dam, in Rs billion per MW. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why the large dam may still be preferred despite its size. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.30
    1 mark: 60 ÷ 200 = 0.30.
  2. (b) 0.20
    1 mark: 900 ÷ 4,500 = 0.20.
  3. (c) It provides storage for dry periods, irrigation and flood control; its total energy output is much larger; it may have a lower running cost per unit over its life; fewer sites are needed to connect to the grid.
    1 mark each, up to 4: storage for irrigation and floods; much larger total output; lower running cost over life; fewer sites needed; grid integration.
Q75Hydel power · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a large dam's storage can help a country cope with both floods and droughts. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) The reservoir stores floodwater in the monsoon and releases it gradually, reducing peak flows downstream; the stored water supplies irrigation and power in dry seasons; it maintains river flow for power when inflows fall; but the reservoir needs spare capacity to take flood water.
    1 mark each, up to 6: stores flood water; gradual release reduces downstream peaks; stores water for dry season; supplies irrigation in drought; maintains flow and power output; needs spare capacity for floods.
