Q1Climate extremes and hazards · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) What is meant by the term drought? [2]
  2. (b) Explain two effects of drought on people living in the Thar Desert. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A long period with much less rainfall than normal, so that there is a serious shortage of water.
    B1 long period / prolonged; B1 below-normal rainfall / water shortage.
  2. (b) Wells and ponds dry up, so women and children walk long distances for water, which may be unsafe to drink and spreads disease; grazing and fodder fail, so cattle, goats and sheep die and families lose their main source of food and income; food shortages lead to malnutrition, especially among children; many families migrate to barrage areas or towns to find work.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: water shortage; livestock deaths; crop failure and hunger / malnutrition; disease; migration; debt.
Q2Climate extremes and hazards · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe the causes of the severe floods in Pakistan in 2022. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two impacts of floods such as those of 2022 on farming in Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Monsoon rainfall from June to August was several times the normal amount in Sindh and Balochistan; very high temperatures earlier in the year increased glacier melt in the north; much of the lower Indus plain is very flat, so water could not drain away; embankments were breached and hill torrents flooded towns.
    1 mark per point up to 3: exceptional monsoon rain (Sindh, Balochistan); heatwave / glacier melt; flat land, poor drainage; breaches / hill torrents; deforestation or climate change accepted with explanation.
  2. (b) Standing crops such as cotton, rice and dates were destroyed, so farmers lost income and the country lost export earnings; livestock drowned, removing a source of milk and income; floodwater stood for months, so wheat could not be sown on time for the next rabi season; irrigation canals and roads were damaged, increasing costs.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: crop losses; livestock deaths; delayed sowing; damaged canals / roads; food prices rise; soil damage or silt.

Q6Climate extremes and hazards · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) What is a glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF)? [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the risk of GLOFs is increasing in Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A sudden flood caused when water stored in a lake formed by a melting glacier breaks through the ice or moraine (rock debris) that dams it.
    B1 lake formed by glacier melt; B1 sudden release when dam of ice/moraine fails.
  2. (b) Rising temperatures are making glaciers melt faster, so more and larger glacial lakes are forming; these lakes are held back by loose moraine or ice, which can give way suddenly; heavy rain or heatwaves can raise lake levels quickly; more people now live and build in narrow valleys below the glaciers, so more are exposed.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: global warming / faster melting; more lakes forming; weak moraine dams; heatwaves and heavy rain; settlement in valleys.

Q8Climate extremes and hazards · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 11 marks

Air quality index (AQI) readings at one monitoring station in Lahore on six days in November:180, 245, 310, 420, 390, 260

  1. (a) State the number of readings that were above 300 ('hazardous'). [1]
  2. (b) Explain why smog is worst in Lahore between November and January. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) 'Smog in Lahore can only be reduced by controlling crop burning.' Evaluate this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3
    B1 3.
  2. (b) In winter cold, calm air near the ground is trapped under a layer of warmer air (temperature inversion), so pollutants cannot rise and spread; winds are light, so smoke builds up; at this time farmers burn rice straw after the harvest and smoke drifts over the city; emissions from vehicles, brick kilns and factories add to the pollution, and fog mixes with the smoke.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: temperature inversion; calm winds; crop residue burning; vehicles, kilns, industry; fog.
  3. (c) For: crop residue burning in Punjab after the rice harvest produces large amounts of smoke exactly when smog peaks; offering farmers machines such as happy seeders or buying straw could cut this quickly. Against: vehicles are a major year-round source, so better fuel, emission checks and public transport (e.g. Lahore Metro Bus and Orange Line) are needed; brick kilns can switch to zig-zag technology; factories and burning of rubbish also contribute; planting trees and banning open burning in the city help. Judgement: e.g. controlling crop burning is important but not enough on its own, because traffic and industry pollute every day.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points. L2 (3–4): developed point(s) for one view, or simple points for both. L3 (5–6): developed points for both views, a named Pakistani example and a judgement.
Q9Climate extremes and hazards · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Pakistan produces less than 1% of the world's greenhouse gas emissions but is one of the countries most affected by climate change. Explain two reasons why Pakistan is very vulnerable to climate change. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) 'Pakistan should spend more on adapting to climate change than on reducing its own emissions.' Evaluate this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Its rivers depend on glaciers in the Himalaya, Karakoram and Hindu Kush, so faster melting first raises flood and GLOF risk and later threatens water supply; most of the country is arid or semi-arid with high temperatures, so heatwaves and droughts become more extreme; farming, which employs a large share of the workforce, depends on irrigation and monsoon rain; many people are poor and live in flood-prone areas, so they cannot easily recover.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: glacier dependence; heat and aridity; agriculture-dependent economy; flood-prone plains / coast; poverty and weak infrastructure.
  2. (b) For adaptation: Pakistan's emissions are small, so cutting them will hardly change world temperatures; its people are already suffering floods, heatwaves and droughts, so money for flood defences, early warning, heat action plans, drought-resistant crops and water storage saves lives now. For reducing emissions: cleaner energy such as solar, wind and hydel reduces costly oil imports and air pollution (smog); reforestation such as large tree-planting programmes both stores carbon and reduces floods and soil erosion; showing action helps Pakistan gain international climate finance. Judgement: e.g. adaptation should come first, but many actions (solar power, trees) do both.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points. L2 (3–4): developed point(s) for one view, or simple points for both. L3 (5–6): developed points for both views, a named Pakistani example and a judgement.

    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: maximum heating in summer afternoons; humid monsoon air; rapid rising and cooling; contrast with winter.

Approximate mean annual rainfall at five places, from north-east to south-west (rounded figures):Murree: 1700 mmIslamabad: 1150 mmLahore: 650 mmMultan: 190 mmJacobabad: 110 mm

  1. (a) Calculate the difference in annual rainfall between the wettest and driest places in the table. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage decrease in annual rainfall from Murree to Jacobabad. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Murree and Islamabad are about 40 km apart. Explain why Murree receives much more rain. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1700 − 110 = 1590 mm
    B1 1590 mm.
  2. (b) 1590 ÷ 1700 × 100 = 93.5%
    M1 1590/1700 × 100; A1 93.5%.
  3. (c) Murree is about 2,000 m up in the hills, so moist monsoon air is forced to rise over the hills, cools and condenses, giving heavy relief rain; Islamabad is lower, at the foot of the hills.

Q23Rainfall, monsoon and depressions · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe western depressions, including where they come from and when they affect Pakistan. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which winter rainfall from western depressions is important for farming in Pakistan. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) They are low-pressure weather systems that form over the Mediterranean Sea region and move eastwards over Iran and Afghanistan; they reach Pakistan mainly from December to March; they bring rain to the north and west, and snow to the mountains.
    1 mark each for: low-pressure systems; origin Mediterranean; move east over Iran/Afghanistan; season (winter, Dec–Mar); rain/snow in north and west. Max 3.
  2. (b) It falls while rabi crops such as wheat and gram are growing, so it reduces the need for canal or tubewell water, which saves costs; in barani (rain-fed) areas of the Potwar Plateau it is the main water for wheat, so a dry winter means a poor harvest; snowfall in the mountains melts in spring and summer, feeding the rivers and canals used for irrigation.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: waters rabi crops (wheat); barani farming dependent on it; snow store feeds rivers; reduces irrigation cost / pumping.
Q24Rainfall, monsoon and depressions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain how relief rainfall forms over the Himalayan foothills during the monsoon. You should develop your answer. [4]
  2. (b) 'Monsoon rainfall brings more benefits than problems to Pakistan.' Evaluate this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Moist monsoon winds blow towards the foothills and are forced to rise over the high ground; as the air rises it expands and cools, and when it reaches its dew point the water vapour condenses to form clouds; the droplets grow and fall as heavy rain on the windward slopes, while the leeward side is drier (rain shadow).
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: moist air forced to rise by mountains; cools on rising; condensation at dew point / clouds form; rain on windward side; rain shadow on leeward side.
  2. (b) Benefits: monsoon rain fills reservoirs such as Tarbela and Mangla, used for irrigation and hydel power; it waters kharif crops such as rice, cotton and sugar cane; it recharges groundwater for tubewells; it cools temperatures after the hot season. Problems: heavy rain causes floods, as in 2010 and 2022, destroying crops, homes and roads; urban flooding in cities such as Karachi and Lahore; landslides in the northern areas; standing water spreads malaria and dengue; rainfall varies from year to year, so a weak monsoon causes drought. Judgement: e.g. the benefits outweigh the problems because farming and power depend on it, provided flood management improves.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): simple points. L2 (3–4): developed point(s) for one view, or simple points for both. L3 (5–6): developed points for both views, a named Pakistani example and a judgement. Use named examples (e.g. 2022 floods, Tarbela, kharif crops).
Q25Rainfall, monsoon and depressions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Annual rainfall (mm) at a rain-fed (barani) farming area for five years:Year 1: 512, Year 2: 680, Year 3: 395, Year 4: 840, Year 5: 448

  1. (a) Calculate the mean annual rainfall for the five years. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate by what percentage the wettest year was above the mean. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two problems that this variability of rainfall causes for farmers who depend on rain. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2875 ÷ 5 = 575 mm
    B1 575 mm.
  2. (b) (840 − 575) ÷ 575 × 100 = 46.1%
    M1 difference ÷ mean × 100; A1 46.1%.
  3. (c) In dry years such as Year 3 crops fail and fodder is scarce, so farmers lose income and may have to sell animals or borrow money; in very wet years such as Year 4 floods and waterlogging damage crops and soil; farmers cannot plan which crops to sow or how much seed and fertiliser to buy, so they choose low-risk, low-yield crops.

Q26Temperature factors · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A town at 250 m has a mean July temperature of 33 °C. Temperature falls by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m of height. Estimate the mean July temperature of a hill resort at 2250 m. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why temperature falls as altitude increases. [2]
  3. (c) Name two factors other than altitude that affect temperatures in Pakistan. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 33 − 6.5 × 2 = 20 °C
    M1 height difference 2000 m → 6.5 × 2 = 13 °C; A1 20 °C.
  2. (b) The air is heated mainly from the ground, so air further from the ground is cooler; at higher altitudes the air is thinner (lower pressure), holds less heat and less water vapour, so it absorbs and keeps less heat.
    B1 heating from the ground surface; B1 thinner air holds less heat.
  3. (c) Any two: latitude; distance from the sea; cloud cover; aspect (direction a slope faces); winds.
    B1 each.
Q27Temperature factors · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) What is meant by the term latitude? [1]
  2. (b) Explain how cloud cover affects temperatures in Pakistan by day and by night. You should develop your answer. [4]
  3. (c) State one way in which the aspect of a slope affects temperature in the northern mountains. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The distance of a place north or south of the Equator, measured in degrees.
    B1.
  2. (b) By day, cloud reflects and absorbs sunlight, so less reaches the ground and daytime temperatures are lower (e.g. during the monsoon); by night, cloud acts like a blanket and traps heat radiated from the ground, so nights are warmer; with clear skies in deserts days are very hot and nights cold, giving a large daily range.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: day – reflects insolation, cooler; night – traps outgoing radiation, warmer; clear skies – large daily range in deserts; example monsoon / Thar.
  3. (c) South-facing slopes receive more direct sunshine, so they are warmer (snow melts earlier) than north-facing slopes.
    B1.
Q28Temperature factors · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Suggest why Jacobabad in upper Sindh is one of the hottest places in Pakistan in May and June. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why places in the far north of Pakistan are colder in winter than places in the far south. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is far from the sea, so there is no cooling sea breeze (continentality); the sky is cloudless and the air very dry, so the sun heats the ground strongly; the sun is almost overhead and days are long in early summer; it is on a low flat plain surrounded by dry land and hot winds (loo) blow across it.
    1 mark per point up to 3: distance from sea; clear skies / dry air; high sun angle and long days; low altitude; hot dry winds.
  2. (b) The north is at a higher latitude, so the winter sun is lower in the sky and its energy is spread over a larger area, and days are shorter, so less heating; most of the north is also at high altitude, where the air is thinner and holds less heat; cold air from Central Asia can reach it, while the south is moderated by the warm Arabian Sea.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: latitude / angle of the sun; shorter winter days; altitude; distance from the warm sea; cold northerly air.
Q29Temperature factors · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Mean temperatures for a coastal city and an inland city in Balochistan (rounded figures):Coastal city: coolest month 17 °C, hottest month 30 °CInland city at 1,600 m: coolest month 3 °C, hottest month 27 °C

  1. (a) Calculate the annual temperature range for each place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the sea breeze keeps coastal areas cooler on summer afternoons. You should develop your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Coastal: 13 °C; inland: 24 °C
    B1 30 − 17 = 13; B1 27 − 3 = 24.
  2. (b) By day the land heats up faster than the sea, so warm air rises over the land and creates lower pressure; cooler, denser air moves in from the higher pressure over the sea to replace it, lowering temperatures on the coast; the moist sea air also adds cloud and humidity, which limits further heating.
    1 mark for each point and 1 mark for its development, for two points. Points: land heats faster than sea; low pressure over land; cool air flows in from the sea; moist air / cloud limits heating.
Q30Temperature factors · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

July mean temperatures at three stations in the same mountain district (rounded figures):Station P: altitude 150 m, July mean 34 °CStation Q: altitude 1350 m, July mean 27 °CStation R: altitude 2350 m, July mean 21 °C

  1. (a) Using a fall of 6.5 °C per 1,000 m, calculate the temperature expected at Q and at R from the temperature at P. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the difference between the actual and the expected temperature at Q and at R. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two factors, other than altitude, that could explain these differences. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q: 34 − 6.5 × 1.2 = 26.2 °C; R: 34 − 6.5 × 2.2 = 19.7 °C
    B1 Q 26.2; B1 R 19.7.
  2. (b) Q: 27 − 26.2 = +0.8 °C (actual warmer); R: 21 − 19.7 = +1.3 °C (actual warmer)
    B1 Q 0.8; B1 R 1.3.
  3. (c) Aspect – the stations may be on south-facing slopes that receive more direct sunshine; sheltered valley sites trap heat; less cloud cover or less forest; local winds (e.g. warm dry winds descending into valleys).
    B1 each for two valid factors with brief explanation.
