Q2Ions & ionic bonding · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Sodium chloride is obtained from rock salt mined at Khewra in Pakistan.

  1. (a) The electronic configuration of a sodium atom is 2,8,1 and of a chlorine atom is 2,8,7. Describe what happens to the electrons when sodium reacts with chlorine to form sodium chloride. [2]
  2. (b) Give the electronic configuration of the sodium ion and of the chloride ion. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each sodium atom transfers (loses) its one outer electron to a chlorine atom, which gains it; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions form.
    B1 one electron transferred from Na to Cl; B1 Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions formed (both with full outer shells).
  2. (b) Na⁺: 2,8 Cl⁻: 2,8,8
    B1 each.

Show answer

  1. (a) State what is meant by an ion. [1]
  2. (b) Give the charge on the ion formed by an element in each of these groups of the Periodic Table: (i) Group I (ii) Group II (iii) Group III (iv) Group VI (v) Group VII. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why positive ions are formed by metals. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) An atom or group of atoms that has lost or gained electrons, so it has an electrical charge.

  2. (b) (i) 1+ (ii) 2+ (iii) 3+ (iv) 2− (v) 1−
    3 marks all five; 2 marks four; 1 mark two or three.
  3. (c) Metal atoms have few outer-shell electrons, which they lose to gain a full outer shell; losing electrons leaves more protons than electrons.
    B1 metals lose (outer) electrons, so protons outnumber electrons.
Q5Ions & ionic bonding · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Magnesium fluoride is used to coat camera lenses.

  1. (a) Magnesium (2,8,2) reacts with fluorine (2,7). Describe the transfer of electrons that takes place. [2]
  2. (b) Give the formula and electronic configuration of each ion formed. [2]
  3. (c) Deduce the formula of magnesium fluoride. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each magnesium atom loses its two outer electrons, one to each of two fluorine atoms.
    B1 magnesium loses two electrons. B1 each fluorine atom gains one electron (two fluorine atoms per magnesium).
  2. (b) Mg²⁺ 2,8; F⁻ 2,8
    B1 Mg²⁺ 2,8. B1 F⁻ 2,8.
  3. (c) MgF₂
    B1.
Q6Ions & ionic bonding · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Calcium oxide (quicklime) is made in lime kilns and used in cement and to treat acidic soil.

  1. (a) Describe, in words, the dot-and-cross diagram for calcium oxide. Show the outer-shell electrons and the charges on the ions. [3]
  2. (b) Calcium oxide melts at about 2600 °C. Explain why it has such a high melting point. [2]
  3. (c) Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Suggest why calcium oxide has a much higher melting point than sodium chloride. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Calcium transfers two electrons to oxygen. Ca²⁺ has 8 electrons in its outer shell (2,8,8) and O²⁻ has 8 electrons in its outer shell (2,8), shown as 6 dots + 2 crosses; charges 2+ and 2−.
    B1 two electrons transferred from Ca to O; B1 both ions shown with 8 outer electrons (O²⁻: 6 of its own + 2 from Ca); B1 charges Ca²⁺ and O²⁻.
  2. (b) It is a giant ionic lattice with strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces.
    B1 giant lattice / many strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions; B1 much energy needed to overcome them.
  3. (c) The ions in calcium oxide have larger charges (2+ and 2−) so the attraction between them is stronger.
    B1 higher charges on ions → stronger attraction.

  2. (b) When dissolved in water (aqueous solution).
    B1.

  1. (a) Describe, in words, the dot-and-cross diagram for potassium sulfide. Show only the outer shells and the charges on the ions. [3]

Q11Ions & ionic bonding · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride. [2]
  2. (b) Magnesium oxide melts at 2852 °C, but sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Both are ionic. Suggest why magnesium oxide has the much higher melting point. [2]
  3. (c) Magnesium oxide is used to line furnaces. Give the property that makes it suitable. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A giant lattice of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions held together by strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
    B1 giant lattice / regular arrangement of positive and negative ions. B1 strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (in all directions).
  2. (b) Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions have larger charges (2+ and 2−) than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (1+ and 1−), so the attraction between the ions is stronger and needs more energy to overcome.
    B1 ions in MgO have higher charges. B1 stronger electrostatic attraction / more energy needed to separate ions.
  3. (c) very high melting point
    B1 high melting point (does not melt at furnace temperatures).
Q12Ions & ionic bonding · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Lithium (2,1) reacts with nitrogen (2,5) to form the ionic compound lithium nitride, Li₃N. Explain, in terms of electron transfer, why the formula is Li₃N. [3]
  2. (b) State the number of protons and electrons in (i) a lithium ion, Li⁺ (ii) a nitride ion, N³⁻. (Proton numbers: Li = 3, N = 7.) [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each lithium atom loses one electron to form Li⁺. A nitrogen atom needs to gain three electrons to form N³⁻ (2,8). So three lithium atoms are needed for each nitrogen atom.
    B1 Li loses 1 e⁻ → Li⁺; B1 N gains 3 e⁻ → N³⁻; B1 three Li needed per N so charges balance.
  2. (b) (i) 3 protons, 2 electrons (ii) 7 protons, 10 electrons
    B1 each pair. Electrons = protons − charge.
Q13Ions & ionic bonding · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) An ion X²⁻ contains 18 electrons and 16 neutrons. Deduce the proton number and nucleon (mass) number of X, and identify X. [3]
  2. (b) X forms an ionic compound with aluminium. Write its formula. [1]
  3. (c) The ion X²⁻ has the same electronic configuration as an argon atom. Suggest why its chemical properties are different from those of argon. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Proton number 16; nucleon number 32; X is sulfur (the ion is S²⁻).
    M1 protons = 18 − 2 = 16; A1 nucleon number = 16 + 16 = 32; B1 sulfur.
  2. (b) Al₂S₃
    B1: 2 × (+3) balances 3 × (−2).
  3. (c) Both have 2,8,8, but the ion has only 16 protons and a 2− charge; it is attracted to positive ions and can lose electrons (be oxidised), whereas argon is a neutral atom.
    B1 same configuration 2,8,8; B1 different number of protons / ion is charged, so attracts positive ions or can be oxidised.

Q15Ions & ionic bonding · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) A crystal of calcium fluoride is hit with a hammer and shatters. Explain why, in terms of the arrangement of the ions. [3]
  2. (b) Calcium chloride is soluble in water. Explain why an aqueous solution of calcium chloride conducts electricity, but solid calcium chloride does not. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why calcium chloride solution conducts electricity but copper conducts by a different kind of particle. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The force shifts one layer of ions relative to the next, so ions of the same charge come next to each other; they repel strongly and the crystal splits apart.
    B1 layers of ions are displaced. B1 like charges are brought side by side. B1 repulsion makes the crystal break (brittle).
  2. (b) In the solution the ions are free to move and carry charge; in the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.
    B1 ions free to move in solution. B1 ions fixed in lattice in the solid (no mobile charge carriers).
  3. (c) In the solution the charge is carried by moving ions (Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻); in copper it is carried by delocalised electrons.
    B1 ions in solution. B1 delocalised electrons in the metal.
Q16Atomic structure & isotopes · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. Which statement about this atom is correct? [1]
Show answer

Q18Atomic structure & isotopes · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Chlorine has two isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. State what is meant by the term isotopes. [2]

  3. (c) State the number of neutrons in an atom of chlorine-37. (Proton number of Cl = 17.) [1]

  1. (a) Explain why the mass of iron(II) sulfide is the same in both experiments. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in iron(II) sulfide. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [1]
  3. (c) Predict the mass of sulfur needed to react exactly with 14.0 g of iron. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Iron and sulfur always combine in a fixed ratio; 5.6 g of iron can only combine with 3.2 g of sulfur, so the extra 0.8 g of sulfur in experiment 2 is left unreacted.
    B1 fixed ratio in a compound. B1 excess sulfur does not react.
  2. (b) 63.6 %
    5.6 ÷ 8.8 × 100 = 63.6 %.
  3. (c) 8.0 g
    14.0 × 3.2 ÷ 5.6 = 8.0 g.

  1. (a) Give two pieces of evidence that air is a mixture and not a compound. [2]
  2. (b) Liquid air at −200 °C is allowed to warm slowly. Use the data to state which gas boils off first and which last. Explain. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two: its composition varies (e.g. water vapour, CO₂ levels); its components can be separated by a physical method (fractional distillation of liquid air); each gas keeps its own properties; there is no fixed formula; liquid air boils over a range.
    1 mark each.

Q105Elements, compounds & mixtures · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A student heats 10.0 g of a white solid strongly in a crucible until its mass stops changing. A gas is given off and 5.6 g of white residue remains.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of the original mass that is lost on heating. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how the result shows that the white solid is a compound and not an element. [2]
  3. (c) The gas turns limewater milky. Identify the gas and suggest the identity of the white solid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 44 %
    (10.0 − 5.6) ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 44 %.
  2. (b) It breaks down on heating into a gas and a different solid, so it must contain more than one element; an element cannot be split into simpler substances.
    B1 it decomposes into two (simpler) substances. B1 elements cannot be broken down by chemical means.
  3. (c) carbon dioxide; calcium carbonate (residue calcium oxide)
    B1 CO₂. B1 calcium carbonate (accept any carbonate consistent with 44 % loss: CaCO₃).
