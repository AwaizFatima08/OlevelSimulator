Q1Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A student plans an experiment to see whether people remember more words in a quiet room than in a noisy room. She tests 10 friends in the quiet room in the morning and 10 different friends in the noisy room in the evening. Identify one weakness of this plan. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest how the weakness you identified could be overcome. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Time of day is a confounding variable (morning vs evening), or the sample of friends is small/biased.
    B1 for any valid weakness.
  2. (b) Test both conditions at the same time of day (e.g. all in the morning), allocating participants to rooms randomly, so time of day cannot explain any difference in recall.
    1 mark: suitable change. 1 mark: explanation of why it improves the study.
Q2Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans to measure aggression in teenagers using a single question: 'Are you an aggressive person? Yes/No'. Explain one weakness of this measure. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest an improved measure of aggression. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is likely to produce socially desirable answers (few will admit aggression) and gives only a yes/no answer, so it cannot show different levels of aggression – low validity and sensitivity.
    1 mark: weakness identified (social desirability, limited detail, closed yes/no). 1 mark: explained in context.
  2. (b) A questionnaire of several items rated on a 1–5 scale (e.g. 'I lose my temper easily'), or an observation of aggressive acts (hitting, shouting) during a sports game, with clear behavioural categories.
    1 mark: improved measure. 1 mark: detail showing it measures levels/behaviour objectively.
Q3Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: Dr Ahmed plans an observation of students in a university library to see how often they check their phones. He will sit at a table with a clipboard and tell students he is observing them. Explain one strength and one weakness of this plan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: it is overt, so students know they are being studied, which is more ethical – consent can be obtained and privacy respected. Weakness: because students know they are being watched, they may check their phones less often than normal (participant reactivity), so the data would be less valid.
    2 marks for strength (identified + explained in context). 2 marks for weakness (identified + explained in context).
Q4Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher wants to investigate whether a mindfulness app reduces stress in workers at a call centre in Lahore. She uses volunteers who respond to an email, asks them to use the app for 4 weeks, and then asks, 'Do you feel less stressed now?' Explain two weaknesses of this plan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) No control group or baseline: without a comparison group (e.g. workers who do not use the app) or a stress measure before the 4 weeks, any change could be due to other factors (e.g. a quieter month at work). Volunteer sample and leading question: volunteers may be keen on mindfulness and the question suggests the answer, so demand characteristics may produce biased results.
    2 marks per weakness: identified (1) + explained in context (1). Accept: no baseline, no control group, volunteer bias, leading/closed question, self-report bias.
Q5Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A psychologist plans a repeated measures experiment in which participants solve anagrams first after drinking water and then, 15 minutes later, after drinking a caffeinated drink. Explain one problem with this plan and how it could be overcome. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Order effects: all participants do the caffeine condition second, so they may do better because of practice with anagrams (or worse because of fatigue), not because of caffeine. It could be overcome by counterbalancing – half do water first, half caffeine first – on separate days, so caffeine from the first session cannot affect the second, with different but equally difficult anagram sets.
    1 mark: order effects identified. 1 mark: explained in context (practice/fatigue/carry-over of caffeine). 1 mark: counterbalancing / separate days. 1 mark: explanation of how this solves the problem.
Q6Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans a correlational study of the relationship between time spent reading for pleasure and vocabulary in 11-year-olds. She uses a 30-item vocabulary test and asks parents to estimate their child's weekly reading time. Explain one strength and one weakness of using parents' estimates as a measure of reading time. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: parents see their child's daily routine at home and can report on reading that the child might forget, and it is quick and cheap to collect from many families. Weakness: estimates may be inaccurate – parents may not know about reading done in the child's room, or may exaggerate to appear good parents (social desirability), reducing validity. A reading diary could be more accurate.
    2 marks for strength (identified + explained). 2 marks for weakness (identified + explained).
Q7Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans to study whether people help a stranger more in a small town than in a big city. She will have a confederate drop a bag of oranges in the main market of a small town in Punjab and in a busy market in Karachi, and record whether passers-by help. Evaluate this plan in terms of its validity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: it is a field setting with real passers-by who do not know they are being studied, so behaviour is natural and has high ecological validity. Weakness: other differences between the two markets (crowd size, time of day, the local language, how busy people are) are uncontrolled and could explain any difference in helping, so it is not certain that town/city size caused the difference; the confederate's behaviour should be standardised and the times of day matched.
    2 marks for a point supporting validity (identified + explained). 2 marks for a point against validity (identified + explained), e.g. extraneous variables, sample bias, confederate inconsistency.
Q8Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A psychologist plans to interview 6 employees about their experience of working night shifts, using an unstructured interview. Explain one strength and one weakness of using an unstructured interview, and suggest one improvement to the plan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: the interviewer can follow up whatever the employee says with new questions, gathering rich qualitative detail about their experience of night shifts that was not anticipated. Weakness: each interview is different, so answers are hard to compare and the data are harder to analyse and replicate (low reliability); the interviewer's own views may also influence the questions. Improvement: use a semi-structured interview with a fixed set of core questions plus follow-ups, and increase the sample to 20 employees from different shifts and companies.
    2 marks strength, 2 marks weakness (each identified + explained in context). 2 marks improvement (suggestion + why it helps).
Q9Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher tests whether chewing gum improves concentration during a 20-minute proofreading task. Write a directional (one-tailed) hypothesis for this study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one control the researcher should use in this study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants who chew gum during a 20-minute proofreading task will detect more spelling errors than participants who do not chew gum.
    1 mark: both conditions of the IV stated (gum vs no gum). 1 mark: operationalised DV with a direction (more errors detected).
  2. (b) All participants proofread the same passage containing the same number of errors (e.g. 30), in the same quiet room.
    B1 for any relevant control (same passage, same time limit, same flavour of gum, same room/time of day).
Q10Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: an experiment tests whether students in Karachi solve more puzzles when working with background traffic noise or in silence. Explain one reason for using an independent measures design in this study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of using an independent measures design in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each student only does the puzzles once, so they cannot improve with practice or become bored between conditions (no order effects), and they are less likely to guess the aim of the study.
    1 mark: avoids order effects (practice/fatigue) or demand characteristics. 1 mark: linked to the puzzles/noise scenario.
  2. (b) Differences in puzzle-solving ability between the two groups (participant variables) could cause the difference in scores rather than the noise.
    1 mark: participant variables. 1 mark: in context (puzzle ability, intelligence, motivation).
Q11Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher wants to test whether a 'growth mindset' video increases how long 12-year-olds persist at a difficult maths task. Explain how she could use a matched pairs design in this study. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest how persistence could be operationalised. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give all children a pre-test of maths ability (and/or a measure of initial persistence). Rank them by score and pair children with the closest scores; then randomly allocate one child of each pair to watch the growth-mindset video and the other to watch a neutral video, before both attempt the maths task.
    1 mark: pre-test on a relevant variable. 1 mark: pairing children with similar scores. 1 mark: one of each pair randomly allocated to each condition.
  2. (b) The number of seconds a child keeps working on an impossible maths problem before choosing to stop (up to a maximum of 10 minutes).
    1 mark: measurable unit (time in seconds/number of attempts). 1 mark: clear definition of start/stop or a maximum.
Q12Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher wants to compare the memory of people who sleep 8 hours with those who sleep 4 hours. Explain why this would be difficult to carry out as a laboratory experiment, and suggest one alternative way to study the question. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Restricting sleep to 4 hours in a lab may harm participants (tiredness, accidents travelling home) and is costly, as they must stay overnight under supervision; also sleeping in a lab is unnatural. Alternative: a natural/quasi-experiment comparing people who already sleep about 4 or 8 hours (measured with a sleep diary or wrist actigraph) on the same memory test.
    1 mark: ethical problem (harm). 1 mark: practical problem (overnight, cost) or validity problem. 1 mark: alternative method named. 1 mark: alternative explained.
Q13Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to test whether listening to Qur'an recitation or calm instrumental music before an exam reduces anxiety more than sitting in silence. Describe how she could conduct a laboratory experiment to investigate this.Your plan must include details about:• the experimental design• how anxiety would be measured• controls. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: IV = 10 minutes of recitation, 10 minutes of instrumental music, or 10 minutes of silence before a timed 15-minute mental arithmetic test (an exam-like task). Independent measures design with random allocation (e.g. names drawn from a box), 30 university students per group, to avoid order effects and practice effects on the test. DV = anxiety, measured by a 10-item self-report rating scale (1 = not at all anxious, 5 = extremely anxious; total 10–50) completed just before the test, plus heart rate measured with a finger pulse monitor. Controls: same room, lighting and temperature; same headphones and volume (e.g. 60 dB); standardised instructions read from a script; same test paper; all tested at the same time of day; participants who are already on anxiety medication excluded. Procedure: consent, baseline heart rate, listening phase, rating scale + heart rate, test, debrief.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: design named and justified (independent measures with random allocation, or repeated measures with counterbalancing); IV conditions operationalised (duration, what is played); DV operationalised (rating scale with anchors and/or heart rate); controls (setting, volume, instructions, time of day); sample and procedure; ethics (consent, right to withdraw, debrief).
Q14Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to find out whether the colour of the paper on which a test is printed (white or yellow) affects how many words students recall. Describe how she could conduct an experiment to investigate this using a repeated measures design.Your plan must include details about:• the materials• how order effects will be controlled• the sample. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Materials – two lists of 20 concrete, two-syllable nouns of similar frequency (list A and list B), each printed in black 14-point font on either white or yellow A4 paper; a stopwatch; blank answer sheets. Procedure – each participant studies a list for 2 minutes, does a 1-minute distractor task (counting backwards in 3s), then writes down as many words as they remember in 2 minutes. They do this twice, once with each paper colour. Order effects – counterbalancing: half do white then yellow, half yellow then white; different but matched word lists in each condition, with list–colour pairing also counterbalanced; a 10-minute break between conditions to reduce fatigue. Sample – 40 Grade 12 students from two colleges in Lahore, chosen by random sampling from class lists (each name given a number, numbers drawn by computer), with parental consent for under-18s. DV – number of words correctly recalled out of 20.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: materials described (word lists matched for length/frequency, paper colour, font); counterbalancing explained (half each order) and matched lists; sample with technique and size; DV operationalised; standardised procedure; ethics.
Q15Designing a correlational study · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher finds a correlation between the number of fruit portions students eat each day and their exam scores. Explain why she cannot conclude that eating fruit causes higher exam scores. [2]
  2. (b) Write a non-directional hypothesis for this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A correlation only shows that two variables are related; neither variable was manipulated, so a third variable (e.g. family income or a healthier lifestyle) could cause both.
    1 mark: correlation does not show cause/no IV manipulated. 1 mark: possible third variable or reverse direction in context.
  2. (b) There will be a correlation between the number of fruit portions eaten per day and percentage exam score.
    1 mark: both co-variables operationalised. 1 mark: non-directional (correlation/relationship, no direction stated).
Q16Designing a correlational study · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher measures 'confidence' using a 1–10 rating and 'public speaking performance' using judges' ratings. Suggest how the judges' ratings could be made reliable. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one strength of using a correlational design for this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Use two or more judges who are trained with the same clear criteria (e.g. eye contact, fluency, clarity each rated 1–5) and practise on sample videos; each judge rates every speaker independently; then the judges' ratings are correlated to check inter-rater reliability (e.g. agreement of +0.8 or above).
    1 mark: clear operationalised criteria/rating scale. 1 mark: training/practice. 1 mark: independent ratings compared to check inter-rater reliability.
  2. (b) Confidence cannot ethically or easily be manipulated, so a correlation lets the researcher study the relationship between naturally occurring levels of confidence and performance.
    1 mark: strength (variables cannot be manipulated / shows strength and direction / quick to collect). 1 mark: in context.
Q17Designing a correlational study · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher collected data from 8 participants on hours of revision (x) and test score out of 50 (y): (2, 20), (4, 26), (5, 25), (6, 31), (7, 35), (9, 38), (10, 41), (12, 44). Describe how she should present these data in a graph. [3]
  2. (b) State the direction of the correlation shown by these data and give a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A scatter graph with hours of revision on the x-axis (labelled, 0–12) and test score on the y-axis (labelled, 0–50); each participant plotted as a single point; a title such as 'Scatter graph showing the relationship between hours of revision and test score'.
    1 mark: scatter graph. 1 mark: correctly labelled axes with units/scales. 1 mark: points not joined / suitable title.
  2. (b) Positive: as hours of revision increase, test scores increase (e.g. 2 hours → 20, 12 hours → 44).
    1 mark: positive. 1 mark: reason with reference to the data.
Q18Designing a correlational study · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to investigate whether there is a correlation between the number of hours that teenagers spend gaming each week and their sleep quality. Describe how she could conduct this correlational study.Your plan must include details about:• how both co-variables will be measured• the sampling technique• how the data will be analysed. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Co-variable 1 – hours gaming per week, measured by a 7-day diary in which teenagers record minutes spent gaming each day (total converted to hours). Co-variable 2 – sleep quality, measured by a 10-item questionnaire with 1–5 rating scales (1 = very poor, 5 = very good), total 10–50, completed at the end of the week. Sample – 100 students aged 14–16 from four schools in Islamabad, using stratified sampling so that boys and girls and each school are represented in proportion; parental consent. Procedure – standardised instructions, diaries returned in sealed envelopes with code numbers. Analysis – plot a scatter graph (hours gaming on the x-axis, sleep quality score on the y-axis) to see direction and strength; calculate a correlation coefficient; a negative correlation is predicted. Note that no causal conclusion can be drawn.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: both co-variables operationalised; named sampling technique explained with size and population; data analysis (scatter graph with labelled axes, correlation coefficient, direction expected); ethics; recognises correlation ≠ causation.
Q19Designing a correlational study · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to investigate whether there is a relationship between how often older people in a care home in Lahore receive visitors and their level of happiness. Describe how this correlational study could be carried out using observation and self-report data.Your plan must include details about:• how visits would be recorded• the self-report measure• ethical issues. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Visits – care staff keep a visitor log for 4 weeks, recording each resident's visitors (date, time, length); co-variable = total number of visits per resident. Happiness – a structured interview with a short rating scale read aloud: 5 questions such as 'How happy have you felt this week?' rated 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely), total 5–35, asked at the end of the 4 weeks by the same trained interviewer. Sample – all 40 residents who are able to consent (opportunity sample). Ethics – informed consent from residents (and family if capacity is doubtful), right to withdraw explained simply and repeated, confidentiality by code numbers, privacy (log records only counts, not conversation content), protection from distress for lonely residents, debrief. Analysis – scatter graph and correlation coefficient; positive correlation predicted.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: visits operationalised (log, count); self-report measure with example question and scale; ethics specific to elderly/vulnerable participants; sample; analysis.
Q20Designing an observation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: an observer watches customers in a shopping mall in Lahore. Explain the difference between a participant and a non-participant observation in this setting. [2]
  2. (b) Give one advantage of a non-participant observation in this study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a participant observation the observer joins in the activity being studied, e.g. acts as a shopper or works as a shop assistant; in a non-participant observation the observer watches from outside the activity, e.g. sitting at a café table without interacting with the shoppers.
    1 mark for each type explained with reference to the mall.
  2. (b) The observer can record behaviour more objectively/continuously because they are not distracted by taking part and are less likely to influence shoppers.
    B1.
Q21Designing an observation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: two observers rate aggression in a video of children playing. Their tallies for six 1-minute intervals were: Observer 1 – 4, 2, 5, 3, 6, 1; Observer 2 – 4, 3, 5, 3, 5, 1. Calculate the percentage of intervals in which the two observers gave exactly the same tally. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way the observers could improve their agreement. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 67%
    M1: matches in intervals 1, 3, 4 and 6 = 4 of 6. A1: 4 ÷ 6 × 100 = 66.7 → 67%.
  2. (b) Agree and write clear operational definitions of 'aggression' (e.g. hitting, pushing, kicking, grabbing a toy), then practise together on a pilot video and discuss differences before the real observation.
    1 mark: clearer operationalised categories / training. 1 mark: explanation of how it reduces subjective judgement.
Q22Designing an observation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between time sampling and event sampling, using an example of an observation of shoppers queuing at a supermarket checkout. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Time sampling: the observer records behaviour only at set time intervals, e.g. every 2 minutes records how many people are in the queue and whether anyone is using their phone. Event sampling: the observer records every time a particular behaviour occurs, e.g. every time someone leaves the queue or pushes in, whenever it happens during the session.
    2 marks for each: 1 for explanation, 1 for a relevant example in the queue setting.
Q23Designing an observation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to study helping behaviour among children aged 5–7 during break time in a school playground in Hyderabad. Describe how she could conduct a naturalistic observation to investigate this.Your plan must include details about:• behavioural categories• the sampling of behaviour (e.g. time or event sampling)• whether the observation is overt or covert. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Behavioural categories – helping a child who has fallen (helps them up, touches them), sharing (offers food/toy), comforting (puts an arm round a crying child), giving physical assistance (carries something with another child); each recorded on a tally chart. Sampling – time sampling: two observers each watch one target child for 30 seconds and record any category shown, then move to the next child, across 20-minute break times for 10 days; or event sampling – record every helping act seen in a defined zone. Observers – two trained observers, positioned at the edge of the playground (e.g. near the staff room window) so as not to interfere, recording independently to check inter-rater reliability. Overt/covert – overt with teachers' and parents' consent, but observers present for a week beforehand so children become used to them (habituation), reducing reactivity. Ethics – parental consent, confidentiality (no names), intervene if a child is at risk.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: at least three operationalised behavioural categories; time or event sampling explained with timings; overt/covert decision justified; observer position and inter-rater reliability; ethics for children.
Q24Designing an observation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to investigate how drivers in Karachi behave at a busy junction when the traffic light turns amber. Describe how this could be investigated using a structured covert observation.Your plan must include details about:• the location and timing• how data would be recorded• how reliability would be checked. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Location – one busy four-way junction with traffic lights; observers seated in a parked car or a café overlooking the junction so drivers cannot tell they are being watched. Timing – 30-minute sessions at morning rush (8–8:30 a.m.), midday and evening rush, over five weekdays, so that different traffic conditions are sampled. Recording – event sampling: each time the light turns amber, record the behaviour of the first car in the lane facing it, using categories: stops before line; speeds up and crosses; crosses at normal speed; stops after the line; and record vehicle type (car, motorbike, rickshaw, bus). Tally chart/coded checklist. Reliability – two observers watch the same light changes independently, then compare their tallies; calculate percentage agreement or correlate their records; categories refined and observers trained in a pilot observation if agreement is low. Ethics – public place, no number plates recorded, so privacy is respected.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: covert position explained; timings and location; behavioural categories and sampling method; data recording sheet; inter-rater reliability method; ethics.
Q25Designing a self-report study · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a questionnaire about phone use is given to 200 office workers. Write one closed question and one open question that could be included. [2]
  2. (b) Give one reason why participants may not answer questionnaires about phone use honestly. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Closed: 'On average, how many hours per day do you use your phone for non-work purposes? (less than 1 / 1–2 / 2–3 / more than 3)'. Open: 'Describe how using your phone affects your concentration at work.'
    1 mark for a closed question with fixed responses. 1 mark for a genuinely open question allowing free answers.
  2. (b) Social desirability bias – they may under-report non-work phone use to appear hard-working to their employer.
    B1.
Q26Designing a self-report study · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher designs a rating scale to measure job stress among nurses in Quetta. Write one rating-scale item, including the anchors, and explain how it would be scored. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why some items on the scale should be reverse-scored. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Item: 'I feel exhausted at the end of my shift.' 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree. Scoring: the number chosen is the score for the item (higher = more stress); scores across all items are added to give a total stress score.
    1 mark: suitable item statement. 1 mark: labelled anchors at both ends (and/or each point). 1 mark: scoring explained (higher = more stress, items totalled; reverse-scored items if any).
  2. (b) If all items are worded in the same direction, participants may tick the same answer down the page without reading (response set/acquiescence); mixing positively and negatively worded items, which are reverse-scored, reduces this bias.
    1 mark: avoids response bias/acquiescence. 1 mark: explanation of how reverse-scoring works or why it matters.
Q27Designing a self-report study · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to investigate the reasons why university students in Pakistan choose to study abroad. Describe how she could conduct an interview study to investigate this.Your plan must include details about:• the type of interview• example questions• how the data would be analysed. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Type – semi-structured interview, so that every student is asked the same core questions but the interviewer can follow up interesting answers. Sample – 20 final-year students from three universities in Lahore and Islamabad who have applied to study abroad, recruited by volunteer sampling through a notice on student portals. Questions – closed: 'Have you applied to study in another country? Yes/No'; 'How important is the reputation of the university to you? 1 (not important) to 5 (very important)'; open: 'What are the main reasons you want to study abroad?'; 'How did your family influence your decision?'; follow-up probes: 'Can you tell me more about that?'. Procedure – 30-minute interviews in a quiet room, audio-recorded with consent; same trained interviewer for all to reduce interviewer effects. Analysis – closed and rating answers summarised as percentages/means; open answers transcribed and analysed by thematic analysis, coding recurring themes (career prospects, quality of education, family expectations, finance), with two researchers coding independently to check reliability. Ethics – consent, confidentiality (codes), right to withdraw.

Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Creditworthy content: interview type named and justified; at least one open and one closed example question; sample; procedure (recording, setting, interviewer); analysis of quantitative and qualitative data; ethics.
Q28Designing a longitudinal study · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by a 'longitudinal study'. [1]
  2. (b) Explain one strength of a longitudinal study compared with testing different age groups at one time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A study in which the same participants are tested or observed repeatedly over a long period of time (months or years).
    B1.
  2. (b) Because the same people are followed, changes over time are not caused by differences between groups (participant variables / cohort effects), so the development of each person can be tracked.
    1 mark: same participants compared with themselves. 1 mark: removes participant variables/cohort effects or shows individual development.
Q29Designing a longitudinal study · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a 5-year study follows 400 teenagers' mental health. In year 1, 400 took part; by year 5, 292 remained. Calculate the percentage of the original sample lost by year 5. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how this attrition could affect the validity of the findings. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 27%
    M1: (400 − 292) ÷ 400 × 100. A1: 108 ÷ 400 × 100 = 27%.
  2. (b) If those who dropped out differ systematically from those who stayed (e.g. teenagers with worse mental health are more likely to drop out), the remaining sample is biased and may make mental health appear better than it really is.
    1 mark: drop-outs may differ from those who stay. 1 mark: biased sample / distorted conclusions in context.
Q30Designing a longitudinal study · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to investigate how children's friendships change between the ages of 6 and 10. Describe how she could conduct a longitudinal study to investigate this.Your plan must include details about:• the sample and how attrition would be reduced• how friendships would be measured• how often data would be collected. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Sample – 120 six-year-olds from six primary schools in Lahore (both girls and boys), selected by stratified sampling from school registers, with parental consent. Attrition – collect several contact details, send newsletters about the study, give small gifts or certificates to children each year, and recruit extra participants at the start to allow for drop-out. Measures – each year, a short structured interview with the child ('Who are your best friends?', 'What do you like doing together?'), a sociometric nomination task (children name up to three classmates they like to play with), and a 15-minute playground observation of each child using time sampling (who they play with, in what kind of activity). Teachers complete a 5-point rating scale of each child's friendship quality. Frequency – once a year in the same month (e.g. October) from age 6 to 10, giving 5 data points, using the same measures and trained researchers each time. Analysis – compare number and type of friends across years (means, line graphs), and themes in interview answers.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: sample with technique and size; specific attrition strategies; measures operationalised (interviews, nominations, observation, rating); intervals and duration; consistency of measures; ethics with children.
