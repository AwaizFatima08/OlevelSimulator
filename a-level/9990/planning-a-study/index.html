Q1Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A student plans an experiment to see whether people remember more words in a quiet room than in a noisy room. She tests 10 friends in the quiet room in the morning and 10 different friends in the noisy room in the evening. Identify one weakness of this plan. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest how the weakness you identified could be overcome. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Time of day is a confounding variable (morning vs evening), or the sample of friends is small/biased.
    B1 for any valid weakness.
  2. (b) Test both conditions at the same time of day (e.g. all in the morning), allocating participants to rooms randomly, so time of day cannot explain any difference in recall.
    1 mark: suitable change. 1 mark: explanation of why it improves the study.
Q2Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans to measure aggression in teenagers using a single question: 'Are you an aggressive person? Yes/No'. Explain one weakness of this measure. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest an improved measure of aggression. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is likely to produce socially desirable answers (few will admit aggression) and gives only a yes/no answer, so it cannot show different levels of aggression – low validity and sensitivity.
    1 mark: weakness identified (social desirability, limited detail, closed yes/no). 1 mark: explained in context.
  2. (b) A questionnaire of several items rated on a 1–5 scale (e.g. 'I lose my temper easily'), or an observation of aggressive acts (hitting, shouting) during a sports game, with clear behavioural categories.
    1 mark: improved measure. 1 mark: detail showing it measures levels/behaviour objectively.
Q3Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: a teacher in Multan wants to test whether background music helps Grade 7 pupils finish a maths worksheet faster. She plays the music to Section A (24 pupils) and gives silence to Section B (21 pupils), and times each pupil with a stopwatch she holds herself. Identify one weakness of this design. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one change to the design that would overcome this weakness. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The two sections are different groups of pupils, so differences in maths ability or motivation between the sections could explain any difference in time, not the music. Alternatively, one person timing all pupils with one stopwatch may time some pupils inconsistently.
    1 mark for a correctly identified weakness (participant variables/confounding, or inconsistent timing by one person). 1 mark for explaining how it affects the conclusion.
  2. (b) Randomly allocate the 45 pupils from the same classes to the two conditions (independent measures with random allocation), or use a repeated measures design with counterbalancing so the same pupils do both conditions.
    1 mark for a specific, appropriate change; 1 mark for explaining how it controls participant variables.
Q4Evaluating the design · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: a school counsellor in Multan asks 40 Year 10 pupils one question: 'How stressed do you feel about exams: a lot or a little?' She will ask the same question after a mock exam to measure change. Explain one weakness of this measure. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest an improved measure of exam stress that she could use. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The terms 'a lot' and 'a little' are vague and undefined, so each pupil may interpret them differently. The answers are therefore not directly comparable between pupils, and a small real change after the mock exam may not be detected.
    1 mark for identifying the weakness (vague, undefined response terms); 1 mark for explaining the consequence for comparing pupils or measuring change.
  2. (b) A rating scale from 1 (not at all stressed) to 7 (extremely stressed), or a validated questionnaire with several items, averaged to give a score.
    1 mark for a suitable measure (rating scale or multi-item questionnaire); 1 mark for explaining how it gives more precise or sensitive data.
Q5Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: Dr Ahmed plans an observation of students in a university library to see how often they check their phones. He will sit at a table with a clipboard and tell students he is observing them. Explain one strength and one weakness of this plan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: it is overt, so students know they are being studied, which is more ethical – consent can be obtained and privacy respected. Weakness: because students know they are being watched, they may check their phones less often than normal (participant reactivity), so the data would be less valid.
    2 marks for strength (identified + explained in context). 2 marks for weakness (identified + explained in context).
Q6Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher wants to investigate whether a mindfulness app reduces stress in workers at a call centre in Lahore. She uses volunteers who respond to an email, asks them to use the app for 4 weeks, and then asks, 'Do you feel less stressed now?' Explain two weaknesses of this plan. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) No control group or baseline: without a comparison group (e.g. workers who do not use the app) or a stress measure before the 4 weeks, any change could be due to other factors (e.g. a quieter month at work). Volunteer sample and leading question: volunteers may be keen on mindfulness and the question suggests the answer, so demand characteristics may produce biased results.
    2 marks per weakness: identified (1) + explained in context (1). Accept: no baseline, no control group, volunteer bias, leading/closed question, self-report bias.
Q7Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A psychologist plans a repeated measures experiment in which participants solve anagrams first after drinking water and then, 15 minutes later, after drinking a caffeinated drink. Explain one problem with this plan and how it could be overcome. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Order effects: all participants do the caffeine condition second, so they may do better because of practice with anagrams (or worse because of fatigue), not because of caffeine. It could be overcome by counterbalancing – half do water first, half caffeine first – on separate days, so caffeine from the first session cannot affect the second, with different but equally difficult anagram sets.
    1 mark: order effects identified. 1 mark: explained in context (practice/fatigue/carry-over of caffeine). 1 mark: counterbalancing / separate days. 1 mark: explanation of how this solves the problem.
Q8Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: a researcher observes shoppers at a supermarket in Karachi to see whether they buy more when music is playing. She sits near the till and records purchases from the till receipt for 30 minutes, on one day each week, for four weeks. Evaluate this design in terms of reliability and validity. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Reliability: a single observer is used and receipts are objective records, so the data should be consistent; however, only 30 minutes on one day each week may not represent the store and days differ. Validity: till receipts show purchases but not whether music caused them; other factors (day, promotions, customers) may affect purchasing; the researcher's presence may change behaviour. Credit for a suggested improvement.
    1 mark each for a reliability strength and weakness with reference to the design (max 2); 1 mark each for a validity strength and weakness (max 2); 1 mark for linking to the aim (causation vs correlation/confounding); 1 mark for a suggested improvement.
Q9Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist tests reaction times and recruits only psychology undergraduates from one university. She then claims the results show the effect of caffeine on reaction time in the general population. Explain one sampling problem with this design. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest a sampling method that would improve the generalisability of the findings, and explain how it would work. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The sample is biased and not representative: psychology students may have different ages, alertness, caffeine habits, or test familiarity from the general population, so generalisability is limited.
    1 mark for identifying the sampling problem (unrepresentative/biased sample); 1 mark for explaining the effect on generalisation to the population.
  2. (b) Random sampling from a list of adults of different ages and backgrounds in the population (e.g. a regional census list of households), so every member has an equal chance of being selected.
    1 mark for naming a suitable method (random, stratified or opportunity sampling with justification); 1 mark for describing how it is done; 1 mark for explaining why it improves representativeness.
Q10Evaluating the design · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: a researcher tells participants that a short study is about 'food tastes'. Participants are then given a tray of snacks alone in a room, and secretly counted through a one-way mirror to see how many snacks they eat while they believe they are unobserved. Identify one ethical issue in this plan and explain how debriefing could address it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Issue: deception and lack of informed consent, plus invasion of privacy (covert observation without knowing). Debriefing could explain the real purpose, reveal the observation, allow participants to withdraw their data, and reassure them about confidentiality.
    1 mark for an ethical issue identified; 1 mark for explaining why it is a problem; 1 mark for a relevant feature of debriefing; 1 mark for explaining how debriefing addresses the issue (e.g. right to withdraw data).
Q11Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans a correlational study of the relationship between time spent reading for pleasure and vocabulary in 11-year-olds. She uses a 30-item vocabulary test and asks parents to estimate their child's weekly reading time. Explain one strength and one weakness of using parents' estimates as a measure of reading time. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: parents see their child's daily routine at home and can report on reading that the child might forget, and it is quick and cheap to collect from many families. Weakness: estimates may be inaccurate – parents may not know about reading done in the child's room, or may exaggerate to appear good parents (social desirability), reducing validity. A reading diary could be more accurate.
    2 marks for strength (identified + explained). 2 marks for weakness (identified + explained).
Q12Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A researcher plans to study whether people help a stranger more in a small town than in a big city. She will have a confederate drop a bag of oranges in the main market of a small town in Punjab and in a busy market in Karachi, and record whether passers-by help. Evaluate this plan in terms of its validity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: it is a field setting with real passers-by who do not know they are being studied, so behaviour is natural and has high ecological validity. Weakness: other differences between the two markets (crowd size, time of day, the local language, how busy people are) are uncontrolled and could explain any difference in helping, so it is not certain that town/city size caused the difference; the confederate's behaviour should be standardised and the times of day matched.
    2 marks for a point supporting validity (identified + explained). 2 marks for a point against validity (identified + explained), e.g. extraneous variables, sample bias, confederate inconsistency.
Q13Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: A psychologist plans to interview 6 employees about their experience of working night shifts, using an unstructured interview. Explain one strength and one weakness of using an unstructured interview, and suggest one improvement to the plan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: the interviewer can follow up whatever the employee says with new questions, gathering rich qualitative detail about their experience of night shifts that was not anticipated. Weakness: each interview is different, so answers are hard to compare and the data are harder to analyse and replicate (low reliability); the interviewer's own views may also influence the questions. Improvement: use a semi-structured interview with a fixed set of core questions plus follow-ups, and increase the sample to 20 employees from different shifts and companies.
    2 marks strength, 2 marks weakness (each identified + explained in context). 2 marks improvement (suggestion + why it helps).
Q14Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical plan: Dr Naveed plans to test whether a one-hour hand-hygiene training session reduces errors in nurses in a Lahore hospital. He observes 20 nurses for two weeks before training and two weeks after, counting hand-hygiene errors on a clipboard. The nurses know he is observing them. Evaluate this design, referring to at least two of: validity, reliability, demand characteristics and ethics. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: pre/post design allows change over time to be measured; observation gives direct behavioural data; counts are objective if categories are clear. Weaknesses: no control group, so improvements could be due to time, seasonal changes, or hospital-wide campaigns (poor internal validity); awareness of observation may change behaviour (demand characteristics and reactivity; low ecological validity); nurses' consent is needed and 'watched' nurses may feel pressured; reliability depends on clear categories and a single observer, so inter-observer reliability should be checked; the sample of 20 from one hospital limits generalisation.
    Mark bands: 1–3 descriptive points on the design with limited evaluation; 4–6 valid evaluation of at least two named concepts with reasons (validity/reliability/demand characteristics/ethics); 7–10 detailed, balanced evaluation with specific links to the study, including a justified improvement such as a control group, covert or blind observation, inter-observer checks or a consent process that avoids deception.
Q15Evaluating the design · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A researcher studies whether online reviews affect purchase decisions. All participants are female students aged 18–22 from one college. Each reads either a five-star or a two-star review of a set of headphones costing Rs 3,500, then rates how likely they would be to buy it on a scale of 1 to 10. The researcher then concludes that 'negative reviews reduce buying among shoppers in Pakistan'. Evaluate the design and the conclusion. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Points: the sample is unrepresentative (one gender, narrow age, one college) so the conclusion cannot be generalised to shoppers in Pakistan; the rating is a single hypothetical question, so it measures intention not actual purchasing (low ecological validity, social desirability in hypothetical choices); the experimental design allocates conditions (IV = review type) which supports cause, but if participants were not randomly allocated, participant variables might differ; price and brand are controlled only if the same headphones are used; the conclusion overreaches the data.
    Mark bands: 1–3 limited, descriptive comments; 4–7 evaluation of design (sample, measure, validity) with links to conclusion; 8–10 balanced evaluation that identifies the overgeneralisation, the nature of the DV (intention vs behaviour), and suggests improvements (a representative sample, a behavioural measure such as a real choice, random allocation).
Q16Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher tests whether chewing gum improves concentration during a 20-minute proofreading task. Write a directional (one-tailed) hypothesis for this study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one control the researcher should use in this study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants who chew gum during a 20-minute proofreading task will detect more spelling errors than participants who do not chew gum.
    1 mark: both conditions of the IV stated (gum vs no gum). 1 mark: operationalised DV with a direction (more errors detected).
  2. (b) All participants proofread the same passage containing the same number of errors (e.g. 30), in the same quiet room.
    B1 for any relevant control (same passage, same time limit, same flavour of gum, same room/time of day).
Q17Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: an experiment tests whether students in Karachi solve more puzzles when working with background traffic noise or in silence. Explain one reason for using an independent measures design in this study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of using an independent measures design in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each student only does the puzzles once, so they cannot improve with practice or become bored between conditions (no order effects), and they are less likely to guess the aim of the study.
    1 mark: avoids order effects (practice/fatigue) or demand characteristics. 1 mark: linked to the puzzles/noise scenario.
  2. (b) Differences in puzzle-solving ability between the two groups (participant variables) could cause the difference in scores rather than the noise.
    1 mark: participant variables. 1 mark: in context (puzzle ability, intelligence, motivation).
Q18Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: Ayesha tests whether a three-minute breathing exercise lowers pulse rate. She records the pulse rate of 20 students before and after the exercise. Identify the experimental design used. [1]
  2. (b) Explain one way that participant variables could affect the results of this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Repeated measures (within participants) design.
    1 mark for repeated measures (or within-subjects).
  2. (b) Students' fitness or anxiety levels before the test could affect resting pulse, and the 'before' reading could be affected by excitement or walking to the room, so the change may not be caused by the exercise alone.
    1 mark for naming a participant variable (fitness, anxiety, prior caffeine); 1 mark for explaining how it could affect pulse readings or the difference.
Q19Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a 'control condition' in an experiment. Give one example for a study on whether coffee improves reaction time. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a control condition makes the results of the experiment more valid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A control condition is a condition in which the IV is not applied (or a neutral condition) to provide a baseline for comparison. Example: participants given decaffeinated coffee or water.
    1 mark for a definition (condition without the IV, or baseline comparison); 1 mark for an appropriate example.
  2. (b) Without a control condition, any change in reaction time could be due to time, practice or expectation; comparing with the control shows the change is specifically due to the IV.
    1 mark for linking the control to isolating the effect of the IV; 1 mark for explaining that other causes are ruled out.
Q20Designing an experiment · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Define 'demand characteristics' in an experiment. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way of reducing demand characteristics in an experiment on memory for a list of words. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cues in the experimental situation that lead participants to guess the aim of the study and change their behaviour to fit what they believe the researcher wants.
    1 mark for indicating that participants are influenced by cues; 1 mark for linking to changing behaviour to fit expectations.
  2. (b) Use a single-blind design where participants are not told the aim, or use a cover story so the real purpose is hidden until debriefing.
    1 mark for an appropriate method (single blind, cover story, filler tasks).
Q21Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher wants to test whether a 'growth mindset' video increases how long 12-year-olds persist at a difficult maths task. Explain how she could use a matched pairs design in this study. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest how persistence could be operationalised. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give all children a pre-test of maths ability (and/or a measure of initial persistence). Rank them by score and pair children with the closest scores; then randomly allocate one child of each pair to watch the growth-mindset video and the other to watch a neutral video, before both attempt the maths task.
    1 mark: pre-test on a relevant variable. 1 mark: pairing children with similar scores. 1 mark: one of each pair randomly allocated to each condition.
  2. (b) The number of seconds a child keeps working on an impossible maths problem before choosing to stop (up to a maximum of 10 minutes).
    1 mark: measurable unit (time in seconds/number of attempts). 1 mark: clear definition of start/stop or a maximum.
Q22Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher wants to compare the memory of people who sleep 8 hours with those who sleep 4 hours. Explain why this would be difficult to carry out as a laboratory experiment, and suggest one alternative way to study the question. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Restricting sleep to 4 hours in a lab may harm participants (tiredness, accidents travelling home) and is costly, as they must stay overnight under supervision; also sleeping in a lab is unnatural. Alternative: a natural/quasi-experiment comparing people who already sleep about 4 or 8 hours (measured with a sleep diary or wrist actigraph) on the same memory test.
    1 mark: ethical problem (harm). 1 mark: practical problem (overnight, cost) or validity problem. 1 mark: alternative method named. 1 mark: alternative explained.
Q23Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A repeated measures experiment compares three conditions (quiet, background music, traffic noise) using 24 participants. Counterbalancing is used to control order effects. Calculate the number of different orders in which the three conditions can be presented. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how the 24 participants could be counterbalanced so that each order is used equally often. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why counterbalancing is needed in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 different orders.
    1 mark for 6 (accept working 3 × 2 × 1).
  2. (b) Each of the 6 orders is used with 24 ÷ 6 = 4 participants, allocated randomly to orders.
    1 mark for dividing 24 by 6 to get 4 per order; 1 mark for explaining that each order is used equally and allocation is random.
  3. (c) Order effects such as practice or fatigue could otherwise make later conditions appear better or worse, which would confound the effect of the IV.
    1 mark for naming an order effect (practice, fatigue, boredom); 1 mark for explaining how it would confound the results.
Q24Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to test whether a revision app improves exam scores of students in Islamabad. Explain how a matched pairs design could be used to control participant variables in this experiment. [3]
  2. (b) State one disadvantage of a matched pairs design in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants are matched in pairs on a key characteristic (e.g. pre-test score or prior GPA). One member of each pair is randomly allocated to the app condition and the other to the control condition, so the groups are equivalent on the matching variable.
    1 mark for pairing participants on a relevant variable; 1 mark for random allocation of one per pair to each condition; 1 mark for explaining that this balances participant variables across conditions.
  2. (b) It is time-consuming to find matched pairs; only the matched variable is controlled, and other participant variables can still differ; if a participant drops out, the partner's data must also be removed.
    1 mark for a disadvantage; 1 mark for an explanation specific to the study.
Q25Designing an experiment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why an independent measures design avoids order effects, and give one disadvantage of an independent measures design in an experiment on the effect of colour on mood. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each participant takes part in only one condition, so practice, fatigue or familiarity cannot affect their performance across conditions. Disadvantage: participant variables may differ between the groups, so more participants are needed and random allocation is required.
    1 mark for different participants in each condition; 1 mark for explaining that order effects are therefore avoided; 1 mark for a disadvantage; 1 mark for an explanation linked to the study.
Q26Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to test whether listening to Qur'an recitation or calm instrumental music before an exam reduces anxiety more than sitting in silence. Describe how she could conduct a laboratory experiment to investigate this.Your plan must include details about:• the experimental design• how anxiety would be measured• controls. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: IV = 10 minutes of recitation, 10 minutes of instrumental music, or 10 minutes of silence before a timed 15-minute mental arithmetic test (an exam-like task). Independent measures design with random allocation (e.g. names drawn from a box), 30 university students per group, to avoid order effects and practice effects on the test. DV = anxiety, measured by a 10-item self-report rating scale (1 = not at all anxious, 5 = extremely anxious; total 10–50) completed just before the test, plus heart rate measured with a finger pulse monitor. Controls: same room, lighting and temperature; same headphones and volume (e.g. 60 dB); standardised instructions read from a script; same test paper; all tested at the same time of day; participants who are already on anxiety medication excluded. Procedure: consent, baseline heart rate, listening phase, rating scale + heart rate, test, debrief.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: design named and justified (independent measures with random allocation, or repeated measures with counterbalancing); IV conditions operationalised (duration, what is played); DV operationalised (rating scale with anchors and/or heart rate); controls (setting, volume, instructions, time of day); sample and procedure; ethics (consent, right to withdraw, debrief).
Q27Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to find out whether the colour of the paper on which a test is printed (white or yellow) affects how many words students recall. Describe how she could conduct an experiment to investigate this using a repeated measures design.Your plan must include details about:• the materials• how order effects will be controlled• the sample. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example plan: Materials – two lists of 20 concrete, two-syllable nouns of similar frequency (list A and list B), each printed in black 14-point font on either white or yellow A4 paper; a stopwatch; blank answer sheets. Procedure – each participant studies a list for 2 minutes, does a 1-minute distractor task (counting backwards in 3s), then writes down as many words as they remember in 2 minutes. They do this twice, once with each paper colour. Order effects – counterbalancing: half do white then yellow, half yellow then white; different but matched word lists in each condition, with list–colour pairing also counterbalanced; a 10-minute break between conditions to reduce fatigue. Sample – 40 Grade 12 students from two colleges in Lahore, chosen by random sampling from class lists (each name given a number, numbers drawn by computer), with parental consent for under-18s. DV – number of words correctly recalled out of 20.
    Level 4 (8–10): plan is detailed, coherent and could be carried out; all named features are included and explained; appropriate psychological terminology used throughout. Level 3 (5–7): plan is mostly clear and could be carried out with some gaps; most named features included; some terminology. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan, some features missing or unclear, limited terminology. Level 1 (1–2): brief, muddled or not the method asked for. 0: no creditable response. Creditworthy content: materials described (word lists matched for length/frequency, paper colour, font); counterbalancing explained (half each order) and matched lists; sample with technique and size; DV operationalised; standardised procedure; ethics.
Q28Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Plan a laboratory experiment to test whether watching a short stress-inducing video (a timed, unfamiliar maths quiz) affects the accuracy of short-term memory in university students in Karachi. Describe the design, the IV and DV with how each is operationalised, the controls you would use, and how you would allocate participants to conditions. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Design: independent measures or matched pairs with two conditions (stress video vs neutral video of similar length), or repeated measures with counterbalancing. IV: type of video (stress or neutral); operationalised as the video shown. DV: number of items recalled correctly from a list of 15 words after a 2-minute delay. Controls: same room, same lighting, same time of day, same instructions, same word list, same delay, filler task, participants without caffeine or sleep deprivation, standardised debriefing. Allocation: random allocation (or matching on baseline memory score) to avoid participant variables.
    Mark bands: 1–3 limited plan with few specific details; 4–7 a clear design with IV, DV and some controls; 8–10 full, operational plan with a sound allocation method and justified controls (e.g. standardising the time of day, order effects, demand characteristics).
Q29Designing an experiment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) A researcher in Lahore wants to know whether noise from a generator affects the speed of simple addition. She tests participants in a room next to a generator and claims the noise caused slower work. Identify two situational variables that she has not controlled and explain how each could have affected the results, and suggest how a laboratory experiment could be designed to control them. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. heat from the generator, room distractions or other people nearby, time of day, familiarity with the task, tester behaviour.
