Q1Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Define the term 'validity' in psychological research. [1]
  2. (b) Define 'ecological validity'. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Validity is the extent to which a study or measure tests or measures what it claims to test or measure.
    1 mark: measures what it intends to measure (accept 'accuracy of the findings / conclusions').
  2. (b) The extent to which the findings of a study can be generalised to real-life settings and everyday behaviour.
    1 mark: findings apply to real-world situations / everyday life.
Q2Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study tested memory by asking university students in Lahore to learn lists of 30 unrelated nonsense syllables in a quiet laboratory. Explain one reason why this study may be low in ecological validity. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one change that would increase the ecological validity of this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Learning nonsense syllables in silence is not like everyday memory tasks, such as remembering a shopping list or a conversation in a noisy setting, so the results may not reflect how people remember in real life.
    1 mark: identifies artificial task/setting. 1 mark: explains why it differs from everyday memory, linked to the scenario.
  2. (b) Ask participants to remember a realistic list, e.g. items to buy at a bazaar, while background market noise is played (or test them in a real shop).
    1 mark: realistic task or setting. 1 mark: detail showing how it is more like everyday life.
Q3Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'demand characteristics'. [2]
  2. (b) In a hypothetical experiment in Islamabad, participants drink either a caffeinated or a decaffeinated cola and then complete a reaction-time task. Suggest one way of reducing demand characteristics in this experiment. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cues in a study that tell participants what the aim is, so they change their behaviour – for example to help the researcher (or to spoil the results) – rather than acting naturally.
    1 mark: features/cues revealing the aim. 1 mark: participants change behaviour, reducing validity.
  2. (b) Use a single-blind procedure: both drinks look and taste the same and participants are not told which one they received or that the study is about caffeine (e.g. say it is about 'drink preference and attention').
    1 mark: single-blind / deception about aim / identical drinks. 1 mark: linked to the caffeine study.
Q4Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by 'social desirability bias' and suggest why it may affect a questionnaire about how often teenagers in Karachi help with household chores. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one way the researcher could reduce this bias. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Social desirability bias is when participants give answers that make them look good rather than truthful ones. Teenagers may over-report how often they help at home because helping parents is seen as respectful and expected, so the results would overestimate helping.
    1 mark: definition – answering to present oneself favourably. 1 mark: application – over-report chores. 1 mark: reason – helping is valued / expected.
  2. (b) Make the questionnaire anonymous (no names, sealed collection box).
    1 mark: anonymity / confidentiality / filler questions / compare with parent reports.
Q5Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a single-blind and a double-blind procedure. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a double-blind procedure would improve the validity of a hypothetical trial testing whether a herbal tea improves sleep quality. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a single-blind procedure the participants do not know which condition they are in (or the aim). In a double-blind procedure neither the participants nor the researcher who collects the data know which condition each participant is in.
    1 mark: single-blind – participants unaware. 1 mark: double-blind – participants and data collector both unaware.
  2. (b) If the researcher rating sleep quality does not know who drank the herbal tea, their expectations cannot bias their ratings (researcher bias), and participants cannot react to knowing they received the 'real' tea (placebo/demand effects), so any difference is more likely due to the tea.
    1 mark: removes researcher bias / expectations. 1 mark: removes participant expectation effects; difference due to IV.
Q6Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between an extraneous variable and a confounding variable. [2]
  2. (b) In a hypothetical study, Class A in a Lahore school is taught spelling with a phone app in the morning, and Class B is taught with workbooks in the afternoon. Identify one confounding variable and explain how it reduces validity. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) An extraneous variable is any variable other than the IV that might affect the DV. A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that has actually varied systematically with the IV, so it is impossible to tell whether the IV or the confound caused the change in the DV.
    1 mark: extraneous – any variable other than the IV that could affect the DV. 1 mark: confounding – varies with the IV/conditions, so effect cannot be attributed to the IV.
  2. (b) Time of day: pupils may be more alert in the morning, so if Class A scores higher it could be due to alertness rather than the app, so the study may not be measuring the effect of the teaching method.
    1 mark: identifies confound (time of day / different class / different teacher). 1 mark: explains why the DV difference cannot be attributed to the method.
Q7Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist designs a new 'exam stress' questionnaire for O Level students in Rawalpindi. Explain what is meant by 'face validity' and suggest how it could be checked for this questionnaire. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Face validity is whether a measure looks, on the surface, as if it measures what it claims to measure. It could be checked by asking experts (e.g. school counsellors or psychologists) and some students to read the questions and judge whether they appear to be about exam stress.
    1 mark: definition – appears to measure what it should. 1 mark: method – experts/others judge items. 1 mark: application to exam stress questionnaire.
Q8Validity · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why using a placebo condition can improve the validity of a drug trial. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants in both conditions believe they may be receiving the drug, so any improvement due simply to expecting to get better (the placebo effect) occurs in both groups; a greater improvement in the drug group can therefore be attributed to the drug itself.
    1 mark: controls for expectation/placebo effect. 1 mark: difference can then be attributed to the drug.
Q9Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher wants to measure 'aggression' in children in a school playground in Peshawar. Explain why it is important to operationalise this variable, and give two examples of operationalised behaviours. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Operationalising means defining the variable in terms of specific, observable behaviours so it can be measured precisely; this means observers record the same thing, improving validity and reliability. Examples: pushing another child with both hands; hitting another child with a closed fist; shouting an insult at another child; kicking.
    1 mark: definition of operationalisation. 1 mark: why – precise/measurable, so the measure is valid/consistent. 1 mark each for two observable behaviours (max 2).
Q10Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A new 10-item self-esteem questionnaire is given to 50 adults in Dubai, who also complete a well-established self-esteem scale. 46 of the 50 adults are placed in the same category (high or low self-esteem) by both measures. Calculate the percentage of adults placed in the same category. [1]
  2. (b) Name this way of checking validity and explain what the result suggests. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 92 %
    B1: 46 ÷ 50 × 100 = 92 %.
  2. (b) Concurrent validity (comparing a new measure with an established one taken at the same time). The high level of agreement (92 %) suggests the new questionnaire measures self-esteem in much the same way as the established scale, so it appears to be valid.
    1 mark: concurrent validity. 1 mark: explanation of method – compare with an established measure. 1 mark: interpretation – high agreement supports validity.
Q11Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a laboratory experiment usually has high internal validity but may have low ecological validity. Use an example of a laboratory experiment in your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a lab the researcher controls extraneous variables (e.g. noise, lighting, instructions), so changes in the DV can be confidently linked to the IV – high internal validity. But the setting and tasks are often artificial, so behaviour may differ from real life – low ecological validity. Example: a lab study of distraction where participants solve puzzles while a recording of a phone ringing is played, which controls noise exactly but is unlike being distracted in a busy office.
    1 mark: control of extraneous variables. 1 mark: cause and effect / internal validity. 1 mark: artificial setting/task reduces ecological validity. 1 mark: suitable example.
Q12Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe researcher bias and explain how it could affect a hypothetical observation of whether boys or girls share more toys at a nursery in Multan. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one way of reducing researcher bias in this observation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Researcher bias is when the researcher's expectations influence how they collect or interpret data. If the observer expects girls to share more, they may record ambiguous behaviour (e.g. a girl passing a toy) as sharing but code the same behaviour by a boy differently, so the data would be biased towards the hypothesis.
    1 mark: definition. 1 mark: application – observer's expectation about girls/boys. 1 mark: effect – biased coding of ambiguous acts, findings invalid.
  2. (b) Use observers who do not know the hypothesis (blind observers), or two observers with clear behaviour categories.
    1 mark: blind observers / operationalised categories / inter-rater checks.
Q13Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by standardisation and explain why it improves validity. Use any examples. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Standardisation means keeping procedures, instructions and materials the same for every participant (e.g. the same written instructions, the same timing and the same room). This means differences in the DV are not caused by differences in how participants were treated, so the IV is more likely to be the cause; for example, if one group were given clearer instructions, their better scores might be due to the instructions rather than the IV.
    1 mark: definition. 1 mark: example. 1 mark: reduces extraneous/situational variables. 1 mark: conclusions about the IV more valid (with example).
Q14Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study in Nairobi asked shoppers to rate a new advertisement while a researcher stood beside them holding a clipboard with the company logo. Explain two threats to validity in this procedure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Demand characteristics / social desirability: shoppers can see the company logo and the researcher watching, so they may give more positive ratings to please the researcher. 2. Researcher (investigator) effects: the researcher's presence, tone or body language may influence how shoppers answer, so ratings may not reflect their true opinions.
    For each threat: 1 mark identification, 1 mark explanation linked to the scenario.
Q15Validity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher measured 'helpfulness' in a hypothetical study by counting how many times participants said they would help a stranger in a questionnaire. Explain one reason why this measure may lack validity, and suggest a more valid measure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) What people say they would do is not necessarily what they actually do: they may overestimate their helpfulness because of social desirability, so the questionnaire may measure self-image rather than helping. A more valid measure would be to observe real helping, e.g. a confederate drops a bag of oranges in a shopping centre and the observer records whether passers-by help pick them up within 10 seconds.
    1 mark: self-report vs actual behaviour. 1 mark: social desirability explanation. 1 mark: suggests behavioural measure. 1 mark: operationalised detail.
Q16Validity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Two researchers disagree. Sana says a field experiment is always more valid than a laboratory experiment. Haris says a laboratory experiment is more valid. Explain who you agree with. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both are partly right because validity has different types. Sana: field experiments take place in natural settings, so participants behave more naturally and findings have higher ecological validity, and participants may not know they are in a study, reducing demand characteristics. Haris: laboratory experiments control extraneous variables more tightly, so they have higher internal validity – we can be more confident that the IV caused the DV. Conclusion: neither is 'always' more valid; the best choice depends on whether control or realism matters more for the research question.
    1 mark: Sana's point explained. 1 mark: Haris's point explained. 1 mark: distinguishes internal vs ecological validity. 1 mark: reasoned conclusion.
Q17Validity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study investigated whether a mindfulness app reduces stress in nurses at a hospital in Karachi. Nurses chose whether to use the app (20 did) or not (20 did not). After four weeks, all nurses rated their stress on a scale of 1 to 10. Explain three reasons why the conclusion 'the app reduces stress' may not be valid. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Self-selection/participant variables: nurses who chose the app may already have been more motivated or less stressed, so differences may not be due to the app. 2. Demand characteristics / placebo: app users knew they were using a stress-reduction tool and may have rated themselves lower to fit expectations. 3. No baseline: stress was only measured afterwards, so we cannot tell whether stress actually changed. Also: other uncontrolled variables (e.g. different shifts or wards) may have affected stress.
    For each of three reasons: 1 mark identification, 1 mark explanation linked to the nurses/app study.
Q18Validity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by 'ecological validity' and 'demand characteristics', using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ecological validity: how far findings can be applied to real-life settings; it depends on the setting, task and materials being realistic, e.g. a study of road-crossing decisions using a virtual-reality street is lower in ecological validity than observing pedestrians at a real crossing. Demand characteristics: cues that reveal the aim of the study so participants change their behaviour, e.g. if participants are asked about their mood before and after watching a comedy, they may guess the aim and report feeling happier. Both affect whether results reflect genuine behaviour.
    3 marks for each concept: 1 mark definition, 1 mark elaboration, 1 mark example.
Q19Validity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a researcher could conduct a laboratory experiment to investigate whether background music affects reading comprehension, so that the study is as valid as possible. Include the IV and DV, the design, controls and how validity is protected. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) IV: background music (instrumental music at a set volume vs silence). DV: reading comprehension score – number correct out of 20 multiple-choice questions on a 600-word passage. Design: independent measures (to avoid practice effects with the same passage), random allocation from a sample of e.g. 40 sixth-form students. Controls: same passage and questions, same 10-minute reading time, same room, lighting, time of day, headphones at same volume; standardised written instructions. Validity: single-blind cover story ('a study of reading speed') to reduce demand characteristics; the person marking answers does not know the condition (double-blind marking); exclude participants who are not fluent in the language of the passage; pilot study to check the questions measure comprehension (face validity). Ethics: informed consent, right to withdraw, debrief.
    Level 4 (9–10): IV and DV operationalised; appropriate design with allocation; several specific controls; at least two explicit validity measures; procedure replicable. Level 3 (6–8): most features with some detail. Level 2 (3–5): basic plan, little detail on validity. Level 1 (1–2): vague.
Q20Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Define 'reliability'. [1]
  2. (b) Outline how the test-retest method could be used to check the reliability of a questionnaire on screen-time habits. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Reliability is the consistency of a measure or procedure: whether it gives the same results if it is repeated.
    1 mark: consistency / same results on repetition.
  2. (b) Give the same questionnaire to the same participants twice, a suitable time apart (e.g. two weeks), and compare (correlate) the two sets of scores; a strong positive correlation shows the questionnaire is reliable.
    1 mark: same people, same test, two occasions with time gap. 1 mark: compare/correlate scores – high agreement = reliable.
Q21Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Two observers recorded the number of times a toddler at a day-care centre in Islamabad made eye contact with a carer during 20 one-minute periods. They agreed on 18 of the 20 periods. Calculate the percentage agreement. [1]
  2. (b) Explain what this result shows about the observation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 90 %
    B1: 18 ÷ 20 × 100 = 90 %.
  2. (b) It shows high inter-rater (inter-observer) reliability: the two observers recorded the behaviour in a consistent way, so the behaviour categories were clear and the data are not just the opinion of one observer.
    1 mark: inter-rater reliability is high. 1 mark: explanation – observers consistent / categories clear.
Q22Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between reliability and validity, using an example of a bathroom scale. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Reliability is consistency, validity is accuracy. A scale that shows 2 kg too heavy every time is reliable (it gives the same reading each time) but not valid (the reading is not the true weight). A valid scale must also be reliable, but a reliable scale is not necessarily valid.
    1 mark: reliability = consistency. 1 mark: validity = measures what it should / accuracy. 1 mark: example showing reliable but not valid.
Q23Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a standardised procedure helps to make a study reliable. [2]
  2. (b) A hypothetical study in Lahore measured how long shoppers looked at a shop window. The researcher timed them with a hand-held stopwatch. Suggest one reason why this measurement may not be reliable and how it could be improved. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) If every participant is given exactly the same instructions, materials and conditions, the procedure can be repeated in the same way by the same or other researchers, so results should be consistent if the study is replicated.
    1 mark: same procedure for all participants; 1 mark: allows replication / consistent results.
  2. (b) The researcher's reaction time in starting and stopping the watch would vary from shopper to shopper, so times would be inconsistent. Video-record the window and time the looks frame by frame (or use two timers and compare).
    1 mark: human timing error/inconsistent judgement of start and stop; 1 mark: improvement (video, automatic sensor, two timers).
Q24Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

In a hypothetical observation of turn-taking among children playing ludo in Hyderabad, two observers coded the same 50 one-minute intervals. They agreed on 46 intervals.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage agreement between the two observers. [1]
  2. (b) Explain what this result suggests about the observation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 92 %
    46 ÷ 50 × 100 = 92 % (B1).
  2. (b) Agreement above 80 % is usually seen as good, so the observers were coding behaviour consistently: the behavioural categories had good inter-rater reliability.
    1 mark: high/good agreement (above 80 %); 1 mark: inter-rater reliability is good / categories clear.
Q25Reliability · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by test–retest reliability. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the time between the two tests should be neither too short nor too long. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Giving the same test to the same participants on two occasions, separated by a suitable time, and checking that their scores are similar (strongly positively correlated).
    1 mark: same test, same people, twice; 1 mark: results compared/correlated for consistency.
  2. (b) If too short, participants may remember their earlier answers and copy them, making the test look more reliable than it is; if too long, the thing being measured (e.g. mood or skill) may have really changed.
    1 mark: too short → remembering answers/practice; 1 mark: too long → genuine change in the trait.
Q26Reliability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe two ways of improving the inter-rater reliability of an observation of customer behaviour in a shopping mall. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Operationalise the behaviour categories clearly (e.g. 'picks up a product and reads the label for at least 3 seconds'), so both observers know exactly what to record. 2. Train the observers together with practice recordings (e.g. video clips of shoppers) and compare scores until they agree highly before the real observation.
    For each: 1 mark identification, 1 mark explanation linked to the mall context. Accept also: time sampling with fixed intervals; use of a coding sheet; video recording for later checking.

Q28Reliability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher is interviewing 30 shopkeepers in Rawalpindi about stress at work. Describe two ways she could make the interviews more reliable. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Use a structured interview with the same questions in the same order for every shopkeeper, so all are asked the same thing. 2. Q29Reliability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a pilot study could improve the reliability of a new observation checklist. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researchers could try out the checklist on a small group first and have two observers use it independently. Where they disagree, unclear categories can be redefined or merged and observers trained, so in the main study observers record behaviour consistently.
    1 mark: small trial before main study; 1 mark: find unclear categories/disagreements; 1 mark: revise categories/train observers → consistency.
Q30Reliability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical memory test was given to 140 students in Lahore and repeated three weeks later. 112 students were placed in the same score band (low, medium or high) both times. Calculate the percentage of students placed in the same band. [1]
  2. (b) Suggest two reasons why some students may have been placed in a different band on the second occasion, even if the test is reliable. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 80 %
    B1: 112 ÷ 140 × 100 = 80 %.
  2. (b) 1. Practice effect: students may remember some items from the first test and score higher the second time. 2. Situational/participant changes: on one occasion a student may have been tired, ill or anxious (or the room was noisy), which changed their performance. Also: genuine change in memory over three weeks (e.g. learning).
    For each: 1 mark reason, 1 mark explanation linked to the test-retest situation.
Q31Reliability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe 'test-retest reliability' and 'inter-rater reliability', using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Test-retest reliability: the same participants complete the same test on two occasions, separated by enough time to avoid remembering answers but not so long that the trait changes; scores are correlated and a strong positive correlation indicates consistency, e.g. an anxiety questionnaire taken two weeks apart. Inter-rater reliability: two or more observers or raters independently record or score the same behaviour; their scores are compared (correlation or % agreement), e.g. two observers coding aggressive acts in a playground. High agreement means the scoring is consistent and not based on one person's interpretation.
    3 marks each: 1 mark definition, 1 mark how it is assessed, 1 mark example.
Q32Reliability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist in Pakistan has written a new Urdu questionnaire to measure test anxiety in secondary-school students. Describe how she could check the test–retest reliability of the questionnaire, and explain one difficulty she might face. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give the questionnaire to a sample of students (e.g. 50 students from several schools) under standardised conditions. Repeat it with the same students after a suitable gap (e.g. two to three weeks), at a time with similar exam pressure. Compare each student's two scores using a correlation; a strong positive correlation (e.g. +0.8 or above) indicates good reliability. Difficulty: test anxiety may genuinely change if an exam gets closer, or students may remember and repeat their answers, so differences or similarities may not reflect the questionnaire itself.
    Up to 4 marks for the method: same participants; same questionnaire and conditions; suitable time gap; correlate scores / strong positive correlation needed. Up to 2 marks: difficulty identified and explained (real change, memory of answers, drop-out, timing of exams).

Q34Reliability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain, using examples, why a measure can be reliable without being valid, but cannot be valid without being reliable. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A measure is reliable if it gives consistent results. A bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy is reliable (same result each time) but not valid (it does not measure true weight). Similarly, measuring intelligence by head size would give consistent but meaningless results. However, if a measure gives different results each time (e.g. a questionnaire with vague questions), it cannot be accurately measuring what it should, so validity needs reliability.
    1 mark: definitions of reliability/validity; 1 mark: example of reliable but not valid; 1 mark: explanation why unreliable cannot be valid; 1 mark: example/elaboration.
Q35Generalisability · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Define 'generalisability'. [1]

Q37Generalisability · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a volunteer (self-selected) sample may reduce generalisability. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a very small sample reduces generalisability. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) People who volunteer tend to be more interested, confident or helpful than others, so the sample may not be typical of the target population.
    1 mark: volunteers share particular characteristics; 1 mark: so not representative.
  2. (b) With few participants, individual differences (e.g. one unusual person) have a big effect on the results, and the sample is unlikely to include the variety of people in the population.
    1 mark: unusual individuals have a large effect; 1 mark: unlikely to represent the range of the population.

Q40Generalisability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the sampling technique and sample size affect the generalisability of a study. Use examples. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A random sample gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, so the sample is more representative, e.g. randomly selecting pupils from the register of every school in a district. An opportunity or volunteer sample may over-represent certain types of people, e.g. volunteers who are more confident. A larger sample reduces the effect of unusual individuals, so findings are more likely to apply to the population; a very small sample may be biased by chance.
    1 mark: representative techniques explained. 1 mark: biased techniques explained. 1 mark: sample size effect. 1 mark: relevant examples.

  2. (b) Explain one reason why the findings may not be generalised to all emergency situations. [2]

  1. (a) About 4450 passengers were observed, a large sample of men and women of different races and ages who used the subway on weekdays, so the results represent a wide range of ordinary people.

  2. (b) The study was on one stretch of a New York subway where passengers could not leave the train between stations; in an open street people can walk away, so helping may be lower there.

Q42Generalisability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why random sampling usually gives more generalisable results than opportunity sampling. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) In random sampling every member of the target population has an equal chance of being chosen, so the sample is not biased by the researcher's choices and is likely to include a range of people. In opportunity sampling the researcher picks whoever is available (e.g. people in one café at one time), who may share characteristics that are not typical of the population.
    1 mark: equal chance in random sampling; 1 mark: reduces researcher bias/more representative; 1 mark: opportunity sample biased to those available.
Q43Generalisability · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

In a hypothetical school in Sialkot, 60 % of the pupils are girls. A researcher studying homework habits recruited 40 pupils, of whom 18 were girls.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of girls in the sample. [1]
  2. (b) Explain whether the sample is representative of the school in terms of gender. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest how the sample could be chosen to match the school's gender balance. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 45 %
    18 ÷ 40 × 100 = 45 % (B1).
  2. (b) Not fully: girls are 60 % of the school but only 45 % of the sample, so girls are under-represented and boys over-represented.
    1 mark: compares 45 % with 60 %; 1 mark: conclusion — girls under-represented.
  3. (c) Use stratified sampling: select 24 girls and 16 boys (60 % and 40 % of 40) at random.
    1 mark: stratified sampling with correct numbers or description.

  2. (b) Explain one reason why animal findings may still tell us something about human behaviour. [2]

  1. (a) Humans have more complex brains and use language, thinking and social motivation when learning, so what works for rats may not work in the same way for people.

Q45Generalisability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical study in the UK found that children aged 8 to 10 remembered more words when they were presented as pictures. A teacher in Quetta wants to use the findings for her pupils. Evaluate whether the findings can be generalised to her class. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: the memory processes studied (picture superiority) are likely to be similar in all children of this age, and her pupils are in the same age range, so the findings may apply. Against: the children were from a different culture and education system; they may differ in language, familiarity with pictures/screens and teaching style, and the sample size and sampling technique are unknown, so the results may not be representative. Conclusion: she could try it but should check whether it works with her own pupils.

Q46Generalisability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical college in Peshawar has 1200 students: 480 in science, 420 in arts and 300 in commerce. A researcher wants to survey 60 students about their use of the library.

  1. (a) Calculate how many students from each faculty should be included in a stratified sample of 60. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a stratified sample would improve the generalisability of this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Science 24; arts 21; commerce 15
    Science 480 ÷ 1200 × 60 = 24 (B1); arts 420 ÷ 1200 × 60 = 21 (B1); commerce 300 ÷ 1200 × 60 = 15 (B1).
  2. (b) Each faculty is represented in the same proportion as in the college, so the views of students in all three faculties are fairly included and the results better reflect the whole college.
    1 mark: same proportions as population; 1 mark: so sample represents all subgroups.
Q47Generalisability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between generalisability and ecological validity, using an example of each. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Generalisability is whether the findings can be applied from the sample to the wider target population (or other populations), e.g. whether Milgram's results with American men apply to women. Ecological validity is whether the findings can be applied from the research setting and task to real-life settings, e.g. whether shocking a learner in a laboratory reflects obedience at work. A study can have a representative sample but low ecological validity, or the reverse.
    1 mark: definition of generalisability (sample → population); 1 mark: example; 1 mark: definition of ecological validity (setting/task → real life); 1 mark: example.
Q48Generalisability · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical laboratory experiment with 30 psychology undergraduates found that people made more errors on a typing task when a supervisor watched them. A company in Lahore wants to remove supervisors from its data-entry office. Evaluate whether the findings can be generalised to the company's workers. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: the task (typing) is similar to data entry, and being watched may have a general effect on performance. Against: the sample was 30 psychology students, who are younger, less experienced and may guess the aim (demand characteristics), unlike experienced office workers who may be used to supervision. The laboratory setting and short task lack ecological validity; in a real office, supervisors may also help or motivate. So the company should test the idea in its own office first.

Q53Objectivity and subjectivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain how an interviewer's own views could make the data collected in an interview subjective. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way of reducing this problem. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The interviewer might ask leading questions, react positively to answers they agree with, or interpret and record answers in a way that fits what they believe, so the data reflect the interviewer's views as well as the participant's.
    1 mark: way the interviewer influences answers or interpretation; 1 mark: consequence (data biased/subjective).
  2. (b) Use a standardised (structured) interview schedule, or record the interview and have a second person, unaware of the aim, code the answers.
    1 mark.

Q55Objectivity and subjectivity · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Identify one objective measure and one subjective measure used in Milgram's study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one advantage of having both kinds of data in this study. [2]
Show answer

Q56Objectivity and subjectivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Q57Objectivity and subjectivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a 'blind' observer and why using one increases objectivity. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A blind observer does not know the aim of the study or which condition each participant is in. This means their expectations cannot influence what they notice or how they record behaviour, so the data are more objective.

Q58Objectivity and subjectivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A researcher uses a 1–7 scale for observers to rate how 'friendly' customers are towards shop staff. Explain why these ratings are subjective. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two ways of making the ratings more objective. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 'Friendly' is not defined, so each observer uses their own idea of friendliness and their own standards for what counts as a 5 or a 6.
    1 mark: vague/undefined term; 1 mark: depends on personal judgement of each observer.
  2. (b) 1. Define each point of the scale with observable behaviours (e.g. 7 = smiles, greets staff and thanks them). 2. Replace the rating with counts of specific behaviours such as smiles and 'thank you's.
    1 mark each: anchored/defined scale points; counting behaviours; training observers with examples; using two observers and comparing.
Q59Objectivity and subjectivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why thematic analysis of interview transcripts can be subjective, and suggest one way of reducing this. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The researcher decides which themes exist and which quotes belong to each theme; their own expectations and background may lead them to notice themes that fit their views and ignore others. To reduce this, a second researcher could analyse the same transcripts independently and the two sets of themes could be compared (or the themes could be checked with the participants).
    1 mark: researcher chooses themes/interprets meaning; 1 mark: influenced by expectations/background; 1 mark: method to reduce (second analyst, agreed coding, participant checks); 1 mark: explained.
Q60Objectivity and subjectivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hypothetical interview study in Lahore asked mothers open questions about how they cope with their child's illness. Explain why the analysis of these answers may be subjective, and suggest two ways of making it more objective. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Open answers produce qualitative data that must be interpreted; the researcher decides which themes are present, and their own views or expectations may influence this. Ways to increase objectivity: (1) use a clear coding scheme with defined themes decided in advance; (2) have two researchers code the answers independently and check inter-rater agreement (or convert themes into counts).

Q62Objectivity and subjectivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) 'Subjectivity is always a weakness in psychological research.' To what extent do you agree? [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Partly disagree. Subjective data from the researcher (e.g. biased interpretation) are a weakness because they reduce validity and reliability. But participants' subjective experiences, such as feelings, pain or reasons for behaviour, are often what psychologists need to study, and self-reports or qualitative descriptions are the only way to access them; they can give more valid insight than objective numbers alone. So subjectivity of the participant's experience can be a strength, provided the researcher's analysis is kept as objective as possible.
    1 mark: why researcher subjectivity is a weakness; 1 mark: some topics are subjective by nature (feelings, pain); 1 mark: subjective data can increase validity/insight; 1 mark: balanced conclusion.
Q63Objectivity and subjectivity · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Compare the objectivity of the data collected in Perry et al.'s study with the data collected in Piliavin et al.'s study. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Perry et al. measured preferred distance in centimetres in a controlled laboratory with a double-blind procedure, so the main data were highly objective and not affected by researchers' expectations. Piliavin et al.'s observers recorded behaviour in a busy carriage and had to judge features such as passengers' race, sex and location, and how long it took for help to arrive, so some recording involved judgement in difficult conditions. Both collected mainly quantitative data, but Perry's measurement was more objective; Piliavin's observers recorded in a busy, unpredictable setting where judgements could be biased, so observer bias is possible.
    1 mark: Perry's measure objective (distance/double-blind); 1 mark: Piliavin's observers making judgements in a natural setting; 1 mark: similarity (both quantitative); 1 mark: conclusion comparing.
Q64Objectivity and subjectivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A researcher plans to investigate whether children in Lahore who play outdoors are more cooperative at school. Teachers who know which children play outdoors will rate each child's cooperation. Explain two problems with the objectivity of this plan and suggest how each could be solved. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Teachers' expectations: teachers who know which children play outdoors may expect them to be more cooperative and rate them higher (observer/researcher bias). Solution: use raters who do not know which children play outdoors (blind ratings). 2. 'Cooperation' is vague, so each teacher will judge it differently (subjective rating). Solution: operationalise it as observable behaviours (e.g. number of times the child shares materials or helps a classmate in a 30-minute lesson), recorded with a checklist by two observers whose agreement is checked.
    For each problem: 1 mark identified; 1 mark explained in context; 1 mark suitable solution.
