Original practice questions written by HomiLabs in the exam style. They are not Cambridge past paper questions. Try each one before opening the answer.

Q1Organisational work conditions · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between rapid rotation and slow rotation shift systems. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a forward-rotating (morning → evening → night) shift system is thought to be better than a backward-rotating one. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) In rapid rotation workers change shifts every few days (e.g. 2 mornings, 2 evenings, 2 nights); in slow rotation they stay on the same shift for longer, e.g. one to three weeks, before changing.
    1 mark each for a correct description of each system.
  2. (b) The body clock naturally finds it easier to delay (stay up later) than to advance (go to sleep earlier), so moving shifts forward (phase delay) causes less disruption to circadian rhythms and sleep.
    1 mark: link to circadian rhythms/body clock. 1 mark: phase delay is easier than phase advance.
Q2Organisational work conditions · Core · Paper 3 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Identify three types of human error that can lead to accidents at work. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any three of: slips (doing the wrong action, e.g. pressing the wrong button); lapses (forgetting a step); mistakes (wrong plan or decision, e.g. misjudging a situation); violations (deliberately breaking a rule).
    1 mark each for any three; credit an example in place of a definition.
Q3Organisational work conditions · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: A psychologist wants to measure how noise in an open-plan call centre in Karachi affects workers' concentration. Suggest one objective way and one self-report way to measure concentration. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Objective: number of errors made when entering customer details per hour, taken from the call-centre computer system. Self-report: a rating scale at the end of each shift, e.g. 'How easy was it to concentrate today?' 1 = very difficult to 7 = very easy.
    2 marks per measure: 1 for the method, 1 for operationalised detail.
Q4Organisational work conditions · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Saima is the manager of a textile factory in Faisalabad where several minor accidents have happened on the night shift. Suggest how she could use a token economy to reduce accidents. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Workers (or teams) earn tokens/stamps for every week (or month) without an accident or safety violation, and for safety suggestions; tokens are lost if an accident happens. Tokens can be exchanged for goods or vouchers (e.g. groceries, phone credit). The scheme should be explained clearly to all workers, with tokens given promptly so safe behaviour is reinforced (operant conditioning).
    1 mark: tokens earned for accident-free periods/safe behaviour. 1 mark: exchanged for rewards (named). 1 mark: practical detail (team tokens, lost after accident, prompt delivery). 1 mark: link to operant conditioning/positive reinforcement or to Fox et al.'s mining study.
Q5Organisational work conditions · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the debate about determinism versus free will. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why the Swiss cheese model of accidents can be seen as deterministic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Determinism is the view that behaviour is caused by factors outside the person's control (biological or environmental); free will is the view that people can choose how they behave.
    1 mark for each side defined.
  2. (b) It explains accidents as the result of weaknesses ('holes') in organisational layers – management decisions, supervision, conditions – lining up, so an individual worker's error is caused by the system rather than freely chosen.
    1 mark: model attributes accidents to system/organisational factors. 1 mark: so the worker's behaviour is determined by forces outside their control.
Q6Organisational work conditions · Standard · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the findings of research into the effects of open-plan offices on workers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Oldham and Brass studied newspaper employees who moved from a conventional office to an open-plan office (a natural experiment), measuring them before and after the move. Employee satisfaction with work and internal motivation decreased after the move; workers reported fewer boundaries, more interruptions and less privacy, and found it harder to concentrate. Friendship opportunities did not increase as expected. Other research suggests open-plan offices increase noise and distraction but can make communication with supervisors easier, and that more private 'quiet zones' improve satisfaction.
    Level 3 (5–6): accurate, detailed description using psychological terms. Level 2 (3–4): mostly accurate, some detail. Level 1 (1–2): brief or partly inaccurate. Creditworthy: Oldham and Brass (natural/quasi-experiment, newspaper employees, measures before and after); decrease in satisfaction and motivation; reasons (lack of privacy, interruptions, no boundaries, distractions); other findings on noise/communication.
Q7Organisational work conditions · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical data: A company introduced a token economy for safety. In the 12 months before, there were 48 lost-time accidents; in the 12 months after, there were 30. Calculate the percentage reduction in accidents. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why this reduction might not be caused by the token economy. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one strength of using a token economy to improve safety. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 37.5%
    M1: (48 − 30) ÷ 48 × 100. A1: 37.5%.
  2. (b) Other changes happened at the same time (e.g. new machinery, safety training, fewer hours worked or fewer workers), or workers may under-report accidents to keep their tokens, so the fall might not be due to the scheme itself.
    1 mark: alternative explanation identified. 1 mark: explained (no control group / confounding variable / under-reporting).
  3. (c) It gives immediate, concrete rewards for safe behaviour, which reinforces it, and the tokens can be exchanged for rewards each worker values, so it can motivate a large workforce cheaply compared with the cost of accidents.
    1 mark: strength identified. 1 mark: explained in a workplace context.
Q8Organisational work conditions · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate what psychologists have found about the effects of temporal conditions of work (shift work), including a discussion about the usefulness of research. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Usefulness: findings (e.g. Czeisler et al. – moving workers to forward-rotating, slower schedules improved satisfaction, health and productivity at an industrial plant) are directly applied to design better rotas; research on nurses (e.g. Gold et al.) showing more sleep problems and errors in rotating shifts helps hospitals reduce risks. Methodology: many studies are field studies with real workers (high ecological validity) but have many uncontrolled variables (family life, second jobs); self-report sleep diaries may be inaccurate. Generalisability: samples are often one factory or one profession. Individual differences: 'larks' and 'owls' adapt differently, so one rota may not suit everyone. Determinism: biological explanation (circadian rhythms) vs individual choice. Ethics: experimental changes to rotas affect workers' health and livelihoods. Conclusion: research is useful but recommendations should be tailored.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, detailed evaluation with a range of points, the named issue discussed in depth, well supported by studies/examples, clear conclusion. Level 3 (6–8): good range of points, named issue included, mostly supported. Level 2 (3–5): some evaluation, may be one-sided or the named issue only touched on. Level 1 (1–2): brief or anecdotal points. Named issue: usefulness (applications to rota design, health, safety; limits).
Q9Satisfaction at work · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the difference between hygiene factors and motivators in Herzberg's two-factor theory. [2]
  2. (b) Describe the method Herzberg used to develop the two-factor theory. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hygiene factors (e.g. pay, company policy, supervision, working conditions) prevent dissatisfaction when they are adequate but do not create satisfaction; motivators (e.g. achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement) create job satisfaction and motivation.
    1 mark for each with an example.
  2. (b) He interviewed engineers and accountants (about 200) in Pittsburgh using the critical incident technique, asking them to describe times when they felt exceptionally good and exceptionally bad about their jobs.
    1 mark: interviews / critical incident technique. 1 mark: detail – engineers and accountants; times they felt very good or very bad.
Q10Satisfaction at work · Core · Paper 3 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Identify the five facets of job satisfaction measured by the Job Descriptive Index (JDI). [3]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of measuring job satisfaction using the JDI. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The work itself, pay, opportunities for promotion, supervision and co-workers.
    3 marks for all five; 2 marks for four; 1 mark for two or three.
  2. (b) Workers may give socially desirable answers (e.g. not criticise their supervisor) if they fear the employer will see their responses, so the scores may not reflect their true satisfaction.
    1 mark: weakness (social desirability, fixed Yes/No/? answers lack detail, may not cover all aspects). 1 mark: explained.
Q11Satisfaction at work · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Bilal works on a mobile-phone assembly line in Lahore, fitting the same screen into each phone all day. His manager wants to improve his job satisfaction. Suggest how the manager could use job enrichment and job rotation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Job enrichment: give Bilal more responsibility and control, e.g. assembling and testing the whole phone, checking quality of his own work, deciding the order of tasks, so the job becomes more meaningful (vertical loading). Job rotation: move Bilal between different stations (screen fitting, battery fitting, packing) every few days so he has more variety and less boredom.
    2 marks for enrichment (idea + applied to Bilal). 2 marks for rotation (idea + applied to Bilal).
Q12Satisfaction at work · Standard · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe what psychologists have found about measuring job satisfaction, including the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSQ) and the Quality of Work Life (QWL) approach. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) MSQ: a self-report questionnaire measuring satisfaction with many specific aspects (facets) of a job, such as ability utilisation, pay, co-workers, independence and working conditions; long form has 100 items, short form 20 items, each rated on a 5-point scale from very dissatisfied to very satisfied. QWL (Walton): judges a job against broad categories such as adequate and fair compensation, safe and healthy working conditions, opportunity to use and develop capacities, opportunity for growth and security, social integration, constitutionalism (rights), work and total life space, and social relevance. Other measures include the JDI (five facets, Yes/No/?).
    Level 3 (5–6): accurate, detailed description using psychological terms. Level 2 (3–4): mostly accurate, some detail. Level 1 (1–2): brief or partly inaccurate. Credit accurate details of MSQ (facets, long/short form, 5-point scale) and QWL categories.
Q13Satisfaction at work · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 24 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist wants to find out whether nurses in a large hospital in Islamabad are more satisfied with their jobs on day shifts or night shifts. Plan a study to investigate this using a questionnaire.Your plan must include: the sampling technique; at least one rating-scale question; the type of data collected. [10]
  2. (b) Describe the psychological knowledge on which your plan is based. [8]
  3. (c) Explain one reason for your choice of sampling technique. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Example: Sample – stratified sample of 80 nurses: 40 who work mainly day shifts and 40 mainly night shifts, chosen randomly from staff rosters within each group, including different wards. Questionnaire – adapted from the JDI/MSQ facets: work itself, pay, supervision, co-workers, promotion; e.g. 'How satisfied are you with the support from your supervisor?' 1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied (10 items, total 10–50); plus open questions such as 'What would improve your job satisfaction?'. Procedure – anonymous, distributed with sealed return boxes; same instructions for all. Data – quantitative (rating totals, compared using means and a bar chart) and qualitative (themes from open answers). Ethics – consent, confidentiality from hospital management, right to withdraw.
    Level 4 (8–10): detailed, coherent plan with all required features and justification. Level 3 (5–7): mostly clear plan, most features. Level 2 (3–4): basic plan with gaps. Level 1 (1–2): brief or confused.
  2. (b) Herzberg's two-factor theory (hygiene factors such as working conditions vs motivators); job satisfaction measures (JDI five facets, MSQ, QWL); research on temporal conditions – shift work disrupts circadian rhythms and sleep and is linked to lower satisfaction and more errors (e.g. studies of nurses on rotating shifts); questionnaire design (rating scales, open/closed questions, social desirability).
    Level 3 (6–8): accurate, detailed and relevant knowledge clearly linked to the plan. Level 2 (3–5): some relevant knowledge, partly linked. Level 1 (1–2): brief, general.
  3. (c) Stratified sampling ensures equal numbers of day and night nurses (and nurses from different wards) are included, so that the comparison is fair and the sample represents each group of the hospital's staff; random selection within strata reduces researcher bias. Because night-shift nurses are fewer and harder to reach, an opportunity sample would probably over-represent day staff and distort the comparison. A weakness is that full staff lists are needed and it takes longer.
    Level 3 (5–6): well-justified reason linked to the aim, with comparison to an alternative or acknowledgement of a weakness. Level 2 (3–4): reason explained with some link to the plan. Level 1 (1–2): reason stated with little justification.
Q14Satisfaction at work · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate what psychologists have found about job design and job satisfaction, including a discussion about reductionism and holism. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Reductionism/holism: Herzberg reduces satisfaction to two sets of factors, which is easy to test and apply, but ignores how factors interact (e.g. pay can also be a form of recognition); job enrichment approaches (e.g. job characteristics model) look at several features together, which is more holistic. Methodology: Herzberg's critical incident interviews may be biased because people attribute good events to themselves and bad events to the company (self-serving bias); samples of engineers/accountants limit generalisability. Usefulness: job rotation, enlargement and enrichment are widely applied and practical. Individual differences: not everyone wants enriched jobs – some prefer routine. Cultural differences: research mainly Western. Conclusion.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, detailed evaluation with a range of points, the named issue discussed in depth, well supported by studies/examples, clear conclusion. Level 3 (6–8): good range of points, named issue included, mostly supported. Level 2 (3–5): some evaluation, may be one-sided or the named issue only touched on. Level 1 (1–2): brief or anecdotal points. Named issue: reductionism vs holism, explained not just labelled.
Q15Motivation to work · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the three needs in Alderfer's ERG theory. [3]
  2. (b) State one way ERG theory differs from Maslow's hierarchy of needs. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Existence – basic material and physical needs (food, pay, safe conditions); Relatedness – the need for relationships with others (colleagues, family, friends); Growth – the need for personal development and fulfilling one's potential.
    1 mark for each need correctly named and described.
  2. (b) ERG allows more than one need to be active at once and includes frustration–regression (if a higher need is blocked, a person focuses more on a lower need), whereas Maslow says needs are satisfied in a fixed order.
    B1.
Q16Motivation to work · Core · Paper 3 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'intrinsic' and 'extrinsic' motivation. [2]
  2. (b) Give one example of a non-monetary reward an employer could use. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Intrinsic motivation comes from within – doing the work because it is interesting or satisfying in itself. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside rewards or pressures – e.g. pay, bonuses, praise or avoiding punishment.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Any one: praise/recognition (e.g. employee of the month), extra holiday, flexible hours, a better job title, training opportunities.
    B1.
Q17Motivation to work · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hina manages a team of sales staff in a bank in Karachi. Using goal-setting theory, suggest how she could increase their motivation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) She should set specific goals (e.g. open 15 new accounts per month) rather than 'do your best'; goals should be difficult but achievable; she should involve staff in setting goals to increase commitment; and give regular feedback (e.g. weekly progress charts) so they can see how close they are to the goal.
    1 mark each for any four applied points: specific; challenging but achievable; commitment/participation in goal setting; feedback; deadline; rewards for reaching goals. Must be applied to Hina's team for full marks.
Q18Motivation to work · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) In Vroom's expectancy (VIE) theory, motivational force = expectancy × instrumentality × valence. An employee believes there is a 0.8 probability that extra effort will lead to high performance (expectancy), a 0.5 probability that high performance will lead to a bonus (instrumentality), and values the bonus at 0.9 (valence, scale 0–1). Calculate the motivational force. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the motivational force would be zero if the employee believed the bonus would never be paid. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.36
    M1: 0.8 × 0.5 × 0.9. A1: 0.36.
  2. (b) Instrumentality would be 0; because the terms are multiplied, a zero for any one of them makes the whole motivational force zero, however high expectancy and valence are.
    1 mark: instrumentality = 0. 1 mark: multiplication means the product is zero.
Q19Motivation to work · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the nomothetic versus idiographic debate. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why McClelland's theory of needs (achievement, affiliation and power) could be seen as idiographic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The nomothetic approach looks for general laws that apply to everyone, often using large samples and quantitative data; the idiographic approach focuses on understanding the unique individual in depth, often using qualitative methods such as case studies.
    1 mark for each side.
  2. (b) It recognises that individuals differ in the strength of each need – one employee may be driven mainly by achievement, another by affiliation – and needs are assessed for each person (e.g. through their stories on the Thematic Apperception Test), so motivation is understood at the individual level.
    1 mark: emphasis on individual differences in need strength. 1 mark: elaboration, e.g. individual assessment with TAT, tailoring jobs to the person.
Q20Motivation to work · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate need theories of motivation to work (such as Maslow, Alderfer and McClelland), including a discussion about cultural differences. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cultural differences: Maslow's hierarchy reflects individualistic Western values (self-actualisation at the top); in collectivist cultures such as Pakistan, belonging/family or group needs may be ranked higher, so the order may not be universal. Evidence: little empirical support for a fixed order of needs; ERG is more flexible and better supported (frustration–regression). McClelland's needs are measurable (TAT) and useful for selection, but TAT scoring is subjective and has low reliability. Usefulness: managers can match rewards to needs (security, social events, development). Reductionism: need theories reduce complex motivation to a few needs and ignore cognitive factors such as goals and expectations (goal-setting, VIE). Individual differences: people vary in which needs dominate. Conclusion.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, detailed evaluation with a range of points, the named issue discussed in depth, well supported by studies/examples, clear conclusion. Level 3 (6–8): good range of points, named issue included, mostly supported. Level 2 (3–5): some evaluation, may be one-sided or the named issue only touched on. Level 1 (1–2): brief or anecdotal points. Named issue: cultural differences, developed with examples.
Q21Leadership and management · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the two dimensions of leadership behaviour identified in the Ohio State studies. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one problem with the 'great person' theory of leadership. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Initiating structure – how far the leader defines and organises tasks, roles and goals for the group; consideration – how far the leader shows concern, trust and respect for followers' feelings and wellbeing.
    1 mark for each dimension.
  2. (b) It assumes leaders are born with special traits, but research has not found a consistent set of traits shared by all effective leaders, and it ignores the situation and followers, so it cannot explain why a leader succeeds in one setting but fails in another.
    1 mark: problem identified (no consistent traits, ignores situation, gender/cultural bias, retrospective). 1 mark: explained.
Q22Leadership and management · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe Muczyk and Reimann's four styles of leadership. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Directive autocrat – makes decisions alone and closely supervises how work is done. Permissive autocrat – makes decisions alone but lets subordinates decide how to carry them out. Directive democrat – involves subordinates in decisions but closely supervises how work is done. Permissive democrat – involves subordinates in decisions and gives them freedom in carrying out the work.
    1 mark for each style correctly described (decision-making: autocratic/democratic; execution: directive/permissive).
Q23Leadership and management · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Mr Farooq has just been promoted to supervise a team of experienced software engineers in Islamabad who are working on a creative new app. Using Fiedler's contingency theory, suggest how he could decide on the most effective leadership style. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) He could complete the Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) scale to see whether he is task-oriented (low LPC) or relationship-oriented (high LPC). He should then judge how favourable the situation is: leader–member relations (new to the team, so may be moderate), task structure (a creative project is low in structure) and position power (as supervisor, moderate–high). In moderately favourable situations Fiedler predicts relationship-oriented leaders do best, so if he is task-oriented he might change the situation (e.g. build relations) since Fiedler sees style as fixed.
    1 mark: LPC scale to identify style. 1 mark: three situational factors named. 1 mark: applied to Mr Farooq's situation. 1 mark: correct prediction/advice (relationship-oriented best in moderate; task-oriented in very favourable/unfavourable; change the situation not the leader).
Q24Leadership and management · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: A researcher uses a questionnaire to measure whether employees in a Lahore factory see their supervisor as a transformational leader. Write two items the questionnaire could include, each linked to a different feature of transformational leadership. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inspirational motivation: 'My supervisor talks enthusiastically about what our team can achieve' (1 = never to 5 = always). Individualised consideration: 'My supervisor spends time helping me develop my own skills' (1 = never to 5 = always). (Others: idealised influence – 'I am proud to work with my supervisor'; intellectual stimulation – 'My supervisor encourages me to find new ways of solving problems'.)
    2 marks per item: 1 for a suitable item with a response scale, 1 for correctly linking it to a feature of transformational leadership (idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration).
Q25Leadership and management · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe what psychologists have found about leadership and the situation, including Fiedler's contingency theory. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fiedler argued that a leader's effectiveness depends on the match between their style and the situation. Style is measured with the Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) scale: low-LPC leaders are task-oriented, high-LPC leaders relationship-oriented. Situational favourableness depends on leader–member relations (good/poor), task structure (high/low) and position power (strong/weak). Task-oriented leaders are most effective when the situation is very favourable or very unfavourable; relationship-oriented leaders are best in moderately favourable situations. Style is seen as fixed, so the situation should be changed to fit the leader. Other situational approaches suggest leaders should adapt their style to followers' readiness/maturity.
    Level 3 (5–6): accurate, detailed description using psychological terms. Level 2 (3–4): mostly accurate, some detail. Level 1 (1–2): brief or partly inaccurate.
Q26Leadership and management · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate universalist and behavioural theories of leadership, including a discussion about individual and situational explanations. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual vs situational: great person and charismatic theories are individual explanations (leadership comes from traits), but they ignore the situation – the same leader may fail in a different context; behavioural theories (Ohio State) focus on what leaders do, which can be learned, and situational/contingency theories (Fiedler) show the interaction. Evidence: Ohio State used large questionnaire studies (LBDQ) – quantitative and reliable, but self-report/followers' ratings may be biased; transformational leadership is linked to follower satisfaction and performance. Usefulness: behavioural theories can be used to train leaders (consideration and structure); great person theory cannot. Cultural/gender bias: great person theory based on historical male leaders. Conclusion: a combination of individual and situational factors explains leadership best.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, detailed evaluation with a range of points, the named issue discussed in depth, well supported by studies/examples, clear conclusion. Level 3 (6–8): good range of points, named issue included, mostly supported. Level 2 (3–5): some evaluation, may be one-sided or the named issue only touched on. Level 1 (1–2): brief or anecdotal points. Named debate: individual vs situational explanations, with examples.
Q27Group behaviour in organisations · Core · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the stages of group development described by Tuckman. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Forming – members meet, are polite and uncertain, and depend on the leader. Storming – conflict over roles, power and ideas. Norming – agreement on rules, roles and ways of working; cohesion grows. Performing – the group works effectively towards its goals. (Adjourning – the group breaks up once the task is finished.)
    1 mark for each of four stages correctly named and described; adjourning can replace one stage.
Q28Group behaviour in organisations · Standard · Paper 3 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A management committee at a cricket equipment company in Sialkot quickly agreed to launch a new bat without any member raising concerns, and the product failed. Suggest how groupthink might explain this and how it could be avoided in future. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Groupthink: in a cohesive group, members want agreement so much that they do not criticise ideas – e.g. self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, pressure on anyone who disagreed, and an illusion of invulnerability. To avoid it, the leader could hold back their own opinion at first, appoint a 'devil's advocate' to challenge the plan, invite outside experts, or use anonymous voting.
    1 mark: definition/explanation of groupthink. 1 mark: named symptom applied to the committee. 1 mark: one way to avoid it. 1 mark: explanation of how it helps.
Q29Group behaviour in organisations · Standard · Paper 3 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Outline the five conflict-handling styles described by Thomas. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Competing – assertive and uncooperative, pursuing one's own concerns at the other's expense. Collaborating – assertive and cooperative, working together to find a solution that satisfies both. Compromising – moderate on both, each side gives up something. Avoiding – unassertive and uncooperative, sidestepping the conflict. Accommodating – unassertive and cooperative, giving in to the other side.
    1 mark for each style named and described (may refer to the dimensions of assertiveness and cooperativeness).
Q30Group behaviour in organisations · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate what psychologists have found about group development and team roles (e.g. Tuckman, Belbin), including a discussion about the usefulness of research. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Usefulness: Tuckman's stages help managers anticipate conflict and support new teams; Belbin's team roles (e.g. plant, shaper, coordinator, monitor evaluator, teamworker, completer finisher) are used to build balanced teams and in training. Limitations: Tuckman's model was based on reviews of small, often therapy and laboratory groups, so may not apply to work teams; groups may not move through stages in order and may return to storming. Belbin's self-perception inventory is self-report and has been criticised for low reliability/validity; roles may change with the task. Reductionism: reducing team behaviour to set stages or nine roles ignores organisational context and culture. Cultural differences in how conflict is expressed. Conclusion: useful frameworks but should be applied flexibly.
    Level 4 (9–10): balanced, detailed evaluation with a range of points, the named issue discussed in depth, well supported by studies/examples, clear conclusion. Level 3 (6–8): good range of points, named issue included, mostly supported. Level 2 (3–5): some evaluation, may be one-sided or the named issue only touched on. Level 1 (1–2): brief or anecdotal points. Named issue: usefulness, discussed with examples of application and its limits.
