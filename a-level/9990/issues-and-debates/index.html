Q1Comparing core studies · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline one similarity between the samples used by Milgram (obedience) and Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans). [2]
  2. (b) Outline one difference between the samples used in these two studies. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both samples were drawn from the USA (Americans living in or near a city), so both studied American adults.
    1 mark for the similarity (e.g. both American / both adults); 1 mark for linking it to both studies (Milgram: men from the New Haven area; Piliavin: passengers on the New York subway).
  2. (b) Milgram's 40 participants were all male volunteers who answered an advertisement; Piliavin's participants were thousands of male and female passengers who did not volunteer and did not know they were in a study.
    1 mark for the difference identified (gender mix / volunteer vs unaware / sample size); 1 mark for detail from both studies.
Q2Comparing core studies · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Identify one feature that the studies by Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) and Andrade (doodling) have in common. [1]
  2. (b) Explain one difference between the participants in the two studies. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both measured memory performance (recognition/identification accuracy in Pozzulo et al.; recall of names and places in Andrade).
    1 mark for any valid shared feature: both cognitive; both measured memory; both laboratory-based; both quantitative data.
  2. (b) Pozzulo et al. tested both children and adults, so age was the independent variable and was not manipulated; Andrade tested only adults (aged 18–55), and the IV (doodling or not) was manipulated by the researcher.
    1 mark for the difference identified; 1 mark for Pozzulo detail; 1 mark for Andrade detail / effect on the type of experiment.
Q3Comparing core studies · Core · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State the research setting used by Milgram (obedience) and by Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans). [2]
  2. (b) Explain one way the difference in setting affected the ecological validity of these two studies. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Milgram: a laboratory at Yale University. Piliavin et al.: a real subway train in New York (a field setting).
    1 mark each: laboratory (accept 'lab'); natural/field setting on a subway train.
  2. (b) Milgram's laboratory was artificial, so participants' behaviour may not match real life, lowering ecological validity. Piliavin's subway was a real setting, so the helping was more natural and ecological validity was higher; however, the researchers had less control over other variables.
    1 mark: lab is artificial or less realistic (Milgram); 1 mark: field setting is natural or more realistic (Piliavin); 1 mark: a trade-off explained, e.g. lab gives control, field gives realism.

  1. (a) Identify one way in which the sample of Bandura et al. (aggression) differed from the sample of Milgram (obedience). [2]

Q5Comparing core studies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Describe the procedure of the study by Hölzel et al. (mindfulness and brain scans). [4]
  2. (b) Explain one similarity and one difference between the study by Hölzel et al. and the study by Dement and Kleitman (sleep and dreams). [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Participants who joined an 8-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) course were given MRI brain scans before and after the course; a waiting-list control group was scanned at the same times without doing the course. Participants recorded their home meditation practice and completed a mindfulness questionnaire. Grey matter concentration in chosen brain areas (e.g. the hippocampus) was compared before and after.
    1 mark each for up to four points: 8-week MBSR course (weekly group sessions plus daily home practice); MRI scans before and after; waiting-list control group; home practice recorded / mindfulness questionnaire; comparison of grey matter concentration in regions of interest such as the left hippocampus.
  2. (b) Similarity: both used objective physiological measuring equipment (MRI scans in Hölzel; EEG recordings of brain waves and eye movements in Dement and Kleitman), giving quantitative data less open to bias. Difference: Hölzel et al. compared an MBSR group with a separate control group (independent measures), whereas Dement and Kleitman had no separate control group – each of the nine participants was woken in different sleep stages (repeated measures).
    Similarity (4): identified 1; explained 1; example from Hölzel 1; example from Dement and Kleitman 1. Difference (4): identified 1; explained 1; example from each study 1 + 1. Other creditworthy points: both biological approach; both small samples; difference in setting (sleep laboratory overnight vs scans at a hospital/medical centre plus normal daily life).
Q6Comparing core studies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Describe the sample used in the study by Baron-Cohen et al. (eyes test). [4]
  2. (b) Explain one similarity and one difference between the study by Baron-Cohen et al. and the study by Andrade (doodling). [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Several groups of adults were tested: a group of adults with Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism, a large group of adults from the general population, a group of university students, and a small group of adults with Tourette syndrome. Most participants were from the UK.
    1 mark for each group correctly described (AS/HFA adults; general population adults; UK university students; adults with Tourette syndrome as a comparison group); credit sample sizes if correct (15, 122, 103, 14).
  2. (b) Similarity: both collected quantitative data from a test (number of correct answers on the 36-item Eyes Test; number of names and places recalled in Andrade), which makes comparison between groups straightforward. Difference: Andrade randomly allocated participants to a doodling or non-doodling condition (a laboratory experiment with a manipulated IV), whereas Baron-Cohen et al. compared pre-existing groups such as adults with and without Asperger syndrome (a quasi-experiment; the IV was not manipulated).
    Similarity (4) and difference (4): 1 identify, 1 explain, 1 + 1 examples from each study. Accept other valid points, e.g. both cognitive approach; both done in controlled conditions; Andrade used deception (surprise memory test) whereas the Eyes Test aim was clear.
Q7Comparing core studies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one similarity between the study by Milgram (obedience) and the study by Perry et al. (personal space) in terms of the research method. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one difference between these two studies in terms of the type of explanation they support. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both were controlled laboratory studies with a standardised procedure, so all participants experienced the same conditions (e.g. the same prods in Milgram; the same nasal spray procedure and distance task in Perry et al.).
    1 mark for the similarity; 1 mark for an example from both studies.
  2. (b) Milgram's results support a situational explanation (obedience produced by the authority of the experimenter and the setting), whereas Perry et al. looked at a biological factor (oxytocin) and how its effect depended on an individual difference (the participant's level of empathy).
    1 mark for each study's explanation, clearly contrasted.
Q8Comparing core studies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Compare the studies by Dement and Kleitman (sleep and dreams) and Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) in terms of ecological validity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Piliavin et al. has higher ecological validity: it was a field experiment on a real New York subway train and passengers behaved naturally because they did not know they were being studied. Dement and Kleitman has lower ecological validity: participants slept in an unfamiliar laboratory, attached to electrodes, and were woken by a doorbell during the night, which is unlike normal sleep at home.
    1 mark for the comparison (Piliavin higher); 1 mark Piliavin explanation; 1 mark Dement and Kleitman explanation; 1 mark for linked detail (doorbell / electrodes / avoided caffeine and alcohol vs real commuters).

Q12Comparing core studies · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain one similarity and one difference between the study by Bandura et al. (aggression) and the study by Saavedra and Silverman (button phobia). [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Similarity: both studied children and both explain behaviour as learned rather than innate – Bandura showed aggression is imitated from a model; Saavedra and Silverman showed a phobia (and its disgust response) can be learned and changed through conditioning-based therapy. Difference: Bandura et al. used a large sample (72 children) in a controlled laboratory experiment with groups, whereas Saavedra and Silverman was a case study of one 9-year-old boy, so it gave detailed, idiographic data that cannot be generalised in the same way.
    Similarity: identified 1, explained 1, example from Bandura 1, example from Saavedra and Silverman 1. Difference: identified 1, explained 1, examples 1 + 1. Credit: method (experiment vs case study), type of learning (social learning vs classical/evaluative conditioning), data (observation counts vs self-rated distress such as a fear thermometer).
Q13Comparing core studies · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Outline the results of the study by Hassett et al. (monkey toy preferences). [4]
  2. (b) Explain one similarity and one difference between the study by Hassett et al. and the study by Fagen et al. (elephant learning). [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Male rhesus monkeys showed a consistent and strong preference for wheeled (masculine) toys over plush (feminine) toys. Female monkeys showed more varied preferences, interacting with both types of toy, with some preferring plush toys. The pattern was similar to the toy preferences found in human children.
    1 mark per point: males preferred wheeled toys; females' preferences more variable / interacted with both; some females preferred plush; similarity to human children; conclusion credited only if linked to results.
  2. (b) Similarity: both studied non-human animals living in a social/managed setting (rhesus monkeys at a field station; juvenile elephants in Nepal), which raises the question of how far findings generalise to humans. Difference: Hassett et al. supports a nature explanation – the monkeys showed gender-typed toy preferences without human socialisation – whereas Fagen et al. shows nurture: the elephants' new behaviours were trained using positive reinforcement with food rewards and a bridging stimulus.
    Similarity 4 marks (identify, explain, two examples); difference 4 marks (identify, explain, two examples). Accept: observation of free choice (Hassett) vs training procedure (Fagen); both raise animal welfare issues handled carefully.
Q14Comparing core studies · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Compare the studies by Dement and Kleitman (sleep and dreams) and Milgram (obedience) in terms of the type of explanation of behaviour each study supports. Your answer should refer to both studies. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Dement and Kleitman: dream reports were linked to REM sleep, an internal physiological state recorded with EEG and eye movements, so the explanation is biological and located within the person (an individual or nature explanation). Milgram: obedience rose or fell with situational features such as the experimenter's authority, the learner's proximity and the setting, so the explanation is situational. Compare: both show a behaviour that is consistent across people, so both can be used to make general claims, but Dement's explanation is internal while Milgram's is external.
    Describe up to 4 marks: Dement and Kleitman, biological/REM sleep, EEG or eye-movement evidence, dreams reported on waking; Milgram, obedience to authority, situational factors, 26 of 40 (65%) went to 450 volts in the original study. Compare up to 4 marks: difference in explanation type (internal vs external); similarity (both show consistency across participants); evaluation point (e.g. a single explanation may ignore the other side). Level-based marking: 7-8 marks for a balanced comparison that uses both studies throughout.
Q15Comparing core studies · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the usefulness of comparing core studies from different approaches (for example Milgram and Bandura) when deciding which explanation of behaviour is best supported. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: comparing studies shows which findings are consistent across different methods and samples, and highlights where explanations agree or conflict. Limitations: studies differ in method, sample, era and setting, so they may not be measuring the same thing and comparisons can be unfair; a conclusion from comparing two studies cannot establish which explanation is correct.
    Level-based: 5-6 marks for a balanced evaluation with at least one strength and one limitation, each explained and linked to the studies; 3-4 marks for one developed point; 1-2 marks for a basic statement.

Q18Application to everyday life · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Ms Sana teaches Grade 6 pupils in Lahore. Some pupils copy aggressive moves from a video game. Using the study by Bandura et al. (aggression), explain one reason why the pupils might copy the violence. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Observational learning: pupils watch a model (the game character) behaving aggressively and imitate the behaviour. Vicarious reinforcement: the model is rewarded for aggression in the game, so pupils expect reward and are more likely to copy it.
    1 mark: observational learning or imitation of a model; 1 mark: the game character is a model shown behaving aggressively; 1 mark: vicarious reinforcement, as the model is rewarded, so copying is more likely.
Q19Application to everyday life · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks

Q22Application to everyday life · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) The manager of a shopping mall in Karachi is worried that shoppers do not help people who collapse. Using the study by Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans), suggest two ethical ways the manager could increase helping. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1) Lower the 'costs' of helping: clear signs and a help button / trained staff so shoppers know helping is quick and safe. 2) Make the person in need seem deserving and not responsible for their state (e.g. publicity showing that people who collapse are often ill, not drunk), because in Piliavin's study the ill victim with a cane was helped far more often than the drunk victim. The campaign must not deceive or frighten shoppers.
    For each suggestion: 1 mark practical idea, 1 mark link to Piliavin (cost–reward model; ill victim helped more than drunk victim; helping was quicker for the cane victim). Max 2 if not ethical.
Q23Application to everyday life · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) The keepers at a wildlife park in Islamabad need their elephants to keep still while blood samples are taken. Using the study by Fagen et al. (elephant learning), suggest how the keepers could train the elephants. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Use positive reinforcement: reward each small step towards the target behaviour (e.g. presenting an ear or leg) with a preferred food. Pair the food with a bridging stimulus (a sound such as a whistle) so the elephant knows exactly which action earned the reward, and shape the full behaviour gradually over short daily sessions.
    1 mark each: positive reinforcement with food; bridging stimulus / secondary reinforcer; shaping in small steps; link to Fagen (juvenile elephants in Nepal trained for a trunk-wash procedure using positive reinforcement instead of negative methods).

Q26Application to everyday life · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A Pakistani university asks students to try a 10-minute mindfulness exercise before exams. Describe one result from Hölzel et al. (mindfulness and brain scans) that the university could use to justify this, and explain one reason for caution. [4]

  1. (a) Result: after an eight-week mindfulness programme, brain scans showed increased grey matter in areas such as the hippocampus, which is linked to learning and memory. Q27Application to everyday life · Standard · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain one limitation of applying Milgram's findings directly to workplaces in Karachi. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Milgram's study was carried out in one laboratory setting in the USA in the early 1960s with adult men; workplaces today have different rules, cultures and forms of authority, and the findings may not transfer to women or to other settings, so applying them directly may be misleading.
    1 mark for identifying a limitation (sample, era, culture or setting); 1 mark for explaining why it matters for workplaces.
Q28Application to everyday life · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A police officer in Faisalabad plans to ask a seven-year-old girl, who saw a theft, to pick the thief from a line-up. Using the study by Pozzulo et al. (line-ups), explain two pieces of advice you would give the officer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1) Tell the child clearly that the thief may not be in the line-up and that it is fine to say 'not there', because Pozzulo et al. found children were more likely than adults to choose someone when the culprit was absent (more false identifications). 2) Treat a child's identification with caution and look for other evidence, because children's identification accuracy in target-absent line-ups was poorer than adults'. The child should be supported and not pressured.
    Each advice: 1 mark for the advice, 1 mark for link to Pozzulo's finding that children made more errors than adults in target-absent line-ups.
Q29Application to everyday life · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A hospital in Quetta plans to move its nurses to night shifts only, to cover staff shortages. Explain two ways that research into sleep stages (for example Dement and Kleitman) could help the hospital plan the rota fairly and safely. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two developed points, e.g.: sleep research shows that sleep occurs in cycles that include REM sleep, so disrupted daytime sleep may reduce the quality of rest; nurses on night shifts may need protected rest periods and time to recover before driving home; staff should not be scheduled for long unbroken night runs without breaks; the hospital could check that workers are getting enough sleep, as REM sleep is needed for memory and emotional processing.
    2 marks per developed point: 1 mark for the sleep-research idea, 1 mark for applying it to the rota. Accept any well-explained link.
Q30Application to everyday life · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A bus company in Rawalpindi wants passengers to stop ignoring a passenger who has collapsed. Using the study by Piliavin et al., explain two factors that might make bystanders on the bus more likely to help, and one reason why the company's plan might still fail. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two factors, e.g.: a victim who appears genuinely ill is more likely to be helped than one who seems drunk, so a clear, visible need increases helping; helping is more likely when the cost to the bystander is low, such as a simple action like calling for the driver (Piliavin's cost-reward idea). Reason the plan may fail: many passengers may assume someone else will act (diffusion of responsibility), or may fear helping a victim who seems drunk, so the company's message may not change behaviour.
    2 marks for each factor (1 to name it, 1 to link it to Piliavin's findings or the cost-reward idea); 1 mark for a reason the plan might fail, linked to bystander behaviour (diffusion of responsibility, or fear about helping a victim who seems drunk). Max 5.

Q32Ethics · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the study by Andrade (doodling) involved deception. [2]

  1. (a) Any two of: ask the child to explain the study in their own words; use simple, child-friendly language or pictures to describe the tasks; ask a few short questions to check understanding; invite questions and repeat the explanation before starting.
    1 mark for each valid check, e.g. child explains the study back, child-friendly explanation or pictures, short comprehension questions, invitation to ask questions.
Q80Using children in research · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one strength and one weakness of using children as participants in the study by Bandura et al. (aggression). [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strength: young children (aged about 3–5) have had less exposure to social norms, so researchers could see whether aggression is learned by observation without much prior learning getting in the way. Weakness: children cannot give fully informed consent and are more vulnerable to harm, e.g. being frustrated and possibly learning aggressive acts.
    2 marks each: point + link to Bandura.
Q81Using children in research · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A psychologist in Hyderabad wants to observe 8-year-old children in a school playground and count cooperative behaviour between them. Explain one ethical issue with this study and one way to address it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Issue: the children are being observed without knowing it, so they cannot give informed consent. Way to address it: obtain consent from the school and parents and tell the children about the observation in age-appropriate language; allow children who do not wish to take part to be excluded from the count; or use a public-observation approach with the school's approval and no identifying data.
    2 marks for the issue (identified and explained with reference to consent or privacy); 2 marks for a suitable way to address it (named and explained).

  1. (a) Explain one reason why an adult model's aggressive behaviour could place children at greater risk of harm than it would place adults. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children are less able to understand that the aggression is pretend and may be more frightened or more likely to imitate it, so the risk of distress or of learning harmful behaviour is greater.
    1 mark: children may not understand that the behaviour is pretend; 1 mark: children are more easily frightened or influenced; 1 mark: this increases the risk of distress or of learned harm.

  1. (a) A school study in Multan uses 46 pupils aged 8 to 10. 19 of the pupils are girls. Calculate the percentage of girls. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [3]

  1. (a) 19 ÷ 46 × 100 = 41.3%.
    1 mark for dividing 19 by 46; 1 mark for multiplying by 100; 1 mark for 41.3% (1 d.p.).
Q84Using children in research · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the right to withdraw may be difficult for a child to use in a school study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children may not know they have the right to leave, and may feel they must follow a teacher or parent who is present; they may also be unable to judge the consequences of stopping, so they may stay in the study even if they are unhappy.
    1 mark: child may not know the right exists; 1 mark: pressure from adults or the school setting; 1 mark: child cannot judge consequences or feels obliged to continue.
Q85Using children in research · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a psychologist in Karachi tests 8-year-old pupils on a memory task during lesson time. The head teacher agrees to the study, but the parents are not asked. Explain one ethical problem with this procedure. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way the researcher could address this problem. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Parents have not given consent, and the children cannot give fully informed consent alone. The school's agreement may not represent the children's own interests, and the study takes time away from lessons without parental knowledge.
    1 mark: parents (who are legally responsible) were not asked for consent; 1 mark: the head teacher cannot replace the children's or parents' informed consent, or the study disrupts school time without parents knowing.
  2. (b) Ask each child's parents or guardians for written consent before the study, explain the task in simple language to the parents and the child, and let the child stop at any time.
    1 mark for a sensible step (asking parents for written consent); 1 mark for a supporting detail (explaining the study in child-friendly language, or giving the child a right to withdraw).
Q86Using children in research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the study by Pozzulo et al. (line-ups) needed to use children as participants, and describe one ethical issue this raised. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The aim was to compare children's and adults' eyewitness identification, so child participants were essential – children do act as real witnesses and their accuracy must be understood. Ethical issue: consent – children cannot give fully informed consent, so parental consent is needed as well as the child's agreement, and the procedure (seeing a staged event and a line-up) must not frighten them; they must be able to stop at any time.
    2 marks reason linked to aim; 2 marks ethical issue explained with link to study.
Q87Using children in research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe two ways that a study using children to investigate aggression, such as the Bobo doll study by Bandura et al., could be made more ethical. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two developed ways, e.g.: use a more gentle model of aggression that is less likely to cause distress; check that children are comfortable before and after the procedure; obtain consent from parents and assent from the children; allow children to stop at any time; provide a full debriefing with a calm activity after the study; limit the number of trials or the amount of exposure to aggression.
    3 marks per way: 1 mark to name it, 1 mark to describe how it is done, 1 mark to explain how it protects the child. Level-based: 5-6 marks where two ways are fully developed.
Q88Using children in research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Some psychologists argue that findings from research with children cannot be generalised to adults, so child research has little value. Discuss this view, using at least one study with children (for example Bandura et al.). [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Against the view: many psychological processes such as imitation and observational learning develop early and continue in adults, so findings from children can show basic principles that apply later; studying children can reveal how behaviour develops. For the view: children's cognitive and emotional development differs from adults', so results may not transfer; the Bandura sample was small and from one nursery school, which further limits generalisation. Balanced conclusion: child research can be valuable for developmental questions but results should be checked with adult samples before being applied to adults.
    Level-based: 7-8 marks for a balanced discussion, using a named study, with both sides developed and a reasoned conclusion; 4-6 marks for one side well developed; 1-3 marks for description only.
Q89Using children in research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one methodological reason why researchers study young children rather than adults when investigating early language development. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Young children are at the stage where language is developing, so studying them shows how language is acquired; adults already have full language, so their results would not reveal how it develops. Researchers can compare children at different ages to see the sequence of development.
    2 marks: reason based on developmental stage (adults have already acquired language); 2 marks: explanation of what the child study shows (sequence, change with age, or development over time).
Q90Using children in research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain why researchers may choose to use children as participants when studying the effects of violent media on aggression. [3]
  2. (b) Evaluate one ethical issue of using children in this type of study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children are still forming their behaviour, so the effect of media on learning aggression can be studied in the developing stage; findings may show how aggression is acquired early in life, which adults who already have set habits would not show.
    1 mark: children are at a developmental stage where behaviour is still being learned; 1 mark: studies of children show how aggression is acquired; 1 mark: adults already have established behaviour, so effects are harder to observe.
  2. (b) Children may be exposed to distressing or harmful content, and they cannot fully understand the risks, so protection from harm is harder to guarantee. Even a short exposure could make a child more anxious afterwards, so a careful debrief is essential.
    1 mark: names an issue (harm or distress from violent content); 1 mark: explains why children are more vulnerable (cannot fully judge risk); 1 mark: evaluates, e.g. debriefing and parental consent may reduce but not remove the risk.
Q91Nature versus nurture · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the study by Hassett et al. (monkey toy preferences) supports the nature side of the nature versus nurture debate. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the study by Bandura et al. (aggression) supports the nurture side. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Male rhesus monkeys preferred wheeled toys, like human boys, even though the monkeys had not been taught gender roles by human society; this suggests a biological (possibly hormonal) basis for gender-typed preferences.
    1 mark result; 1 mark link to nature.
  2. (b) Children who watched an aggressive model copied specific aggressive acts they had not shown before, so aggression was learned from the environment through observation.
    1 mark result; 1 mark link to nurture.
Q92Nature versus nurture · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by the "nature" side of the nature versus nurture debate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Nature is the view that behaviour is largely inherited or biologically determined, through genes and the physical structure of the brain.
    1 mark: inherited or genetic influence; 1 mark: biological or innate determinism (not learned from experience). Accept the equivalent for nurture: behaviour learned from the environment.
Q93Nature versus nurture · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Identify one biological factor and one environmental factor that can influence human behaviour. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Biological: genes (or hormones or brain structure). Environmental: upbringing, family or school (or peers, culture).
    1 mark for a biological factor; 1 mark for an environmental factor. Accept any sensible example of each.
Q94Nature versus nurture · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe one way in which the study by Bandura et al. (aggression) supports the nurture side of the debate. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children who watched an adult model act aggressively towards a Bobo doll copied the aggressive behaviour, including actions they had not seen before. This suggests aggression is learned through observation rather than being innate.
    1 mark: children imitated the adult model's aggression; 1 mark: imitation included novel behaviour, so it was not just a pre-existing response; 1 mark: conclusion that aggression was learned from the environment (social learning).
Q95Nature versus nurture · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a heritability estimate of 0.5 for a trait. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) About half of the differences between people in that trait, within the studied population, are due to genetic differences; the other half is due to environmental differences.
    1 mark: genetic differences account for about half of the variation; 1 mark: the remaining variation is due to environmental influences. Do not accept "half of a person's trait is inherited".
Q96Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain one way in which the study by Dement and Kleitman (sleep and dreams) relates to the nature versus nurture debate. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) It supports nature: all participants showed the same regular cycles of REM and NREM sleep through the night, and dream recall was linked to REM sleep, suggesting that sleep stages and dreaming are biologically determined. However, dream content (what the person dreams about) is likely to be influenced by experience, which is nurture.
    1 mark position; 1 mark evidence; 1 mark further explanation or counterpoint.
Q97Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: 50 adopted children in Lahore are compared with their biological parents and their adoptive parents on a measure of reading ability. Explain how this design helps separate the influence of nature from nurture. [4]
  2. (b) State one ethical or practical limitation of this design. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children share genes with their biological parents but not their adoptive parents, while they share the home environment with the adoptive parents. If the children's scores are more like the biological parents, nature is more influential; if more like the adoptive parents, nurture is more influential.
    1 mark: biological parents share genes with the children; 1 mark: adoptive parents share the home environment; 1 mark: comparison of similarity with each group; 1 mark: conclusion about which influence is stronger.
  2. (b) Adoption agencies may not give access to records, or the adoptive home may differ from the biological family in ways other than the environment; families may also be hard to recruit.
    1 mark for any sensible limitation, e.g. difficulty recruiting families, adoption placements are not random, or an ethical issue of contacting families about their adoption history.
Q98Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the interactionist approach can be seen as a compromise between the nature and nurture positions. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Interactionism accepts that genes influence behaviour (nature) and that experience shapes how those genetic tendencies develop (nurture). It does not treat either as the only cause, so it accepts both kinds of influence working together.
    1 mark: accepts biological influence; 1 mark: accepts environmental influence; 1 mark: says the two work together or interact, neither acting alone.
Q99Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain one limitation of using the study by Dement and Kleitman (sleep and dreams) as evidence for the nature side of the nature versus nurture debate. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The study mainly links REM sleep to dream reports; it does not show that the content of dreams is fixed by biology, because dream content is also shaped by a person's experiences, so the evidence supports only part of the nature view.
    1 mark: REM sleep is linked to dream reports, but this does not show what the dreams contain; 1 mark: dream content is shaped by experience (nurture); 1 mark: conclusion that the study supports nature only partly.
Q100Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why it is difficult to separate nature and nurture when studying language development in children. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Children inherit the biological capacity to learn language, but they only learn a particular language through the speech they hear. Parents also pass on their genes and provide the environment, so the two influences are correlated and hard to separate.
    1 mark: language needs both an inherited capacity and exposure to speech; 1 mark: genes and environment are correlated (parents provide both); 1 mark: experiments that separate them would be unethical in humans.
Q101Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher in Peshawar measures the reading time at home and the language score of 200 children at age 6. She finds a positive correlation. Explain why this correlation cannot show whether nature or nurture caused the difference in language scores. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A correlation shows only an association. Children with more reading time may have parents with stronger verbal ability, who also passed on genes that affect language. So the correlation may reflect shared genes as well as the home environment.
    1 mark: correlation shows association, not cause; 1 mark: a third factor (parents' genes or ability) may explain both; 1 mark: the design does not separate genetic from environmental influence.
Q102Nature versus nurture · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Hypothetical study: a researcher in Hyderabad compares the personality scores of 60 pairs of identical twins raised together with 60 pairs of non-identical twins raised together. Identical twins share almost all their genes, non-identical twins share about half. Explain how a greater similarity in identical twins would support the nature side of the debate, and state one limitation of this design. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) If identical twins are more similar on personality than non-identical twins, the extra shared genes suggest a genetic influence (nature). A limitation is that identical twins may also be treated more alike by parents and others, so the shared environment may explain some of the similarity rather than genes alone.
    1 mark: identical twins share more genes, so greater similarity suggests genetic influence; 1 mark: comparison with non-identical twins is the key control; 1 mark: limitation that identical twins experience more similar environments; 1 mark: clear conclusion about nature.
Q103Nature versus nurture · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Hölzel et al. (mindfulness and brain scans) found changes in brain structure after an eight-week mindfulness course. Explain how this study can be used as evidence for the nurture side of the debate, and identify one limitation of the study. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why a researcher might argue that the change could also reflect nature. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Brain structure changed after experience (mindfulness training), so the brain is shaped by practice, which supports nurture. A limitation is that the sample was small, so the results may not generalise and some of the change could be due to chance or to individual differences.
    1 mark: brain changed after training (experience shaped structure); 1 mark: link to nurture or plasticity; 1 mark: limitation identified (small sample); 1 mark: limitation explained (results may not generalise or may reflect chance).
  2. (b) People who are naturally more able to focus or regulate attention may be more likely to take up and stick with mindfulness, so some of the difference may reflect inherited traits rather than the training alone.
    1 mark: individual differences in genetic traits may make some people more likely to benefit; 1 mark: this suggests the training effect and the trait interact.
Q104Nature versus nurture · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Discuss the interactionist view of behaviour, using one named study to support your answer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Interactionism says behaviour results from the combination of inherited tendencies and environmental experiences, which cannot be separated. A named study can illustrate this, for example the Bandura et al. (aggression) study: children may have different tendencies to be aggressive (nature), but whether they show aggression depends on the model they observe and the situation (nurture), so both interact. Evaluation: the approach accepts complexity, but it is harder to test and to predict individual behaviour than either pure nature or pure nurture view.
    Up to 6 marks. AO1: definition of interactionism (up to 2 marks). AO1: relevant named study described accurately (up to 2 marks). AO2/AO3: explains how the study shows both influences interact, or evaluates the view (up to 2 marks). Credit reasonable alternative studies that are correctly described.
Q105Nature versus nurture · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Two students argue. Student A says: "Behaviour is entirely learned." Student B says: "Behaviour is entirely innate." Evaluate both claims, using evidence from one study. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both claims are too extreme. Evidence from a study such as Bandura et al. supports learning through observation, but it does not show that every behaviour is learned, since children may have biological tendencies that affect how they respond to a model. Evidence from biological studies such as Dement and Kleitman shows innate processes, but experience also affects behaviour. The best conclusion is that both nature and nurture contribute.
    Up to 5 marks. 1 mark each for: a reasoned rejection of the "entirely" claim; use of an accurately described named study; a point supporting the learned claim; a point supporting the innate claim; a justified overall conclusion (interactionist or balanced).
