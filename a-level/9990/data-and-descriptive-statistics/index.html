Q1Graphs and tables · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical study in a Lahore college tested whether background music affects revision. Students learned a list of 30 words either with pop music playing or in silence and then recalled as many words as possible.Condition | Mean number of words recalledMusic | 14.2Silence | 17.6

  1. (a) Name the type of graph that should be used to display the results in the table. Give one reason for your choice. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest a suitable title, a label for the x-axis and a label for the y-axis for this graph. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the difference between the two means. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A bar chart, because the IV (music or silence) is made of separate categories, and the bars show the mean for each condition.
    1 mark: bar chart; 1 mark: reason – discrete/categorical conditions (bars do not touch) / comparing two means.
  2. (b) Title: 'Mean number of words recalled when revising with music and in silence'. x-axis: 'Condition (music or silence)'. y-axis: 'Mean number of words recalled (out of 30)'.
    1 mark each: title mentioning both variables; x-axis = conditions of the IV; y-axis = mean words recalled (must say 'mean').
  3. (c) 3.4 words
    17.6 − 14.2 = 3.4 words (B1).
Q2Graphs and tables · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical time-sampling observation of children's play at break time in an Islamabad primary school recorded the following behaviour categories.Behaviour | FrequencyRunning | 18Chasing | 12Sitting and talking | 24Ball games | 30Playing alone | 6Give percentages correct to 3 significant figures.

  1. (a) Calculate the total number of observations recorded. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of observations that were ball games. [1]
  3. (c) Describe how these data should be displayed as a graph. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 90
    18 + 12 + 24 + 30 + 6 = 90 (B1).
  2. (b) 33.3 %
    30 ÷ 90 × 100 = 33.3 % (to 3 s.f.).
  3. (c) A bar chart with the five behaviour categories along the x-axis, with gaps between bars, and the frequency (number of observations) on the y-axis starting from zero; with a title such as 'Frequency of play behaviours observed during break time'.
    1 mark: bar chart; 1 mark: categories on x-axis with separated bars; 1 mark: frequency on labelled y-axis from 0 / suitable title.
Q3Graphs and tables · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

In a hypothetical experiment, each participant chose one of two brands of juice, X or Y. In the 'with friends' condition, the participant shopped with two friends who (as secretly instructed) chose brand X first. In the 'alone' condition, the participant shopped alone but saw a sign saying that most shoppers chose brand X.Condition | Chose brand X | Chose brand YWith friends | 22 | 8Alone | 12 | 18Give percentages correct to 3 significant figures.

  1. (a) State how many participants took part in the study. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of participants in the 'with friends' condition who chose the same brand as their friends. [1]
  3. (c) Using the data, state one conclusion from this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 60
    B1: 22 + 8 + 12 + 18 = 60.
  2. (b) 73.3 %
    22 ÷ 30 × 100 = 73.3 % (3 s.f.).
  3. (c) People are more likely to choose the same brand as others when they shop with friends who choose it (73.3 %) than when they shop alone and only see others' choices (40.0 %), suggesting friends exert stronger social influence on choices.
    1 mark: conclusion about social influence/conformity; 1 mark: supported with comparative data (73.3 % vs 40 %).
Q4Graphs and tables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A hypothetical correlational study recorded the average daily screen time and average nightly sleep of eight teenagers in Islamabad.Teenager | Screen time (hours) | Sleep (hours)A | 1 | 8.5B | 2 | 8.0C | 3 | 7.6D | 4 | 7.4E | 5 | 6.8F | 6 | 6.9G | 7 | 6.1H | 8 | 5.5

  1. (a) Name the type of graph that should be used to display these data. [1]
  2. (b) Describe two features your graph should have. [2]
  3. (c) State the type of correlation shown by the data and explain what it means here. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why these data do not show that screen time causes less sleep. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A scatter graph (scattergram).
    1 mark: scatter graph.
  2. (b) Daily screen time (hours) on one axis and sleep duration (hours) on the other, both labelled with units; each teenager plotted as a single point (no lines joining the points); a title stating both co-variables.
    1 mark each for any two: both axes labelled with co-variables and units; points plotted not joined; suitable title; sensible scales starting from a stated value.
  3. (c) A (strong) negative correlation: the more hours of screen time a teenager has, the fewer hours they sleep.
    1 mark: negative (strong); 1 mark: interpretation in context.
  4. (d) A correlation only shows that two variables change together. A third variable, such as exam stress or a late bedtime routine, might increase screen use and reduce sleep, or poor sleep could lead to more screen use.
    1 mark: correlation ≠ causation / no manipulation of variables; 1 mark: example of a third variable or reverse direction.

  1. (a) Name a type of graph that could show the change in anxiety ratings across the five weeks. [1]

Q8Graphs and tables · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Two observers watched the same 10-minute video of children in a playground and tallied aggressive behaviours (hypothetical data).Category | Observer 1 | Observer 2Hitting | 5 | 5Grabbing toys | 8 | 7Pushing | 3 | 3Verbal threat | 6 | 6Excluding others | 4 | 5

  1. (a) For how many of the categories did the two observers record exactly the same tally? [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total number of behaviours recorded by each observer. [2]
  3. (c) Explain what these data suggest about the inter-rater reliability of the observation, and suggest how it could be improved. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3
    Same tally for hitting (5), pushing (3) and verbal threat (6): 3 categories.

Q9Graphs and tables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In a hypothetical field experiment in a Karachi shopping mall, a confederate dropped a pile of papers near 80 passers-by. For 50 passers-by the confederate was smartly dressed and for 30 scruffily dressed. 35 people helped the smartly dressed confederate and 12 helped the scruffily dressed confederate.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of people who helped in each condition. [2]
  2. (b) Draw a results table to summarise these findings. Your table should show the number and percentage of people who helped and did not help in each condition (describe the rows and columns in words). [4]
  3. (c) Explain why percentages are more useful than raw numbers for comparing the two conditions in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Smartly dressed: 70 %; scruffily dressed: 40 %
    M1: 35 ÷ 50 × 100 = 70 %; A1: 12 ÷ 30 × 100 = 40 %.
  2. (b) Rows: smartly dressed; scruffily dressed. Columns: number who helped | number who did not help | % who helped. Smart: 35 | 15 | 70 %. Scruffy: 12 | 18 | 40 %. Title: 'Number and percentage of passers-by who helped a person who dropped papers, by how the person was dressed'.
    1 mark: rows/columns correctly organised by condition; 1 mark: helped and not-helped numbers correct (35/15, 12/18); 1 mark: percentages correct; 1 mark: informative title / headings with units.
  3. (c) The conditions had different numbers of passers-by (50 and 30), so raw numbers of helpers cannot be compared directly; percentages put both on the same base (out of 100), so the comparison is fair.
    1 mark: common base / standardised; 1 mark: allows fair comparison between unequal groups.
Q10Graphs and tables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Sana drew a bar chart of the mean test scores (out of 100) of students who revised on paper (mean 52) or on a tablet (mean 56). Her y-axis begins at 40 and has no label. The two bars touch each other and the graph has no title.

  1. (a) Identify three problems with Sana's graph. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how the problem with the y-axis could mislead a reader. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1) The y-axis starts at 40, not 0, so the difference between the two conditions looks far larger than it is. 2) The y-axis has no label, so it is not clear that it shows mean scores out of 100. 3) The bars touch, which wrongly suggests continuous data, and there is no title.
    1 mark each, up to 3: truncated y-axis exaggerates the difference; missing y-axis label/units; bars touching for categorical data; no title; x-axis categories not named.
  2. (b) The means of 52 and 56 differ by only 4 marks, but starting the axis at 40 makes one bar look about 1.3 times taller in relation to the axis (12 vs 16 units above 40), so a reader may think the effect of the IV is large.
    1 mark: exaggerates a small difference; 1 mark: use of the data (only 4 points apart / bar heights from 40 are 12 and 16).
Q11Graphs and tables · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A hypothetical study of 20 university students in Kuala Lumpur plotted hours of sport played per week (x-axis, 0–14) against a self-rated mood score (y-axis, 1–10). Most points rise from the bottom left to the top right of the graph. One student who played 12 hours of sport per week had a mood score of 3.

  1. (a) Describe the relationship between the two variables shown by the scatter graph. [2]
  2. (b) One student (12 hours of sport, mood 3) does not fit the pattern. Suggest two reasons why a researcher might find a point like this. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why the researcher should not simply remove this point from the data. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) There is a positive correlation: students who reported more hours of sport per week generally reported higher mood scores, though the points are moderately spread, so it is not perfect.
    1 mark: positive; 1 mark: interpretation in context, with strength (moderate/not perfect).
  2. (b) The student may have had a personal problem (e.g. a family illness) that lowered mood that week; or they may have misread the rating scale or recorded their answer wrongly.
    1 mark each for two plausible reasons: individual circumstances / other variables; recording or self-report error; misunderstanding the scale; social desirability.
  3. (c) Removing data that do not fit the hypothesis is researcher bias; it would make the correlation look stronger than it really is and reduce the validity of the conclusion.
    1 mark: bias / dishonest reporting; 1 mark: the correlation would be misrepresented.

Q14Quantitative and qualitative data · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Students in Hyderabad were asked an open question: 'Describe how you felt before your last exam.' Seven answers were:1 I was too nervous to eat2 My mind went blank3 I prayed and felt calmer4 My hands were shaking5 I could not sleep the night before6 I talked to my friends and felt better7 My heart was racing

  1. (a) Sort the seven answers into two categories: 'physical symptoms' and 'coping strategies' (any answer that is neither should be identified). [3]
  2. (b) Explain how sorting answers into categories changes the type of data collected. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Physical symptoms: too nervous to eat; hands shaking; could not sleep; heart racing (4). Coping strategies: prayed and felt calmer; talked to friends (2). Neither (cognitive symptom): my mind went blank (1).
    1 mark: physical symptoms correct (4 answers; accept 'could not sleep' or 'too nervous to eat' in either a physical or 'other' category); 1 mark: coping strategies correct (2); 1 mark: 'mind went blank' identified as a cognitive symptom/neither.
  2. (b) Counting how many answers fall into each category turns qualitative data (words) into quantitative data (frequencies), which can be compared and graphed but loses some detail.
    1 mark: converts qualitative to quantitative (frequencies); 1 mark: consequence – easier comparison/analysis but loss of depth/meaning.
Q15Quantitative and qualitative data · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical study interviewed 30 elderly people living in Faisalabad about their experiences of loneliness, recording their answers in full.

  1. (a) Explain one strength of collecting qualitative data in this study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of collecting qualitative data in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Unstructured answers let the elderly participants explain in their own words why they feel lonely (e.g. children moving abroad), giving detailed insight that fixed questions might miss, which increases validity.
    1 mark: depth / detail / participant's own words; 1 mark: linked to loneliness context.
  2. (b) It is difficult to compare participants or summarise 30 long interviews objectively; the researcher's interpretation of what counts as loneliness may be biased.
    1 mark: hard to analyse/compare or subjective interpretation; 1 mark: linked to the study.
Q16Quantitative and qualitative data · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Q17Quantitative and qualitative data · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Employees at a bank in Lahore rated 'How satisfied are you with your job?' on a scale from 1 to 5.

  1. (a) Explain why the data from the 1–5 rating scale are quantitative. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the data are still subjective. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one way of making the scale less open to interpretation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each answer is a number that can be added up or averaged.
    1 mark: numerical scores.
  2. (b) Each employee judges what '4' means for themselves; two people with similar satisfaction might choose different numbers, so the score depends on personal interpretation.
    1 mark: based on personal opinion/interpretation; 1 mark: example of different interpretations of the scale points.
  3. (c) Label every point with a description (e.g. 1 = very dissatisfied, 3 = neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied).
    1 mark: anchor/label every point, or give examples for each point.
Q18Quantitative and qualitative data · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by quantitative data and qualitative data, using any examples. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Quantitative data are numerical: e.g. the number of aggressive acts counted in an observation, reaction times in ms, or scores on a rating scale. They are easy to summarise with means and graphs and to compare objectively, but can lack depth. Qualitative data are descriptive: e.g. interview transcripts about how a patient experiences anxiety, or diary entries. They give rich insight into reasons and meanings but are harder to analyse and may be interpreted subjectively.
    Up to 3 marks for each type: 1 for definition, 1 for a relevant example, 1 for elaboration (feature, strength or weakness). Examples may come from any study (e.g. Milgram's voltage = quantitative, participants' comments = qualitative).
Q19Quantitative and qualitative data · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A hypothetical study asked patients recovering from knee surgery in a Toronto hospital to rate their pain each evening from 0 to 10 and also to write a short diary entry about their day.

  1. (a) Explain why collecting both quantitative and qualitative data could improve the validity of this study. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one problem the researcher may have when analysing the diaries. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The rating scale gives comparable numbers showing whether pain changed over the week, while the diary explains why (e.g. pain worse after physiotherapy). If both show the same pattern, the conclusions are supported by two kinds of evidence (triangulation); the diary can also reveal if numbers were misunderstood.
    1 mark: quantitative allows comparison/objectivity; 1 mark: qualitative gives reasons/depth; 1 mark: combining checks one against the other (triangulation) to increase validity.
  2. (b) Diaries will vary greatly in length and detail, and deciding which themes appear requires interpretation, so two researchers might code them differently (low inter-rater reliability).
    1 mark: problem (time-consuming, subjective interpretation, differing detail); 1 mark: consequence for the study.
Q20Measures of central tendency · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Seven participants in a hypothetical memory study recalled the following number of items: 4, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean, median and mode of the scores. Give the mean to 2 decimal places. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one strength of the mean as a measure of central tendency. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mean = 8.14; median = 8; mode = 7
    B1 mean: 57 ÷ 7 = 8.14; B1 median = 8 (4th of 7 ordered values); B1 mode = 7.
  2. (b) It uses every score in the data set, so it is the most sensitive measure and represents all participants' performance.
    1 mark: uses all scores; 1 mark: elaboration (most sensitive/representative when there are no extreme scores).
Q21Measures of central tendency · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

In a hypothetical independent-measures study in a Lahore school, Group 1 learned a word list and Group 2 learned pictures of the same items. Correct answers:Group 1: 6, 8, 5, 9, 7Group 2: 10, 9, 12, 11, 8

  1. (a) Calculate the mean number of correct answers for each group. [2]
  2. (b) State what the means suggest about the hypothesis that pictures are remembered better than words. [1]

  1. (a) Group 1 (word lists): 7; Group 2 (pictures): 10
    B1 each: 35 ÷ 5 = 7; 50 ÷ 5 = 10.
  2. (b) The picture group had a higher mean (10 vs 7), which supports the hypothesis.

Q22Measures of central tendency · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

In a hypothetical study, seven shoppers in a Karachi supermarket were timed (in minutes) from entering the store to reaching the checkout: 15, 18, 17, 16, 19, 20, 95.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean and the median for these data. Give the mean to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain which measure gives the better summary of these data. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mean = 28.6 minutes; median = 18 minutes
    B1: mean = 200 ÷ 7 = 28.6; B1: median = 18 (ordered: 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 95).
  2. (b) The median, because the extreme value of 95 minutes distorts the mean, making it higher than six of the seven scores; the median is not affected by this outlier.
    1 mark: median; 1 mark: reason linked to the outlier of 95 inflating the mean.
Q23Measures of central tendency · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Eight customers rated a new tea brand on a scale from 1 (strongly dislike) to 7 (strongly like): 4, 6, 3, 4, 2, 5, 4, 3.

  1. (a) Calculate the median rating. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the median is more appropriate than the mean for these rating-scale data. [2]
  3. (c) Name the only appropriate measure of central tendency for the question 'Which is your favourite brand of tea?' and explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4
    M1: ordered 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 6 – median is the mean of the 4th and 5th values; A1: (4 + 4) ÷ 2 = 4.
  2. (b) Rating-scale data are ordinal: the gap between 'agree' and 'strongly agree' is not necessarily the same as between other points, so averaging the numbers is not meaningful; the median only uses rank order.
    1 mark: data are ordinal / intervals not equal; 1 mark: median uses order only, mean assumes equal intervals.
  3. (c) The mode, because the answers are categories (nominal data) that cannot be ordered or averaged.
    1 mark: mode; 1 mark: nominal/categorical data.
Q24Measures of central tendency · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) The mean of eight participants' scores is 12.5. Seven of the scores are 10, 14, 12, 11, 15, 13 and 9. Calculate the eighth score. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the median of all eight scores. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the mean and median are equal here and what this suggests about the distribution of the scores. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 16
    M1: total = 12.5 × 8 = 100; known total = 84; A1: 100 − 84 = 16.
  2. (b) 12.5
    Ordered: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 → (12 + 13) ÷ 2 = 12.5.
  3. (c) The scores are spread evenly (one at each value from 9 to 16), with no extreme values, so the distribution is symmetrical; in a symmetrical distribution the mean and median are equal.
    1 mark: evenly spread / no outliers; 1 mark: symmetrical distribution.
Q25Normal distribution · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe three features of a normal distribution. [3]
  2. (b) State what should be shown on each axis when sketching a normal distribution of reaction times. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is bell-shaped and symmetrical about the centre; the mean, median and mode are all equal and at the highest point; the tails extend in both directions and get closer to the x-axis without touching it; most scores lie near the middle.
    1 mark each, up to 3: bell-shaped; symmetrical; mean = median = mode at the peak; tails approach but never touch x-axis; about 68 % of scores within one standard deviation of the mean.
  2. (b) x-axis: reaction time (ms), the measured variable/score; y-axis: frequency (number of participants).
    1 mark: x-axis = score/variable measured with units; 1 mark: y-axis = frequency.
Q26Normal distribution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A hypothetical concentration test was given to a large group of Pakistani secondary-school students. The scores were normally distributed with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 8.

  1. (a) Between which two scores would you expect about 68 % of the students to fall? [2]
  2. (b) Approximately what percentage of students would you expect to score above 66? [1]
  3. (c) Out of 400 students, approximately how many would you expect to score between 42 and 58? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 42 and 58
    M1: one standard deviation either side of the mean (50 ± 8); A1: 42 and 58.
  2. (b) About 2.5 % (accept 2–2.5 %)
    66 is two standard deviations above the mean; about 95 % lie within ±2 SD, so about 2.5 % lie above (exact 2.3 %).
  3. (c) About 272 students
    68 % of 400 = 272.
Q27Normal distribution · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a hypothetical proof-reading task in Singapore, the numbers of errors missed by 50 participants had a mean of 14, a median of 11 and a mode of 10.

  1. (a) State whether this distribution is normal, positively skewed or negatively skewed. Explain your answer using the three measures. [3]
  2. (b) Describe what the graph of this distribution would look like. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why the distribution of errors might not be normal in this study. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Positively skewed. The mean (14) is greater than the median (11), which is greater than the mode (10); a few participants made many errors, pulling the mean towards the long tail on the right.
    1 mark: positively skewed; 1 mark: mean > median > mode; 1 mark: explanation – a few high scores create a long tail to the right/pull the mean up.
  2. (b) The peak (highest frequency) is towards the left (at low numbers of errors) and there is a long tail stretching out to the right towards high numbers of errors.
    1 mark: peak to the left; 1 mark: long tail to the right.
  3. (c) The task may have been easy for most people, so most made few errors (a floor effect), while a few tired or distracted participants made many.
    1 mark: plausible reason (floor effect, task too easy, a few outliers, small sample).
Q28Measures of spread · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Two groups of trainee drivers in Islamabad completed a hazard-perception test (hypothetical scores).Group A: 22, 25, 24, 23, 26Group B: 10, 35, 18, 30, 27

  1. (a) Calculate the range for each group. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean for each group. [1]
  3. (c) Using your answers, explain what the results show about the two groups. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Group A: 4; Group B: 25
    B1 each: 26 − 22 = 4; 35 − 10 = 25.
  2. (b) Both groups have a mean of 24
    120 ÷ 5 = 24 for each group.
  3. (c) Although both groups have the same mean, Group B's scores are much more spread out (range 25 vs 4), so performance in Group B varied a great deal between individuals while Group A performed consistently.
    1 mark: same mean; 1 mark: Group B much more variable/less consistent with data.
Q29Measures of spread · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

In a hypothetical study in Melbourne, workers who did a mindfulness course had a mean stress score of 32 (standard deviation 2.1). A control group also had a mean of 32 (standard deviation 7.8).

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by 'standard deviation'. [2]
  2. (b) Compare the two groups using the means and standard deviations. [2]
  3. (c) Give one advantage of the standard deviation compared with the range. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A measure of spread showing the average amount by which scores differ from the mean; a larger standard deviation means scores are more spread out.
    1 mark: spread/variation around the mean; 1 mark: larger SD = more spread (or calculation idea: based on every score's distance from the mean).
  2. (b) Both groups have the same mean stress score (32), but the mindfulness group has a much smaller standard deviation (2.1 vs 7.8), so their scores were closer together and more consistent; the control group varied more.
    1 mark: same mean; 1 mark: mindfulness group less spread/more consistent, quoting SDs.
  3. (c) It uses every score, so it is not distorted by a single extreme score as the range is.
    1 mark: uses all data / less affected by one outlier.
Q30Measures of spread · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Five participants in a hypothetical study in Cairo rated their sleep quality: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean of the scores. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the standard deviation of the scores, using the sample formula s = √(Σ(x − mean)² ÷ (n − 1)). Give your answer to 2 decimal places. [3]
  3. (c) A second group had the same mean but a standard deviation of 0.71. Explain what this tells you. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8
    40 ÷ 5 = 8.
  2. (b) 3.16
    M1: deviations −4, −2, 0, 2, 4; squares 16, 4, 0, 4, 16, total 40; M1: 40 ÷ 4 = 10; A1: √10 = 3.16.
  3. (c) The second group's scores were much closer to the mean of 8, so participants in that group were far more similar to each other than in the first group.
    1 mark: less spread / more consistent; 1 mark: comparison with 3.16.
