Q1Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Identify the research method used by Piliavin et al. in their subway Samaritan study. [1]
  2. (b) Outline two ways in which the victim's condition was varied in this study. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A field experiment.
    B1: field experiment (accept 'experiment carried out in a natural setting'). Not 'natural experiment' – the researchers manipulated the IVs.
  2. (b) The victim either carried a black cane and appeared sober (cane/ill condition), or smelled of alcohol and carried a bottle of liquor wrapped in a brown paper bag (drunk condition).
    1 mark for the cane condition with a detail (black cane, appeared sober/ill); 1 mark for the drunk condition with a detail (smelled of liquor, bottle in a brown paper bag).
Q2Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe the sample of passengers in Piliavin et al.'s study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one weakness of this sample. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) About 4450 men and women travelling on the New York subway (8th Avenue line) on weekdays between 11 a.m. and 3 p.m.; roughly 45% black and 55% white.
    1 mark each for any two of: about 4450 passengers; New York subway / 8th Avenue express line; weekdays, 11 a.m.–3 p.m.; about 45% black, 55% white; both men and women; an opportunity sample of whoever was in the carriage.
  2. (b) The passengers were all from one city and one subway line in 1960s New York, so helping norms may differ in other cultures or in rural areas, which limits generalisability.
    1 mark for identifying a weakness (e.g. one location/culture, could not collect demographic details, same passengers may have seen more than one trial); 1 mark for explaining its effect on the study.
Q3Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Core · Paper 1 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Suggest one reason why the researchers chose the stretch of the subway between 59th Street and 125th Street. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) There were no stops on this stretch for about 7½ minutes, so the passengers could not leave the carriage and the full trial could be observed under the same conditions each time.
    1 mark: non-stop journey of about 7½ minutes. 1 mark: so passengers stayed in the carriage / trials were standardised / the whole incident could be observed.
Q4Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State the aim of Piliavin et al.'s subway Samaritan study. [2]
  2. (b) Describe what the victim did at the start of each trial. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) To investigate, in a natural setting, how the type of victim (ill or drunk), the race of the victim, the presence of a helping model and the number of bystanders affect the speed and frequency of helping a person who collapses.
    1 mark: investigating helping behaviour/bystander helping in a real-life (field) setting; 1 mark: naming at least two of the factors: type of victim (cane/drunk), race of victim, model, group size.
  2. (b) About 70 seconds after the train passed the first station, the victim (standing near a pole in the critical area) staggered forward and collapsed, then lay on the floor looking up at the ceiling until someone helped him.
    1 mark: staggered and collapsed (about 70 seconds after the train left the first station); 1 mark: stayed lying on the floor until helped (the model helped if no passenger did).
Q5Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline how the race of the victim was varied in Piliavin et al.'s study. [2]
  2. (b) Outline one finding about the race of the victim. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) There were four teams, each with its own male victim. Three of the victims were white and one was black, so passengers saw either a white or a black victim collapse.
    1 mark: different victims in different teams/trials; 1 mark: three white victims and one black victim.
  2. (b) Race had little effect when the victim carried a cane, but in the drunk condition there was a slight tendency for passengers to help a victim of their own race.
    1 mark: same-race helping tendency; 1 mark: mainly/especially in the drunk condition (cane victim helped regardless of race).
Q6Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Piliavin et al. found that the cane victim received spontaneous help on 62 of 65 trials. Calculate this as a percentage. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [1]
  2. (b) The drunk victim received spontaneous help on 19 of 38 trials. Calculate this as a percentage. [1]
  3. (c) Using the cost–reward model, explain why the drunk victim was helped less often than the cane victim. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 95%
    62 ÷ 65 × 100 = 95.38… → 95%.
  2. (b) 50%
    19 ÷ 38 × 100 = 50%.
  3. (c) Helping a drunk victim has higher costs (disgust, possible embarrassment, fear he may be violent or vomit) and lower rewards/costs of not helping (others blame him less because his condition seems self-inflicted), so bystanders reduce their arousal in other ways, e.g. by leaving or ignoring him.
    1 mark: costs of helping are higher for the drunk (any example: disgust, harm, embarrassment). 1 mark: costs of NOT helping are lower (he is seen as responsible for his state, less self-blame). 1 mark: link to arousal being reduced by a route other than helping (leaving the area, rationalising).
Q7Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'diffusion of responsibility'. [1]
  2. (b) Explain whether Piliavin et al.'s results supported the idea of diffusion of responsibility. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The more bystanders there are, the less responsible each one feels for helping, so the less likely any one of them is to help.
    B1 for the idea that responsibility is shared/spread among bystanders so each feels less obliged to help.
  2. (b) They did not support it: in this subway setting the victim was helped very quickly and often, and help was not reduced or delayed when more people were in the critical area. The researchers suggested this may be because the victim could clearly be seen and bystanders could not leave the moving train.
    1 mark: not supported. 1 mark: evidence – high rate of help / help was not reduced or delayed when more passengers were present. 1 mark: reason – face-to-face situation, victim clearly visible, passengers could not easily leave between stations.
Q8Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how data were recorded in Piliavin et al.'s study. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Two female observers sat in the adjacent area of the carriage. One recorded the race, sex and location of every passenger in the critical area and adjacent area, and the number who helped. The other recorded the race, sex and location of every helper and timed how long it took for help to arrive (latency), including after the model's help. They also noted any spontaneous comments made by passengers.
    1 mark each for any four of: two female observers; seated in the adjacent area; recorded number/race/sex/location of passengers; recorded race/sex/location of helpers; timed latency of first help (stopwatch); recorded latency after model acted; recorded comments by passengers; data were mostly quantitative with some qualitative comments.
Q9Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the role of the model in Piliavin et al.'s study. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one reason why the effect of the model could not be fully tested. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The model was a white male confederate who helped the victim if nobody else had, either after about 70 seconds (early) or about 150 seconds (late), standing either in the critical area or the adjacent area, to see whether his example increased helping by others.
    1 mark: confederate who helped the victim if no one else did. 1 mark: detail of the conditions – early (70 s) or late (150 s); critical or adjacent area.
  2. (b) In most trials, especially with the cane victim, a passenger helped before the model was due to act, so there were too few trials in which the model actually intervened to compare conditions.
    1 mark: passengers often helped spontaneously before the model was due. 1 mark: so few model trials happened / not enough data for each model condition.
Q10Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A man collapses on a crowded Metrobus in Lahore. Using the cost–reward model from Piliavin et al.'s study, explain whether a passenger standing next to him is likely to help. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Seeing him collapse causes unpleasant arousal in the passenger. The passenger reduces this arousal in the way that costs least and rewards most. Costs of helping (getting dirty, embarrassment, missing a stop) are low if the man looks ill and harmless, and the costs of not helping (guilt, others' disapproval, being next to him) are high because the passenger is close. So the passenger is likely to help directly. If the man seemed drunk or dangerous, costs of helping rise and the passenger might move away or leave it to others.
    1 mark: arousal caused by seeing an emergency; 1 mark: arousal reduced by the response with the best cost–reward balance; 1 mark: applied costs of helping (time, effort, embarrassment, disgust, harm); 1 mark: applied costs of not helping (guilt, blame) / conclusion linked to scenario (close passenger, ill victim → likely to help).
Q11Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Identify two ethical guidelines that were not followed in Piliavin et al.'s study. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one reason why the researchers might argue that the study was still justified. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Informed consent was not obtained; passengers were deceived (they thought the collapse was real). (Also accept: no right to withdraw, no debrief.)
    1 mark each for any two of: informed consent, deception, right to withdraw, debriefing, protection from (psychological) harm.
  2. (b) The study could only produce valid, natural helping behaviour if passengers did not know it was a study; the distress was brief and similar to events passengers might meet anyway, and the findings about when people help in emergencies are useful to society.
    1 mark: reason (realism needed / brief and mild distress / everyday event / usefulness); 1 mark: explained in context of the study.
Q12Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Two friends are discussing Piliavin et al.'s study. Farah says the study has high ecological validity. Hamza says its ethics are so poor that the results should not be used. Explain one point that supports each friend's view. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Farah: the study took place in a real subway carriage with real passengers who did not know they were being studied, so their helping behaviour was natural and reflects how people behave in everyday emergencies. Hamza: passengers did not give informed consent, were deceived into believing a real emergency was happening, could not withdraw, and were not debriefed, so some may have been distressed or felt guilty for not helping.
    2 marks for Farah: real setting/real passengers (1) + unaware so natural behaviour, generalises to everyday life (1). 2 marks for Hamza: named guideline broken – no consent/deception/no right to withdraw/no debrief (1) + consequence, e.g. possible distress or guilt among passengers who did not help (1).
Q13Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the findings of Piliavin et al.'s study about who helped the victim. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one way the findings about the gender of helpers could be applied in a public transport setting today. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most first helpers were male (about 90% of first helpers were men). There was a slight same-race effect: in the drunk condition, people were more likely to help a victim of their own race. Help was given more often and more quickly to the cane victim than to the drunk victim. More passengers left the critical area in the drunk trials than in the cane trials.
    1 mark each for any four of: about 90% of first helpers male; same-race helping more common, especially for the drunk victim; cane victim helped on 62 of 65 trials vs drunk 19 of 38; cane victim helped faster; spontaneous help occurred on 81 of the 103 trials; more people left the critical area with the drunk victim; there was no diffusion of responsibility.
  2. (b) Because women were less likely to give direct physical help (perhaps because of perceived costs), transport staff training or announcements could stress simple low-cost actions everyone can take, such as pressing the emergency alarm, so all passengers feel able to help.
    1 mark: link to finding (men were much more likely to help directly). 1 mark: an ethical, practical application in a transport context (e.g. publicise easy help options, emergency buttons, training).
Q14Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Piliavin et al.'s subway Samaritan study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your evaluation points must be about the use of quantitative data. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: quantitative data (number of trials helped, latencies, percentages of helpers by sex/race) allowed objective comparison of the cane and drunk conditions and statistical analysis; high ecological validity as it was a real-life setting with real passengers; standardised procedure (same route, timings, victim clothing, 70 s collapse) improved reliability. Weaknesses: quantitative data did not explain why passengers chose to help or not – only a few spontaneous comments gave qualitative insight; ethics – no consent, deception, no debrief, possible distress; lack of control over passengers (some may have seen more than one trial, numbers in carriage varied); the drunk/cane conditions were unequal in number of trials; sample limited to New York.
    Level 4 (8–10): two strengths and two weaknesses, each well explained with examples from the study, quantitative data issue included and discussed thoroughly, balanced. Level 3 (5–7): four points, mostly explained, quantitative data issue included; or three detailed points. Level 2 (3–4): points identified with limited explanation or not linked to the study. Level 1 (1–2): brief or generic points. Points: quantitative data (objective, comparable vs lacks reasons), ecological validity, standardisation/reliability, ethics, control of extraneous variables, generalisability.
Q15Piliavin et al. (subway Samaritans) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Q19Milgram (obedience) · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe the shock generator used in Milgram's (1963) study. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) It had a row of 30 switches running from 15 volts to 450 volts in 15-volt steps. Groups of switches had verbal labels from 'Slight Shock' up to 'Danger: Severe Shock', and the last two switches were labelled 'XXX'. It looked realistic but gave no real shocks (the teacher received one genuine 45 V shock as a sample before the study).
    1 mark each for any three of: 30 switches; 15 V to 450 V; increases of 15 V; verbal labels from 'Slight Shock' to 'Danger: Severe Shock'; last switches marked XXX; not real/fake.
Q20Milgram (obedience) · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Outline the learning task the 'learner' was supposed to do in Milgram's study. [2]
  2. (b) Describe what the teacher was told to do each time the learner gave a wrong answer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) It was a word-pair (paired-associate) task. The teacher read pairs of words, then gave one word and four options; the learner chose an answer by pressing one of four switches, which lit up a light on the teacher's panel.
    1 mark: memorising word pairs; 1 mark: chose from four options by pressing a switch/light.
  2. (b) Give the learner an electric shock and move one level (15 volts) higher on the generator for each wrong answer, announcing the voltage level before giving the shock.
    1 mark: give a shock; 1 mark: increase by one level/15 V each time (announce the voltage).
Q21Milgram (obedience) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why Milgram gave each participant a sample shock of 45 volts. [2]
  2. (b) Describe one piece of qualitative data that Milgram recorded. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) To convince the participant that the shock generator was real and that the shocks were painful, so that they believed they were really harming the learner.
    1 mark: to make them believe the shocks/generator were genuine. 1 mark: so the obedience measured was real – they believed they were hurting the learner (increases validity).
  2. (b) Many participants showed signs of extreme tension such as sweating, trembling, stuttering, groaning, biting their lips and digging fingernails into their flesh; 14 showed nervous laughter and three had uncontrollable seizures.
    1 mark for a type of observation (signs of stress, nervous laughter, comments). 1 mark for detail (e.g. sweating, trembling, 14 nervous laughter, 3 full-blown seizures).
Q22Milgram (obedience) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Before the study, Milgram asked 14 Yale psychology students to predict the results. Outline what they predicted and compare this with the actual result. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) They predicted that only a very small proportion – between 0% and 3% (mean about 1.2%) – would go to 450 V. In fact 65% (26 of 40) did, far more than predicted.
    1 mark: predictions 0–3% (or 'hardly anyone'). 1 mark: actual result 65%. 1 mark: comparison – actual obedience was much higher than expected (65 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 54, about 50 times more).
Q23Milgram (obedience) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe what happened when the 'teacher' reached 300 volts in Milgram's study. [2]
  2. (b) Give one reason why this study is described as a controlled laboratory study even though Milgram did not compare conditions. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The learner (Mr Wallace) pounded on the wall of the room. After this he no longer gave answers to the word-pair questions, and the teacher was told to treat no answer as a wrong answer.
    1 mark: learner pounded/banged on the wall. 1 mark: no further answers given / silence treated as wrong answer / five participants stopped at 300 V.
  2. (b) It was carried out in a controlled environment (a laboratory at Yale University) with a highly standardised procedure – the same script, prods, shock generator and confederates for every participant.
    1 mark: controlled setting (Yale lab). 1 mark: standardised procedure/controls (same prods, same learner responses, same shock generator).
Q24Milgram (obedience) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

In Milgram's (1963) study, 26 of the 40 participants continued to the maximum of 450 volts.

  1. (a) Five of the 40 participants stopped at 300 volts. Calculate this as a percentage of the sample. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of participants who stopped before reaching 450 volts. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why 300 volts was an important point in the procedure. [2]

  1. (a) 12.5 %
    5 ÷ 40 × 100 = 12.5 % (B1).
  2. (b) 35 %

  1. (a) Milgram suggested that features of the setting may have increased obedience. Explain two features of the setting at Yale University that could have increased obedience. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest how obedience might change if the same study were run in an ordinary rented office with no university link. [2]

  1. (a) 1. Yale was a respected university, so participants trusted that the experimenter was a legitimate authority and that the study was safe and worthwhile. 2. Q26Milgram (obedience) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how Milgram's study could be used to support a situational explanation of behaviour. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one piece of evidence from the study that suggests individual factors also played a part. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Most participants obeyed to 450 V even though they showed great distress, suggesting that features of the situation – the prestige of Yale, the experimenter's lab coat and authority, the prods and the gradual 15 V steps – rather than their personality caused the behaviour.
    1 mark: definition – situational explanation = behaviour caused by the environment/setting. 1 mark: evidence – 65% obeyed to 450 V despite distress. 1 mark: named situational features (Yale, lab coat, prods, small increments, payment).
  2. (b) Not everyone obeyed: 14 of the 40 participants refused to continue at some point between 300 V and 450 V, so differences between people (e.g. personality, values) must have affected obedience.
    1 mark: 14 (35%) disobeyed / five stopped at 300 V. 1 mark: same situation, different responses, so individual differences matter.
Q27Milgram (obedience) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain whether Milgram's study broke the ethical guideline of protection from harm, and describe what Milgram did to reduce any harm. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Yes: participants experienced extreme stress, e.g. sweating, trembling and three had seizures, more than they would meet in everyday life. To reduce harm, Milgram debriefed them, introduced them to the unharmed learner in a friendly reconciliation, and explained their behaviour was normal; follow-up questionnaires suggested most were glad to have taken part.
    2 marks: guideline broken with evidence (stress signs, seizures). 2 marks: steps taken – debrief, reunion with learner, reassurance, follow-up questionnaire / psychiatric check.
Q28Milgram (obedience) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate Milgram's (1963) study in terms of two strengths and two weaknesses. At least one of your evaluation points must be about generalisability. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Strengths: standardised procedure (same prods, scripted learner responses, same generator) gives high reliability and allows replication; quantitative data (maximum voltage) allowed clear comparison, alongside rich qualitative data on stress which helps explain behaviour; deception meant participants believed the situation, raising internal validity. Weaknesses: generalisability – only 40 American men from New Haven, volunteers, so cannot generalise to women or other cultures; low ecological validity – an artificial task and an unusual setting; ethical problems – deception, harm, the prods undermined the right to withdraw.
    Level 4 (8–10): two strengths and two weaknesses, each well explained and linked to the study, generalisability included and fully discussed. Level 3 (5–7): four points with some explanation, generalisability included. Level 2 (3–4): limited explanation or few links to the study. Level 1 (1–2): brief, generic points.   1. (a) The area immediately around a person's body which they feel belongs to them, and which they become uncomfortable if others enter without invitation.
    B1: the space around a person that they feel is theirs / the preferred distance kept from others.
  2. (b) To provide a comparison: any difference in preferred interpersonal distance between the two sessions could then be caused by the oxytocin itself rather than by expectations or the experience of having a nasal spray, since both sessions were the same apart from the hormone (and neither participants nor researchers knew which spray was given).
    1 mark: placebo acts as a control/comparison condition. 1 mark: so effects can be attributed to oxytocin, not to expectations/the procedure of being sprayed (accept reference to double-blind reducing demand characteristics).

Q66Perry et al. (personal space) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain one reason why laboratory studies of personal space, such as Perry et al.'s, may lack ecological validity. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest one way findings about personal space could be applied in a crowded hospital waiting area in Lahore. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) In real life a real person approaches us in a natural setting for a reason; in Perry et al.'s study participants only imagined a figure approaching and marked on paper where they would like it to stop, so their answers may not match how they react when a stranger really moves close in everyday life.
    1 mark: identifies an artificial feature of a lab study of personal space. 1 mark: explains why behaviour may differ from real life.
  2. (b) Seating could be arranged with small gaps or partitions between chairs so strangers are not forced too close, reducing discomfort and stress for waiting patients.
    1 mark: practical, ethical suggestion. 1 mark: link to the idea that invasion of personal space causes discomfort/arousal.
Q67Perry et al. (personal space) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Each participant was tested once after oxytocin and once after a placebo, on different days. Explain why the two tests were carried out on different days. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how counterbalancing could be used in this design and why it is needed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) So that the oxytocin had fully worn off before the placebo session (or vice versa), meaning the hormone from one session could not affect behaviour in the other.
    1 mark: effect of the hormone wears off / washout; 1 mark: avoids carry-over between conditions.
  2. (b) Half the participants receive oxytocin on the first day and placebo on the second; the other half receive them in the opposite order. This balances out order effects such as being more relaxed with the task the second time.
    1 mark: half each order (ABBA/AB-BA); 1 mark: controls order effects (practice/familiarity).

Q71Perry et al. (personal space) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain whether a person's preferred personal space is better explained by individual or by situational factors. Use Perry et al.'s study and one other example in your answer. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Individual: Perry et al. investigated whether differences between people, such as their level of empathy, relate to how close they like others to be, finding that oxytocin led highly empathic people to prefer closer distances, so personal space depends partly on individual characteristics. Situational: preferred distance also changes with the situation – e.g. people tolerate being very close to strangers in a crowded bus but not in an empty park, and with friends rather than strangers. Both are needed: individual traits set a typical distance, which the situation then adjusts.
    1 mark: defines individual vs situational explanations. 1 mark: individual side linked to Perry et al. (individual differences such as empathy, interacting with oxytocin). 1 mark: situational side with an example. 1 mark: reasoned conclusion.
Q72Perry et al. (personal space) · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain two ethical issues that researchers must deal with when giving participants a hormone such as oxytocin, and how each could be dealt with. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Protection from harm: the hormone could have side effects, so participants should be screened for medical problems, a safe standard dose used and a doctor available. 2.
