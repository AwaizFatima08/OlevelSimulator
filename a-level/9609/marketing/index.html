Q1Product, branding and new product development · Core · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define the term 'product life cycle'. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one extension strategy a company could use for a brand of washing powder in the maturity stage. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The stages a product passes through from its launch to its withdrawal from the market: introduction, growth, maturity and decline (with development before launch), shown by its sales over time.
    1 mark: stages a product goes through over time / sales over its life; 1 mark: names stages (introduction, growth, maturity, decline).
  2. (b) Relaunch it with a new formula (e.g. 'works in cold water') and new packaging, advertised to show the benefit; this gives customers a reason to buy again, so sales rise and the maturity stage is extended. Also accept: new markets, new uses, new sizes, price cut, promotions.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q2Product, branding and new product development · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

SnackPak's main brand, Crunchy Chips, has sold about 2 million packs a month for four years. SnackPak is developing baked lentil crisps for health-conscious consumers.

  1. (a) Identify the stage of the product life cycle that Crunchy Chips appears to be in. [1]
  2. (b) Explain the term 'new product development'. [3]
  3. (c) Explain one benefit to SnackPak of test marketing its baked lentil crisps before a national launch. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Maturity (or saturation).
    1 mark: maturity — sales high but no longer growing.
  2. (b) The process of creating a new product, from generating and screening ideas, concept testing, business analysis and product development to test marketing and launch. For SnackPak, developing baked lentil crisps would involve this process.
    1 mark: creating/designing new products; 1 mark: stages of the process (ideas → screening → development → test → launch); 1 mark: application.
  3. (c) Selling the crisps in one city first (e.g. Islamabad) shows real customer reactions to taste, price and packaging; SnackPak can change the product before spending heavily on a national launch, reducing the risk of an expensive failure.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q3Product, branding and new product development · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

BrightHome sells home electrical goods. Its SolarLite solar lamps had sales of Rs 45 million last year in a market worth Rs 300 million, growing at 18 % a year. The market leader had sales of Rs 60 million. BrightHome's AC Fans are the market leader in a market that is no longer growing.

  1. (a) Calculate the market share of SolarLite lamps. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate SolarLite's relative market share compared with the market leader. [2]
  3. (c) Using the Boston Matrix, explain one reason why BrightHome should continue to invest in SolarLite lamps. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15 %
    45 ÷ 300 × 100 (M1) = 15 % (A1).
  2. (b) 0.75
    Relative market share = own share ÷ largest rival's share = 45 ÷ 60 (M1) = 0.75 (A1) — below 1, so SolarLite is not the market leader.
  3. (c) SolarLite is in a fast-growing market (18 % a year) but has a relative share below 1, so it is a question mark (problem child); with investment in promotion and distribution it could increase share and become a star, and later a cash cow, providing future cash flow when the AC Fans cash cow declines.
    1 mark: identifies question mark (high growth, low relative share); 1 mark: investment could make it a star; 1 mark: portfolio balance/future cash.
Q4Product, branding and new product development · Standard · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Analyse one benefit to a bottled water company of having a strong brand. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bottled water is similar whatever the brand, so a strong, trusted brand (associated with purity and safety) creates customer loyalty; customers choose it over cheaper rivals, so demand becomes more price inelastic and the company can charge a premium price, increasing its profit margin. Q5Product, branding and new product development · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 12 marks

MediGen is a Pakistani pharmaceutical company that mostly makes generic medicines (copies of medicines whose patents have expired). Its profit fell by 20 % last year as several new competitors cut prices. It currently spends 1 % of revenue on R&D; multinational rivals spend about 15 %.

  1. (a) Evaluate whether increasing spending on research and development (R&D) is the best way for MediGen to remain competitive. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: in pharmaceuticals, new and improved medicines (and patents) provide a USP and higher margins; MediGen's main products are generics facing price competition, so R&D into new formulations could differentiate it; R&D can also improve processes, lowering costs. Against: R&D is very expensive and risky — most projects fail and returns come after many years; MediGen's profit has fallen, so finance is limited; larger multinationals have far bigger R&D budgets. Alternatives: cost leadership through efficient production of generics, licensing products from overseas firms, joint ventures, strong distribution to pharmacies and doctors, export markets. Judgement: some targeted R&D (e.g. improving delivery forms of existing medicines) is worthwhile, but given limited funds and the generic market, efficiency and marketing may be more important in the short term; depends on finance and the firm's long-term strategy.
    R&D benefits/risks in context of a generic medicine maker; alternative routes to competitiveness; judgement. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 2, AO4 evaluation 6. Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis in context plus a supported judgement that answers the question for this business (e.g. 'it depends on…' weighed, with a clear conclusion). Level 2 (5–8): developed analysis with some, limited or unsupported evaluation. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and some application; little or no analysis.
Q6Elasticity and sales forecasting · Core · Paper 3 style · 6 marks

ComfortStep sells trainers online. When it raised the price from Rs 500 (per pair, wholesale) to Rs 550, weekly sales fell from 2,000 pairs to 1,700 pairs.

  1. (a) Calculate the price elasticity of demand for ComfortStep's trainers. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the change in ComfortStep's weekly revenue from the trainers. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the revenue changed in this way. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) -1.5
    %ΔQ = (1,700 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = −15 %; %ΔP = (550 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = +10 % (M1). PED = −15 ÷ 10 = -1.5 (A1).
  2. (b) Revenue falls by Rs 65,000
    Before: 2,000 × 500 = Rs 1,000,000; after: 1,700 × 550 = Rs 935,000 (M1). Change = −Rs 65,000 (A1).
  3. (c) Demand is price elastic (PED −1.5, greater than 1 in absolute value): the percentage fall in quantity is larger than the percentage rise in price, so total revenue falls.
    1 mark: demand elastic; 1 mark: %ΔQ > %ΔP so revenue falls.
Q7Elasticity and sales forecasting · Standard · Paper 3 style · 7 marks

Royal Tours sells holiday packages in Pakistan. Average real incomes rose by 6 % and the number of holidays sold rose from 40,000 to 43,600, with no other changes. In a separate period, a 20 % increase in advertising spending led to a 5 % increase in holidays sold.

  1. (a) Calculate the income elasticity of demand for Royal Tours' holidays. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the promotional (advertising) elasticity of demand. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how Royal Tours could use these results in its marketing planning. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) +1.5
    %Δ quantity demanded = (43,600 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 9 % (M1). YED = 9 ÷ 6 = +1.5 (A1).
  2. (b) 0.25
    Promotional elasticity = %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ advertising spending = 5 ÷ 20 (M1) = 0.25 (A1).
  3. (c) Holidays are a luxury (YED +1.5), so demand will rise strongly when incomes grow; Royal Tours can plan more capacity and premium packages in a boom, but should prepare for a sharp fall in a recession. The low promotional elasticity (0.25) shows extra advertising has little effect on sales, so it may be better to spend on other parts of the mix.
    1 mark: interprets YED (luxury, sensitive to income); 1 mark: interprets promotional elasticity (inelastic, advertising ineffective); 1 mark: planning use.
Q8Elasticity and sales forecasting · Standard · Paper 3 style · 7 marks

PureCool sells air coolers. Quarterly sales (thousand units): Year 1 — Q1 42, Q2 58, Q3 66, Q4 50. Year 2 — Q1 46, Q2 62, Q3 74, Q4 54.

  1. (a) Calculate the four-quarter centred moving average for Year 1 Quarter 3 and Year 1 Quarter 4. [4]
  2. (b) Calculate the seasonal variation for Year 1 Quarter 3. [1]
  3. (c) Explain one limitation of using moving averages to forecast PureCool's sales. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1 Q3: 54.5; Year 1 Q4: 55.5 (thousand units)
    Four-quarter moving totals: Y1Q1–Q4 = 216; Y1Q2–Y2Q1 = 220; Y1Q3–Y2Q2 = 224 (M1). Centred total for Y1Q3 = 216 + 220 = 436; ÷ 8 = 54.5 (M1 A1). Y1Q4 = (220 + 224) ÷ 8 = 55.5 (A1).
  2. (b) +11.5
    Actual − centred moving average = 66 − 54.5 = +11.5 (B1).
  3. (c) Moving averages assume past trends and seasonal patterns will continue; an unusually hot summer, a new competitor or a price change would make the forecast inaccurate.
    1 mark: based on past data/assumes continuation; 1 mark: example of external change for an air cooler business.
Q9Elasticity and sales forecasting · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 8 marks

Frosty sells ice cream tubs. Quarterly sales (thousand tubs): Year 1 — Q1 120, Q2 210, Q3 260, Q4 150. Year 2 — Q1 132, Q2 226, Q3 284, Q4 162.

  1. (a) Calculate the centred moving averages for Year 1 Quarter 3 and Year 2 Quarter 2. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the average change in the trend per quarter between Year 1 Quarter 3 and Year 2 Quarter 2. [1]
  3. (c) Using the trend and the Year 1 Quarter 3 seasonal variation, forecast Frosty's sales for Year 3 Quarter 3. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Y1Q3: 186.5; Y2Q2: 199.5
    Moving totals: 740, 752, 768, 792, 804 (M1). Y1Q3 = (740 + 752) ÷ 8 = 186.5 (A1). Y2Q2 = (792 + 804) ÷ 8 = 199.5 (A1).
  2. (b) 4.33 thousand per quarter
    (199.5 − 186.5) ÷ 3 = 4.33 (B1).
  3. (c) 294.7 thousand tubs
    Seasonal variation Y1Q3 = 260 − 186.5 = +73.5 (M1). Y3Q3 is 5 quarters after Y2Q2 (M1). Trend = 199.5 + 5 × 4.3333 = 221.17 (M1). Forecast = 221.17 + 73.5 = 294.7 (A1).
Q10Elasticity and sales forecasting · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 16 marks

AirIndus (an invented airline) carries 50,000 leisure passengers a month at an average fare of Rs 30,000. Its estimates: PED for leisure passengers −1.8; PED for business passengers −0.4. It has some empty seats on most flights.

  1. (a) Calculate the forecast change in monthly revenue from leisure passengers if AirIndus cuts leisure fares by 10 %. [4]
  2. (b) Evaluate how useful price elasticity of demand estimates are to AirIndus when setting its fares. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Revenue rises from Rs 1.500 billion to Rs 1.593 billion: an increase of Rs 93 million.
    %ΔQ = −1.8 × −10 % = +18 % (M1). New passengers = 50,000 × 1.18 = 59,000; new fare = Rs 27,000 (M1). New revenue = 59,000 × 27,000 = Rs 1,593,000,000 (M1). Increase = Rs 93,000,000 (A1).
  2. (b) Useful: they show leisure demand is elastic (−1.8), so a fare cut raises revenue (by Rs 93 million a month), while business demand is inelastic (−0.4), so business fares could be raised to increase revenue; supports price discrimination between segments. Limitations: PED estimates are based on past data and may change (new low-cost rival, recession, fuel costs); they assume other things are equal; revenue rising does not mean profit rises — 9,000 extra passengers need seats (capacity, load factor) and add variable costs (fuel, catering); rivals may match the cut, so the forecast increase may not happen; PED varies along the demand curve, so a larger cut may give a different result. Judgement: PED estimates are a useful guide for segment pricing, but AirIndus should combine them with cost/capacity data and competitor analysis and update them regularly.
    Use the calculation; PED by segment; assumptions and limitations; revenue vs profit; competitor reaction; judgement. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 2, AO4 evaluation 6. Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis in context plus a supported judgement that answers the question for this business (e.g. 'it depends on…' weighed, with a clear conclusion). Level 2 (5–8): developed analysis with some, limited or unsupported evaluation. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and some application; little or no analysis.

Q12Marketing objectives and strategy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Nisa Organics sells premium organic flour, rice and honey in Karachi to high-income health-conscious families. It plans a new marketing strategy.

  1. (a) Explain the term 'coordinated (integrated) marketing mix'. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one way the 4Cs (customer solution, cost to customer, communication, convenience) could help Nisa Organics plan its marketing. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The elements of the marketing mix (product, price, promotion, place) are consistent with each other and with the target market. For Nisa Organics, a premium organic product should have a premium price, be promoted through health and lifestyle media and sold in up-market stores and online.

Q14Marketing objectives and strategy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks

GlowSkin (an invented company) makes skin-care products in Pakistan. Appendix 1: market share 2025 — two multinationals 28 % and 22 %; GlowSkin 6 %; others 44 %. Appendix 2: online beauty sales grew 35 % in 2025; sales through traditional shops grew 3 %. 85 % of GlowSkin's sales are through traditional shops. Appendix 3: survey of women aged 18–30 in cities: 22 % had heard of GlowSkin; 64 % prefer 'natural' ingredients. Appendix 4: marketing budget Rs 120 million (multinationals spend over Rs 1 billion each).

  1. (a) Advise GlowSkin on the marketing strategy it should adopt to achieve its objective of increasing market share from 6 % to 10 % by 2028. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Analysis: the market is crowded with multinational brands; GlowSkin can build on the strong preference for 'natural' ingredients (64 % of young urban women); its weaknesses are low awareness among young urban consumers and dependence on traditional retailers. Options: (1) Target young urban women via digital and influencer marketing, e-commerce and a refreshed brand — builds awareness where the market is growing fastest (online beauty sales +35 %); (2) Product development: a premium 'natural' range to differentiate from multinationals; (3) Price: keep competitive pricing but avoid price wars with deeper-pocketed rivals; (4) Place: expand into pharmacies and online marketplaces. Recommendation: a coordinated mix focused on the young urban segment — USP of natural ingredients, digital promotion, online distribution, mid-range pricing — with a marketing budget increase funded from the cash-cow traditional range. Justify using appendix data; consider budget limits, competitor response, and need to measure progress (market share by channel). Judgement on feasibility of reaching 10 %.
    Use appendices on market share, growth by channel, survey results, budget. Coordinated marketing strategy with clear recommendation and conditions. Levels: Level 4 (16–20): accurate knowledge, strong use of the case data, three or four developed chains of analysis and a well-supported judgement/recommendation throughout, specific to this business and its situation. Level 3 (11–15): developed analysis in context with some supported evaluation. Level 2 (6–10): some analysis, mostly descriptive use of the case; evaluation weak or unsupported. Level 1 (1–5): knowledge with little application.

Q16Nature of marketing (demand, mass/niche, USP, CRM) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

    1 mark: feature that differentiates; 1 mark: reason for customers to choose it; 1 mark: application.

Q18Nature of marketing (demand, mass/niche, USP, CRM) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks

Mass marketing alternative: higher volume, economies of scale, but requires machinery and big promotion budgets, losing the handmade USP. Judgement: niche marketing suits its resources and USP; the key is to strengthen the brand (story, quality) and widen within the niche (export online) rather than move to mass marketing.
    Niche vs mass marketing; USP; scale, risks; context of handmade products; judgement. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 2, AO4 evaluation 6. Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis in context plus a supported judgement that answers the question for this business (e.g. 'it depends on…' weighed, with a clear conclusion). Q20Market research · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

FruitFlow surveyed 200 shoppers (120 women and 80 men) in one shopping mall in Islamabad about a new guava juice. 74 said they would buy it.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of respondents who said they would buy the new juice. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the term 'quota sampling'. [3]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why the survey results might not be reliable. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 37 %
    74 ÷ 200 × 100 (M1) = 37 % (A1).
  2. (b) A sampling method in which the population is divided into groups (e.g. by age or gender) and the researcher chooses a set number of people from each group, in proportion to the target market, but not randomly. FruitFlow chose 120 women and 80 men to match its customer profile.
    1 mark: population split into segments/groups; 1 mark: a set number interviewed from each group (non-random); 1 mark: application.
  3. (c) All 200 people were interviewed in one shopping mall in Islamabad, so they may have higher incomes than FruitFlow's national target market; also people may say they would buy but not actually do so. The sample is small relative to the market.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q21Market research · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) JuiceCo plans to use a survey to decide whether to launch a sugar-free drink nationally. Analyse two factors that could affect the reliability of the results. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Sample size and method: a small or convenience sample (e.g. only people at one office) may not represent the national market, so findings about demand could be wrong, leading to over-production and losses. 2. Questionnaire design and bias: leading questions ('Would you like a healthier drink?') encourage positive answers, and respondents may give socially acceptable answers; JuiceCo may overestimate demand. Also: interviewer bias, time lag, sampling frame.
    Factors: sample size, sampling method, questionnaire bias, respondent honesty, interviewer bias, timing, secondary data reliability. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 4. Level 2 (5–8): two developed chains of analysis, each clearly applied to this business. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and/or application with undeveloped or generic analysis. Evaluation earns no extra marks.
Q22Market research · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether market research is worth the cost for a small café opening in Islamabad. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Worth it: reduces risk — learning about local customers (students, office workers), preferred prices, menus and opening times avoids costly mistakes; cheap methods exist (observation of footfall, short surveys, social media polls, competitor visits); helps obtain bank finance. Not worth it (or limited): professional research is expensive relative to a small budget; the owner may already know the area; results may be unreliable with small samples; tastes change quickly; can test and adapt quickly after opening. Judgement: low-cost primary and secondary research is worth it, especially for location and price decisions, but expensive formal research is not justified; the value depends on the size of the investment and owner's knowledge.
    Benefits vs costs; low-cost methods; reliability; small business context; judgement. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 2, AO4 evaluation 6. Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis in context plus a supported judgement that answers the question for this business (e.g. 'it depends on…' weighed, with a clear conclusion). Level 2 (5–8): developed analysis with some, limited or unsupported evaluation. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and some application; little or no analysis.
Q23International marketing · Core · Paper 3 style · 7 marks

Sialkot Sports makes footballs. Revenue (Rs million): 2024 — exports 180, total 600. 2025 — exports 270, total 750.

  1. (a) Calculate exports as a percentage of total revenue for Sialkot Sports in 2024 and 2025. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in export revenue between 2024 and 2025. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why Sialkot Sports might want to increase its international sales. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2024: 30 %; 2025: 36 %
    180 ÷ 600 × 100 = 30 %; 270 ÷ 750 × 100 = 36 % (M1 A1).
  2. (b) 50 %
    (270 − 180) ÷ 180 × 100 (M1) = 50 % (A1).
  3. (c) Selling in more countries spreads risk: if demand for footballs falls in Pakistan, sales in Europe and the Gulf can keep revenue stable; foreign currency earnings also help when the rupee falls. Also accept: saturated home market, economies of scale, higher prices abroad.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q24International marketing · Standard · Paper 3 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Sabir Foods plans to sell its ready-to-cook spice mixes in Saudi Arabia. It is considering using a local agent rather than opening its own sales office. Analyse two benefits to Sabir Foods of using a local agent. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Local knowledge: the agent understands Saudi consumers' tastes, retail chains and regulations (labelling, halal certification), so Sabir Foods avoids costly mistakes and gets products onto supermarket shelves faster, increasing sales. 2. Lower cost and risk: no need to invest in offices and staff abroad; the agent is paid commission on sales, so costs rise only if sales are made, reducing financial risk if the market entry fails. Also: faster entry, existing contacts.
    Benefits: local knowledge, contacts, lower investment and risk, speed, flexibility. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 4. Level 2 (5–8): two developed chains of analysis, each clearly applied to this business. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and/or application with undeveloped or generic analysis. Evaluation earns no extra marks.
Q25International marketing · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks

TrendWear (an invented company) is a fast-fashion brand from Pakistan with 60 stores at home. Appendix 1: target countries Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda; average income per head is 40–60 % of Pakistan's in two of them. Appendix 2: 70 % of Kenya's 18–30 year olds use social media daily; much lower in rural Tanzania. Appendix 3: climate and dress customs differ between coastal and inland regions. Appendix 4: TrendWear's current advertising and designs are produced centrally in Lahore.

  1. (a) Advise TrendWear whether it should use a global (standardised) marketing strategy or adapt its marketing mix for each country when it expands into East Africa. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Global/standardised: same brand, designs, promotion — economies of scale in production and advertising, consistent brand image, lower cost; works for young, fashion-aware urban consumers who follow global trends online. Adapted (local/glocal): different climates, cultures (modest fashion in some areas), incomes (lower average income means smaller, cheaper ranges), distribution (markets and small shops vs malls), languages; adaptation increases relevance and sales but raises costs and complexity. Pan-regional approach: standardise the brand and core designs, adapt price points, sizing, product range and promotion channels by country. Recommendation: 'think global, act local' — keep the TrendWear brand and online presence, adapt price and product range using the appendix data (income per head, social media use, climate and dress customs), enter first the market with highest fit; justify; mention exchange-rate risk and need for local partners.
    Use appendices on incomes, culture, internet use and costs. Weigh standardisation vs adaptation; clear recommendation with conditions. Levels: Level 4 (16–20): accurate knowledge, strong use of the case data, three or four developed chains of analysis and a well-supported judgement/recommendation throughout, specific to this business and its situation. Level 3 (11–15): developed analysis in context with some supported evaluation. Level 2 (6–10): some analysis, mostly descriptive use of the case; evaluation weak or unsupported. Level 1 (1–5): knowledge with little application.
Q26Pricing · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Rang Fashions makes kurtas. The cost of making each kurta is Rs 1,200. It uses cost-plus pricing with a mark-up of 35 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the selling price of each kurta using cost-plus pricing. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the term 'penetration pricing'. [3]
  3. (c) Explain one disadvantage to Rang Fashions of using cost-plus pricing. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 1,620
    Price = unit cost × (1 + mark-up) = 1,200 × 1.35 (M1) = Rs 1,620 (A1).
  2. (b) Setting a low price when launching a product to gain market share quickly, then raising it once customers are loyal. Rang Fashions could launch its kurtas at Rs 1,300 in the new online store to attract customers from established brands.
    1 mark: low initial price; 1 mark: to gain market share/enter market, price rises later; 1 mark: application.
  3. (c) It ignores demand and competitors' prices: if rivals sell similar kurtas for Rs 1,400, Rang's Rs 1,620 price may be too high and sales could fall; or it may underprice a popular design customers would pay more for.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q27Pricing · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether price skimming is the best pricing strategy for the launch of a new smartwatch brand. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: if the watch has new features (e.g. health monitoring), early adopters will pay a high price; skimming recovers R&D costs quickly, gives high margins and supports a premium image; price can be cut later to reach the mass market. Against: a new, unknown brand lacks the reputation that skimming relies on; the smartwatch market is competitive, with established brands and cheap alternatives, so customers may compare and refuse to pay a premium; high margins attract imitators; later price cuts may annoy early buyers. Alternatives: penetration pricing to gain share, competitive pricing. Judgement: skimming is appropriate only if the watch has a genuine, protected USP and a strong launch promotion; otherwise competitive or penetration pricing is likely better for a new brand.
    Skimming conditions (USP, inelastic demand, early adopters, brand), alternatives, competition; judgement. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 2, AO4 evaluation 6. Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis in context plus a supported judgement that answers the question for this business (e.g. 'it depends on…' weighed, with a clear conclusion). Level 2 (5–8): developed analysis with some, limited or unsupported evaluation. Level 1 (1–4): knowledge and some application; little or no analysis.

  1. (a) Define the term 'channel of distribution'. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one benefit to a new cosmetics brand of promoting its products on social media. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The route a product takes from the producer to the final consumer, including any intermediaries such as wholesalers, retailers or agents.
    1 mark: route/path from producer to consumer; 1 mark: intermediaries (wholesaler, retailer, agent) or direct.
  2. (b) Social media allows the brand to target young women interested in beauty precisely and cheaply, e.g. with influencer videos showing the products; this builds awareness quickly on a small budget and allows two-way interaction and feedback.
    1 mark for knowledge of the term/concept; up to 2 marks for explaining it in the context of the business (application).
Q29Promotion and distribution · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Kashmir Crafts makes handmade shawls and wooden boxes. It sells to three wholesalers, who sell to shops in tourist areas. It plans to start selling directly to consumers through its own website.

  1. (a) Analyse two benefits to Kashmir Crafts of selling its products online directly to consumers instead of through wholesalers. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Higher profit margins: without wholesalers and retailers taking a share, Kashmir Crafts can sell shawls at a lower price than shops while keeping more profit per item, increasing total profit. 2. Wider market and customer contact: a website and online marketplaces reach customers across Pakistan and abroad (diaspora, tourists), 24 hours a day, increasing sales; direct contact gives customer feedback on designs, helping product development and loyalty.
    Benefits: margins, control of brand and price, wider/international reach, customer data, lower costs of premises. Mark scheme: AO1 knowledge 2, AO2 application 2, AO3 analysis 4. Q30Market segmentation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Chai Express sells packaged tea across Pakistan, from loose-leaf black tea to green tea and single-serve tea sachets.

  1. (a) Explain the term 'market segmentation'. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two ways Chai Express could segment the market for its tea products. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Dividing a market into distinct groups of consumers with similar characteristics or needs (e.g. by age, income, location, lifestyle) so that a business can target them with a suitable marketing mix.
    1 mark: dividing the market into groups; 1 mark: similar characteristics/needs; 1 mark: purpose — target with tailored marketing mix.
  2. (b) 1. Demographic (age/income): offer premium loose-leaf tea for higher-income older customers and cheaper single-serve sachets for students. 2. Geographic: stronger, milky tea blends for Punjab and green (kahwa) tea for Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the northern areas, reflecting regional tastes.
    For each of two methods: 1 mark method identified, 1 mark applied to tea.
