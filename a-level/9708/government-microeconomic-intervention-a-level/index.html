  1. (a) Define marginal revenue product of labour. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a firm's demand curve for labour slopes downwards. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The extra revenue a firm earns from employing one more worker: marginal physical product × marginal revenue (or price in perfect competition).
    1 change in total revenue from one more worker; 1 MPP × MR.
  2. (b) Diminishing marginal returns: as more workers are added to fixed capital, MPP and therefore MRP fall; the firm hires up to where MRP = wage, so at a lower wage it hires more workers; the MRP curve is the demand curve.

Q8Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain, with the help of a diagram, how a trade union may raise the wage in a competitive labour market, and the effect on employment. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether the effect of a trade union on employment would be different if the employer were a monopsonist. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) By collective bargaining the union sets a wage above equilibrium; the supply curve becomes horizontal at the union wage up to the original supply; firms move up the demand (MRP) curve so employment falls.
    1 collective bargaining/threat of strike sets wage above equilibrium; 1 diagram: labour market with W and L axes, wage floor; 1 employment falls along MRP/demand curve; 1 excess supply of labour (or effect smaller if demand is wage inelastic / productivity agreements shift MRP).
  2. (b) Yes: a union facing a monopsonist acts as bilateral monopoly; a negotiated wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive level can raise both wage and employment. The outcome depends on bargaining strength and where the wage is set; above the competitive level employment falls.
    Analysis (up to 3): monopsony pays below MRP; union wage makes MCL flat; employment can rise. Consideration (1+): if wage set too high employment falls; firms may substitute capital; depends on relative bargaining power; judgement.

Q10Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why an individual's supply curve of labour may bend backwards at high wage rates. [4]
  2. (b) Consider the implications of a backward-bending supply curve for a government that cuts income tax rates to increase hours worked. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A higher wage has a substitution effect (leisure becomes more expensive → work more) and an income effect (higher income → want more leisure, a normal good → work less). At high wages the income effect can outweigh the substitution effect, so hours offered fall.
    1 substitution effect explained; 1 income effect explained; 1 at low wages substitution dominates (upward slope); 1 at high wages income effect dominates (backward bend).
  2. (b) A tax cut raises the net wage; for workers on the upward part hours rise, but for high earners on the backward-bending part hours may fall, so the policy may not raise labour supply; effect varies between groups and contracts often fix hours.
    Analysis (up to 3): tax cut = higher net wage; outcome depends on which effect dominates for each group; may reduce hours of high earners. Consideration (1+): many workers cannot choose hours; participation decisions (second earners) may respond more; judgement.

    1: intended aim; 1: information failure/unintended consequence (smuggling because domestic price is below foreign price); 1: higher cost/opportunity cost of public funds; 1: net welfare lower than intended → government failure.

In the market for rented flats in a large city (invented data), demand is Qd = 120 − 2R and supply is Qs = 20 + 3R, where R is the monthly rent in Rs thousand and Q is in thousand flats.

  1. (a) Calculate the equilibrium rent and number of flats rented. [2]
  2. (b) The government sets a maximum rent of Rs 15 000 a month. Calculate the shortage of flats. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two ways in which this rent control could lead to government failure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rent Rs 20 000 a month; 80 000 flats.
    120 − 2R = 20 + 3R → R = 20 (1); Q = 80 (1).
  2. (b) 25 000 flats.
    Qd = 120 − 30 = 90; Qs = 20 + 45 = 65 (1); shortage = 25 thousand (1).
  3. (c) Fewer flats are offered (65 000 instead of 80 000), so some households who would pay the market rent cannot find homes; landlords may cut maintenance, ask for illegal 'key money' or switch flats to short-term or commercial use; flats go to those with connections rather than greatest need; long-term building of new rental housing falls.

Q53Government failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why it is difficult for a government to measure the marginal external cost of pollution accurately. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether tradable pollution permits avoid this problem. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) External costs fall on many people over a wide area and a long time; health damage, lost crops and harm to ecosystems have no market price and need shadow prices; pollution comes from many sources, so the share caused by each firm is hard to separate; scientific evidence is uncertain and costs rise as pollution builds up.
    1 mark each for four points: many third parties/diffuse; no market price/shadow pricing; multiple sources; long-term and uncertain effects; MEC varies with output and location.
  2. (b) Permits let the government set the total quantity of pollution, and the market sets the price, so it does not need to know each firm's abatement costs; but it still needs to know the socially optimal level of emissions, which depends on the external cost, and to measure each firm's emissions; if too many permits are issued the price collapses.
    Up to 2 for analysis: cap fixes quantity; firms with low abatement costs cut most; price discovered by market. Up to 2 for evaluation: setting the right cap still needs MEC information; monitoring and enforcement; risk of over-allocation, lobbying; judgement.
Q54Government failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

In an invented country, households pay an average of Rs 40 per unit of electricity, while the cost of supplying it is Rs 52 per unit. Households use 120 billion units a year. The government has kept the price unchanged for two years before an election.

  1. (a) Calculate the annual cost of keeping the household price below the cost of supply, in Rs billion. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how political self-interest can lead to government failure, using this example. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 1 440 billion.
    Subsidy per unit = 52 − 40 = Rs 12 (1); 12 × 120 billion = Rs 1 440 billion (1).
  2. (b) Politicians may keep electricity prices low before elections to win votes, even though the price is below the cost of supply; this encourages over-use of electricity, leaves suppliers short of money to maintain the network, and uses tax revenue that has a high opportunity cost, so resources are misallocated.
    1: politicians act to maximise votes/short-term popularity (public choice); 1: price below cost → overconsumption (P < MC); 1: losses/debts for suppliers → under-investment, power cuts; 1: high opportunity cost of public funds/benefits go mainly to large users.
Q55Government failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a government's promise to rescue state-owned banks if they get into difficulty may create moral hazard. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one other way in which government intervention may distort incentives. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Because the banks expect to be rescued, they do not bear the full cost of failure, so their managers take bigger risks (e.g. lending to unprofitable or politically connected borrowers); if the risks go wrong, taxpayers pay.
    1: guarantee protects the bank from consequences; 1: incentive to take excessive risks; 1: losses shifted to taxpayers / resources misallocated.
  2. (b) E.g. means-tested benefits withdrawn as income rises reduce the incentive to work more (poverty trap); guaranteed crop prices encourage farmers to over-produce one crop; free services with no price can lead to over-use.
    1: identifies intervention; 1: incentive affected; 1: resulting misallocation.
Q56Government failure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

In an invented market, MPB = MSB = 110 − Q and MPC = 20 + Q. Production causes a constant marginal external cost of $10 per unit. Q is in thousand units and values are in $ per unit.

  1. (a) Calculate the free-market output. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the socially optimal output and the correct tax per unit. [2]
  3. (c) The government overestimates the external cost and sets a tax of $30 per unit. Calculate the new output. [1]
  4. (d) Calculate the welfare loss with the $30 tax and the welfare loss in the free market, and explain what your answers show. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 45 thousand units.
    110 − Q = 20 + Q → Q = 45.
  2. (b) 40 thousand units; $10 per unit.
    MSC = 30 + Q; 110 − Q = 30 + Q → Q = 40 (1); tax = MEC = $10 (1).
  3. (c) 30 thousand units.
    110 − Q = 50 + Q → Q = 30.
  4. (d) Loss with the tax: $100 000; free-market loss: $25 000. The badly set tax causes a larger welfare loss than doing nothing — government failure.
    With tax: at Q = 30, MSB − MSC = 80 − 60 = 20; loss = ½ × (40 − 30) × 20 = 100 thousand (1). Free market: at Q = 45, MSC − MSB = 75 − 65 = 10; loss = ½ × 5 × 10 = 25 thousand (1). Intervention made allocation worse (1) because of imperfect information about the size of the externality (1).

(a) State-owned enterprises often have soft budget constraints, political interference, conflicting objectives and weak incentives, causing X-inefficiency and losses; but they may provide socially valuable services that private firms would not, and private firms can also be inefficient or abuse market power. In the market for a grain crop in an invented country, demand is Qd = 30 − 0.05P and supply is Qs = −6 + 0.04P, where P is in $ per tonne and Q is in million tonnes a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity without intervention. [2]
  2. (b) The government sets a guaranteed minimum price of $500 per tonne and buys any surplus. Calculate the surplus and the cost of buying it. [2]
  3. (c) Storage costs $25 per tonne a year. Calculate the annual cost of storing the surplus. [1]
  4. (d) Consider whether this support price is an example of government failure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) $400 per tonne; 10 million tonnes.
    30 − 0.05P = −6 + 0.04P → 0.09P = 36 → P = 400 (1); Q = 30 − 20 = 10 (1).
  2. (b) Surplus 9 million tonnes; cost $4.5 billion.
    Qd = 30 − 25 = 5; Qs = −6 + 20 = 14; surplus = 9 million tonnes (1); 9 million × $500 = $4 500 million (1).
  3. (c) $225 million.
    9 million × $25 = $225 million.
  4. (d) Farmers gain a higher, stable income and food security may improve, but consumers pay more (and buy less: 5 instead of 10 million tonnes), taxpayers pay $4.5 billion plus storage, and resources are drawn into over-producing a crop that partly goes to waste; unless the surplus is used (exports, food aid, buffer stock) the net welfare loss suggests government failure.

Q60Government failure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 9 marks

Invented data: legal cigarette sales in a country were 4.0 billion packs a year, with a tax of Rs 100 per pack; illicit (smuggled or untaxed) packs were estimated at 15 % of total consumption. The tax was raised to Rs 160 per pack. Legal sales fell to 2.8 billion packs and the illicit share rose to 30 % of total consumption.

  1. (a) Calculate the tax revenue from legal sales before and after the tax rise, in Rs billion. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate total consumption (legal plus illicit) before and after the tax rise, in billion packs to two decimal places, and the percentage change in total consumption to one decimal place. [3]
  3. (c) Consider whether the tax rise should be judged a success or an example of government failure. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before Rs 400 billion; after Rs 448 billion.
    4.0 billion × Rs 100 = 400 (1); 2.8 billion × Rs 160 = 448 (1).
  2. (b) Before 4.71 billion packs; after 4.00 billion packs; a fall of 15.0 %.
    Before: legal sales are 85 % of total, so 4.0/0.85 = 4.71 (1); after: 2.8/0.70 = 4.00 (1); (4.00 − 4.706)/4.706 × 100 = −15.0 % (1).
  3. (c) Partly a success: revenue rose by 12 % and total smoking fell by 15 %, reducing health costs. But legal sales fell 30 % while illicit packs rose from about 0.71 to 1.20 billion, so much of the effect was a switch to untaxed, unregulated cigarettes, which may be more harmful and fund crime; the outcome depends on enforcement against smuggling.
    Up to 2 for analysis using the data: revenue +48 bn; total consumption −15 %; illicit packs 0.71 → 1.20 bn (smuggling as unintended consequence). Up to 2 for evaluation: objective (health vs revenue), enforcement costs, quality risks of illicit products, justified judgement.
