Q2Labour markets and intervention · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define marginal revenue product of labour. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a firm's demand curve for labour slopes downwards. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The extra revenue a firm earns from employing one more worker: marginal physical product × marginal revenue (or price in perfect competition).
    1 change in total revenue from one more worker; 1 MPP × MR.
  2. (b) Diminishing marginal returns: as more workers are added to fixed capital, MPP and therefore MRP fall; the firm hires up to where MRP = wage, so at a lower wage it hires more workers; the MRP curve is the demand curve.
    1 diminishing marginal returns/falling MPP in short run; 1 MRP falls as employment rises; 1 profit-maximising firm employs where wage = MRP, so a lower wage → more workers (MRP curve = demand curve).

  2. (b) Explain why economic rent forms a large part of the earnings of top sports players. [3]
  3. (c) State two factors that affect the elasticity of supply of labour to an occupation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 74 million a year
    1 80 − 6 = Rs 74 million.
  2. (b) Their supply is highly inelastic because talent is rare and cannot be quickly trained; demand (from leagues, sponsors, broadcasters) is high, so the wage is far above transfer earnings.

Q5Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The supply of labour to an occupation is L = 20W − 200, where W is the hourly wage in $ and L the number of workers. The market wage is $30 per hour. What is the total economic rent earned by the workers per hour? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A $4,000
    L = 400 at W = 30; supply starts at W = 10 when L = 0. Total earnings = 30 × 400 = $12,000. Transfer earnings = area under the supply curve = ½ × 400 × (10 + 30) = $8,000. Economic rent = area above supply, below the wage = ½ × 400 × 20 = $4,000. C is transfer earnings.
Q6Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain two reasons why software engineers in Lahore usually earn more than food delivery riders. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether a higher national minimum wage would reduce the wage differential between the two groups. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Higher MRP: engineers' productivity and the value of software output is high, so demand for them is high; supply is limited by long training and scarce skills (inelastic), whereas riders need few skills, so supply is large and elastic.
    Up to 2 for demand/MRP reason; up to 2 for supply reason (training, skills, barriers to entry); or other valid reasons such as international demand for engineers.
  2. (b) It would raise riders' pay (if the minimum is above their market wage and enforced) so the gap narrows, but riders may lose jobs or hours, many are self-employed gig workers outside the law, and engineers' pay is unaffected or rises further.
    Analysis (up to 3): minimum wage above equilibrium raises pay of low-paid; engineers paid well above minimum so differential falls; possible excess supply/unemployment among riders. Consideration (1+): enforcement in informal/gig work, platform responses, differentials may re-emerge; judgement.
Q7Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain, with the help of a diagram, how a trade union may raise the wage in a competitive labour market, and the effect on employment. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether the effect of a trade union on employment would be different if the employer were a monopsonist. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) By collective bargaining the union sets a wage above equilibrium; the supply curve becomes horizontal at the union wage up to the original supply; firms move up the demand (MRP) curve so employment falls.
    1 collective bargaining/threat of strike sets wage above equilibrium; 1 diagram: labour market with W and L axes, wage floor; 1 employment falls along MRP/demand curve; 1 excess supply of labour (or effect smaller if demand is wage inelastic / productivity agreements shift MRP).
  2. (b) Yes: a union facing a monopsonist acts as bilateral monopoly; a negotiated wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive level can raise both wage and employment. The outcome depends on bargaining strength and where the wage is set; above the competitive level employment falls.
    Analysis (up to 3): monopsony pays below MRP; union wage makes MCL flat; employment can rise. Consideration (1+): if wage set too high employment falls; firms may substitute capital; depends on relative bargaining power; judgement.
Q8Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Invented article. A food delivery platform in Karachi used 15,000 riders. It cut the payment per delivery from Rs 120 to Rs 100. Three months later 14,100 riders were still working for it. Two smaller platforms also operate in the city.

  1. (a) Calculate the wage elasticity of supply of riders. Give your answer to two decimal places. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why the supply of riders might be wage inelastic. [3]
  3. (c) Consider whether the data suggest that the platform company has monopsony power. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.36
    M1 %ΔL = −900 ÷ 15,000 = −6%; %ΔW = −20 ÷ 120 = −16.67%. A1 −6 ÷ −16.67 = 0.36.
  2. (b) Few alternative jobs for low-skilled workers in the city; riders have already bought motorcycles; many need income urgently and cannot afford to be unemployed; low occupational mobility.
    1 + 1 for two reasons; 1 for development linking to small change in labour supplied.
  3. (c) Possibly: it could cut pay by 17% and still keep 94% of riders, suggesting few alternative employers; but riders may work for several platforms, the data cover a short period and other factors (unemployment rising) may explain it.
    1 monopsony = dominant buyer of labour able to set wage; 1 evidence: large pay cut, small fall in riders; 1 consideration of other explanations/limitations.
Q9Labour markets and intervention · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain why an individual's supply curve of labour may bend backwards at high wage rates. [4]
  2. (b) Consider the implications of a backward-bending supply curve for a government that cuts income tax rates to increase hours worked. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A higher wage has a substitution effect (leisure becomes more expensive → work more) and an income effect (higher income → want more leisure, a normal good → work less). At high wages the income effect can outweigh the substitution effect, so hours offered fall.
    1 substitution effect explained; 1 income effect explained; 1 at low wages substitution dominates (upward slope); 1 at high wages income effect dominates (backward bend).
  2. (b) A tax cut raises the net wage; for workers on the upward part hours rise, but for high earners on the backward-bending part hours may fall, so the policy may not raise labour supply; effect varies between groups and contracts often fix hours.
    Analysis (up to 3): tax cut = higher net wage; outcome depends on which effect dominates for each group; may reduce hours of high earners. Consideration (1+): many workers cannot choose hours; participation decisions (second earners) may respond more; judgement.

Q11Labour markets and intervention · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

In the market for unskilled factory labour in an invented country, labour demand is Ld = 1000 − 20W and labour supply is Ls = −200 + 40W, where W is the daily wage in $ and L is in thousand workers.

  1. (a) Calculate the equilibrium wage and level of employment. [2]
  2. (b) A minimum wage of $24 per day is introduced. Calculate the change in employment and the excess supply of labour. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why a minimum wage could raise both the wage and employment if the labour market were a monopsony. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) W = $20 per day; L = 600 thousand
    M1 1000 − 20W = −200 + 40W → 60W = 1200. A1 W = $20, L = 600 thousand.
  2. (b) Employment falls by 80 thousand (to 520 thousand); excess supply = 240 thousand
    M1 Ld = 1000 − 480 = 520; M1 Ls = −200 + 960 = 760. A1 employment −80 thousand and excess supply 240 thousand.
  3. (c) A monopsonist restricts hiring to pay a low wage (MCL above supply). A minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage makes MCL horizontal at the minimum wage up to the supply curve, so the firm hires more workers at a higher wage.
    1 monopsonist hires where MCL = MRP and pays below MRP; 1 minimum wage removes the need to raise the wage for all workers when hiring one more, so MCL = minimum wage; 1 employment rises towards the competitive level if the minimum is set below the competitive wage.
Q12Labour markets and intervention · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether a national minimum wage is the best way to reduce poverty among low-paid workers. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) A minimum wage raises pay for those who keep their jobs and can raise employment under monopsony, but it may cause unemployment in competitive markets, does not reach informal workers or the unemployed, and poverty depends on household circumstances. Alternatives (in-work benefits, negative income tax, training, progressive taxes) may target poverty better; a combination is usually best.
    Mark bands (20): Level 4 (15–20) accurate, well-explained analysis of both sides, relevant diagram(s) fully used, and a reasoned conclusion that answers the question in context; Level 3 (10–14) mostly accurate analysis, some evaluation but conclusion partly asserted; Level 2 (5–9) limited or partly inaccurate analysis, evaluation mostly descriptive; Level 1 (1–4) a few relevant points. One-sided answers are capped at Level 2. Analysis: competitive labour market diagram with minimum wage above equilibrium → higher wage, lower employment, excess supply; monopsony diagram → higher wage and employment; higher spending of low-paid workers; incentive to work. Evaluation: size of minimum relative to median wage; elasticity of demand for labour; informal sector and enforcement (large in low-income countries); poverty is household-based (number of earners, dependants); alternatives: transfer payments, tax credits, universal basic income, education/training to raise MRP, each with costs (funding, disincentives, time lags). Conclusion: 'best' must be judged against alternatives and context.

Q16Policies to correct market failure · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how extending property rights could reduce overfishing in a coastal fishery. [3]
  2. (b) State two problems with using property rights to solve this problem. [2]

  1. (a) Open-access fish stocks are a common resource, so each boat overfishes. Giving communities or firms ownership/quotas of the stock makes them bear the cost of depletion, so they limit catches to protect future income.

Q17Policies to correct market failure · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

The weekly market for plastic shopping bags in a city has demand Qd = 900 − 100P and supply Qs = −100 + 100P, where P is the price in Rs per bag and Q is in thousand bags. To reduce litter, the government puts a specific tax of Rs 2 per bag on sellers.

  1. (a) Calculate the new price paid by consumers and the new quantity. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the government's weekly tax revenue. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate how much of the tax per bag is paid by consumers and how much by producers. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 6; 300 thousand bags
    M1 new supply Qs = −100 + 100(P − 2) = −300 + 100P; 900 − 100P = −300 + 100P. A1 P = Rs 6, Q = 300 thousand.
  2. (b) Rs 600 thousand
    1 2 × 300 thousand = Rs 600 thousand.
  3. (c) Consumers Rs 1 per bag; producers Rs 1 per bag
    1 original equilibrium P = Rs 5 (900 − 100P = −100 + 100P), so consumers pay 6 − 5 = Rs 1; 1 producers receive 6 − 2 = Rs 4, so they bear 5 − 4 = Rs 1.

Q19Policies to correct market failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

In the market for household solar water heaters in an invented country, MPB = 80 − Q, MSB = 100 − Q and MPC = MSC = 20 + Q, where prices are in $ and Q is in thousand heaters per year.

  1. (a) Calculate the free-market output and the socially optimal output. [2]
  2. (b) The government pays a subsidy of $20 per unit to producers. Calculate the total cost of the subsidy. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why the government may set the subsidy at the wrong level. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Free market: 30 thousand; social optimum: 40 thousand
    1 80 − Q = 20 + Q → Q = 30; 1 100 − Q = 20 + Q → Q = 40.
  2. (b) $800 thousand ($0.8 million)
    M1 with subsidy MPC falls to Q; 80 − Q = Q → Q = 40 thousand. A1 20 × 40 thousand = $800 thousand.
  3. (c) The external benefit is hard to measure in money (information failure), so the subsidy may be too high or too low; lobbying may also distort it.
    1 difficulty valuing external benefits/imperfect information; 1 consequence: over- or under-provision.
Q20Policies to correct market failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Invented data. To reduce winter smog and congestion, a city government introduces a daily charge of Rs 500 for cars entering the central business area of Lahore. Car trips into the area fall from 400,000 to 340,000 a day.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage fall in car trips and the daily revenue from the charge. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a road-user charge can correct the negative externalities of car use. [3]
  3. (c) Consider one reason why the charge may not achieve the socially optimal number of trips. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15% fall; Rs 170 million a day
    1 60,000 ÷ 400,000 = 15%; 1 340,000 × 500 = Rs 170 million.
  2. (b) Drivers ignore the costs they impose on others (congestion, smog). The charge raises the private cost of driving towards the social cost, so drivers with low benefit switch mode or time, reducing trips towards the social optimum.
    1 external costs of congestion and pollution; 1 charge internalises the externality (MPC + charge ≈ MSC); 1 trips fall towards where MSC = MSB.
  3. (c) The external cost is hard to measure, so the charge may be set too low or high; demand for car trips may be inelastic with poor public transport; traffic may divert to roads outside the zone.
    1 reason; 1 explanation; 1 judgement/consideration (e.g. effect depends on using revenue to improve buses).
Q21Policies to correct market failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a large tax on cigarettes may not reduce smoking by very much. [3]
  2. (b) Consider whether such a tax is still worthwhile for the government. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cigarettes are addictive with few substitutes, so demand is price inelastic: the tax raises price but quantity falls only slightly; smokers may also switch to cheaper illegal or untaxed products.
    1 addictive/habit-forming, few substitutes; 1 price inelastic demand → small fall in quantity; 1 smuggling/illegal market or cheaper brands.
  2. (b) Yes in some ways: it raises large revenue (inelastic demand) that can fund healthcare and anti-smoking campaigns, and may deter young people whose demand is more elastic; but it is regressive, may encourage smuggling, and does little to cut external costs if consumption barely falls.
    Analysis (up to 3): revenue, long-run elasticity higher, effect on new smokers, internalising external costs. Consideration (1+): regressive, illegal markets, enforcement; judgement.

Q23Policies to correct market failure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether tradable pollution permits are more effective than direct regulation in reducing carbon emissions from power stations. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Permits fix the total quantity of emissions and let firms that can cut cheaply do so, so the target is reached at least cost and firms have an incentive to innovate; regulation is simpler, more certain for each plant and easier to enforce in some countries. Effectiveness depends on the cap, monitoring, permit allocation and market conditions.

Conclusion on 'more effective' with conditions.
Q24Policies to correct market failure · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that providing information and using nudges are better ways to reduce the consumption of sugary drinks than placing a tax on them. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Information and nudges tackle the root cause (information failure, present bias) cheaply and preserve choice, but effects are often small; a tax directly raises price, cuts consumption if demand is elastic enough, raises revenue and pushes firms to reformulate, but is regressive and may be weak if demand is inelastic. A combination is often most effective.

Q28Equity and redistribution · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by a universal basic income. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether a universal basic income would reduce inequality more effectively than means-tested benefits. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) A regular, unconditional cash payment made by the state to every adult, whatever their income or employment status.
    1 regular payment to all citizens/adults; 1 not means-tested or conditional on work.
  2. (b) It avoids the poverty trap and stigma, has full take-up and low administration costs, so it reaches all the poor; but paying everyone is very expensive, so the amount may be too low to cut inequality unless financed by progressive taxes; means-tested benefits target money on the poorest.
    Analysis (up to 4): means-tested benefits are withdrawn as income rises → high effective marginal tax rates/poverty trap; low take-up, stigma, admin cost; UBI simple and keeps work incentives at the margin; distribution depends on how it is financed. Evaluation (up to 2): fiscal cost, possible inflation, may reduce labour supply (income effect), equal payment to rich and poor; judgement.
Q29Equity and redistribution · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether progressive income taxes are the most effective way to reduce inequality of income and wealth in a developing economy. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Progressive taxes narrow post-tax income gaps, but in many developing economies few people pay income tax, the informal sector is large and evasion is common; wealth inequality needs wealth, property or inheritance taxes. Spending on education and health, transfers, minimum wages and land reform may be more effective; a mix is needed.

Evaluation: narrow tax base, informal economy, evasion and avoidance; disincentives to work and invest; capital flight; income taxes do little for wealth inequality (land and property); regressive indirect taxes may dominate the tax system; alternatives — cash transfers (e.g. targeted programmes for poor households), universal education and health, property taxes, minimum wage, microfinance — with their limitations. Conclusion on 'most effective'.
Q30Government failure · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by government failure in microeconomic intervention. [2]
  2. (b) Using the example of a maximum price set below the equilibrium price for wheat flour, consider how such intervention may lead to government failure. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) When government intervention to correct a market failure leads to a less efficient allocation of resources than before (a net welfare loss).
    1 intervention in a market; 1 results in a worse/less efficient outcome than without it.
  2. (b) The price ceiling causes excess demand: shortages, queues, rationing by waiting, black markets, hoarding and smuggling; farmers and millers have less incentive to supply, so output falls. But the policy may protect the poor if combined with subsidies or a buffer stock, so failure depends on design and enforcement.
    Analysis (up to 4): diagram with max price below equilibrium; Qd > Qs; shortages, illegal markets at higher prices, lower quality; reduced supply incentive; administrative costs; unintended consequences. Evaluation (up to 2): information problems vs benefits to low-income consumers; can be avoided with release of government stocks, targeted subsidies; judgement on whether the outcome is worse than the free market.
