Q2Policies to correct current account imbalances · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A North African country has a current account deficit equal to 5 % of its GDP, mainly because of high imports of consumer goods.

  1. (a) State two expenditure-reducing policies a government could use to reduce a current account deficit. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a fall in national income is likely to reduce spending on imports. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: higher income tax or sales tax; lower government spending; higher interest rates; a reduction in the money supply/credit.
    1 mark each for any two (contractionary fiscal or monetary measures).
  2. (b) With lower incomes households and firms spend less in total, and part of every unit of spending goes on imports (the marginal propensity to import), so imports fall.
    1 mark: lower income → lower total spending/consumption; 1 mark: some of that spending is on imported goods (imports depend on income, normal goods), so import spending falls.

Q6Policies to correct current account imbalances · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Extract (invented for practice): A South Asian country's imports of mobile phones rose to US$2.1 billion last year. Its current account deficit is 3.8 % of GDP. The government proposes a 25 % tariff on imported phones. Most local phone factories assemble phones from imported parts.

  1. (a) Distinguish between expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing policies. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether the proposed tariff on mobile phones is likely to reduce the current account deficit. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expenditure-switching policies change the relative price or availability of imports and domestic goods so spending moves towards domestic output (e.g. tariffs, devaluation); expenditure-reducing policies lower total spending in the economy, which reduces spending on imports (e.g. higher taxes or interest rates).
    1 mark for each correctly explained type (with or without an example).
  2. (b) It may reduce imports of phones if demand is price elastic and domestic substitutes exist, but if demand is inelastic and there are no local producers, spending on imports may fall only a little; retaliation and higher costs for businesses may also reduce exports.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: tariff raises the domestic price of imported phones → quantity of imports falls; switch to domestic assemblers; tariff revenue. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: effect depends on PED for phones (likely inelastic for a necessity), availability of domestic substitutes (domestic phones may use imported parts), retaliation against exports, smuggling, and it treats a symptom not the cause (low competitiveness).

Q10Policies to correct current account imbalances · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Invented data for Country S: GDP $300bn; current account deficit 4.5 % of GDP; marginal propensity to import 0.3. The government cuts its spending by $6bn. The value of the multiplier is 1.5.

  1. (a) Calculate the current account deficit in $bn before the spending cut. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the fall in national income and the fall in imports that the spending cut causes. [2]
  3. (c) Assuming exports are unchanged, calculate the new current account deficit as a percentage of the new GDP. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  4. (d) Consider whether cutting government spending is a good way to reduce this deficit. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) $13.5bn
    4.5 % × 300 = 13.5.
  2. (b) National income falls by $9bn; imports fall by $2.7bn
    6 × 1.5 = 9; 0.3 × 9 = 2.7. 1 each.
  3. (c) 3.7 %
    M1 13.5 − 2.7 = 10.8 and new GDP 300 − 9 = 291; A1 10.8 ÷ 291 × 100 = 3.7 %.
  4. (d) It works (deficit falls from 4.5 % to 3.7 % of GDP) but at a high cost: $9bn of lost output and higher unemployment to save $2.7bn of imports. It does not tackle competitiveness; other policies (supply-side, exchange rate) may be better in the long run.
    1 effective in reducing imports; 1 large output/jobs cost relative to import saving; 1 judgement/alternative.
Q11Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Data (invented for practice): Before a 10 % devaluation, a country's exports were worth US$30 billion and its imports US$45 billion a year, both priced in US dollars. In the first year after the devaluation, the US dollar price of its exports fell by 10 % and export volumes rose by 4 %. The US dollar price of its imports was unchanged and import volumes fell by 3 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the export earnings and import spending, in US$ billion, one year after the devaluation, and state whether the trade deficit has become larger or smaller. Give your answers to two decimal places. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the trade deficit might become smaller over a longer period after the devaluation. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Exports US$28.08 bn; imports US$43.65 bn; the deficit rises from US$15.00 bn to US$15.57 bn, so it becomes larger.
    M1: exports = 30 × 0.90 × 1.04 = 28.08; M1: imports = 45 × 0.97 = 43.65; A1: deficit = 43.65 − 28.08 = 15.57, larger than 15.00.
  2. (b) In the short run demand for exports and imports is price inelastic because contracts are fixed and buyers take time to find substitutes; over time demand becomes more elastic, export volumes rise more and import volumes fall more, so earnings rise and the deficit falls (a J-curve pattern).
    1 mark: short-run demand is inelastic (contracts, habits, lack of substitutes); 1 mark: over time PED for exports and imports rises as buyers adjust; 1 mark: when the combined responsiveness is large enough, export earnings rise relative to import spending and the deficit narrows (J-curve).
Q12Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether expenditure-switching policies are more effective than expenditure-reducing policies in correcting a current account deficit. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expenditure-switching avoids a deliberate fall in national income and targets competitiveness, but depends on price elasticities, retaliation and inflation; expenditure-reducing policies work more reliably on imports but at the cost of lower growth and higher unemployment. The more effective choice depends on the cause of the deficit.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: switching (devaluation, tariffs, quotas, export subsidies) changes relative prices so domestic and foreign buyers move to domestic goods; reducing (contractionary fiscal and monetary policy) lowers income and therefore imports; diagrams of AD shifts where useful. Evaluation: switching may fail if demand for imports and exports is price inelastic, if devaluation raises import costs and inflation, or if tariffs bring retaliation/break trade agreements; reducing works whatever the elasticities but creates unemployment and may harm investment; if the deficit is due to excess demand, reducing is appropriate; if due to low competitiveness, switching plus supply-side policy is better; justified conclusion.
Q13Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 14 marks

Invented data. Country V devalued its currency by 15 % at the end of Quarter 0. Its trade balance in goods and services ($bn) was: Q0 −4.0; Q1 −4.8; Q2 −5.1; Q3 −4.2; Q4 −3.0; Q5 −1.9.

  1. (a) Calculate the change in the trade balance between Quarter 0 and Quarter 2, and between Quarter 0 and Quarter 5. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the J-curve effect accounts for the pattern in the data. [4]
  3. (c) Using the data, evaluate whether the devaluation has been successful in correcting the trade deficit. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q0 to Q2: worsened by $1.1bn; Q0 to Q5: improved by $2.1bn
    −5.1 − (−4.0) = −1.1; −1.9 − (−4.0) = +2.1. 1 each.
  2. (b) Straight after a devaluation, trade volumes respond slowly because contracts are fixed and demand is price inelastic in the short run, so higher import prices raise import spending and the deficit widens (Q1–Q2). Over time buyers switch to the cheaper exports and away from dearer imports; once the Marshall–Lerner condition holds the balance improves (Q3–Q5).
    1 short-run inelastic demand/contracts; 1 deterioration explained; 1 elasticities rise over time; 1 Marshall–Lerner condition met → improvement.
  3. (c) Partly: after five quarters the deficit is less than half its original size, suggesting expenditure-switching is working. But the deficit has not been eliminated; other factors (world demand, oil prices, a domestic slowdown) may explain the change; devaluation raises import prices and inflation, which may erode competitiveness; costs to consumers and debt in foreign currency. Longer data and data on inflation and GDP are needed.
    Up to 4 for analysis using the data (improvement after an initial worsening, size of change, J-curve); up to 4 for evaluation (other causes, inflation erodes the effect, deficit remains, side effects, short time period) with a justified judgement.
Q14Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether supply-side policies are the best way for a country such as Sri Lanka to correct a persistent current account deficit. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Supply-side policies (education, infrastructure, export diversification, productivity) raise competitiveness and export capacity and reduce dependence on imports, tackling the root cause without cutting output. But they take years, are costly when public finances are weak, and results are uncertain. In a crisis, expenditure-reducing (tighter fiscal/monetary policy) and expenditure-switching (depreciation) policies act faster. A combination is likely best.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides, diagram where relevant, and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is only asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Content: causes of persistent deficits (low competitiveness, narrow export base, high import dependence for fuel/food); how supply-side policy shifts LRAS and lowers costs; alternatives and how they work. Evaluation: time lags, cost and financing, IMF conditions, inflation side-effects of depreciation, recession side-effects of austerity; judgement on 'best'.
Q15Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that a country with a persistent current account deficit should allow its currency to depreciate rather than use contractionary fiscal and monetary policy. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Depreciation switches expenditure towards domestic output without reducing total demand, preserving jobs, and can work if the Marshall–Lerner condition holds (after the J-curve). But it raises import prices and inflation, increases the cost of foreign-currency debt and may be eroded by wage rises. Contractionary policy cuts imports through lower incomes but at a cost in output and jobs; it also curbs inflation. The best choice depends on elasticities, the cause of the deficit, inflation, debt and the economy's cycle; supply-side policy is needed for a lasting cure.
    Mark bands (20): AO1/AO2 knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 — accurate, developed chains of reasoning with an accurate, correctly labelled diagram where relevant; AO3 evaluation up to 6 — weighs both sides, considers context and assumptions, and reaches a justified conclusion that answers the question. One-sided answers are capped at mid Level 2. Content: expenditure-switching vs expenditure-reducing; Marshall–Lerner and J-curve; AD/AS effects of each; interest rates and capital flows. Evaluation: elasticities in the short and long run, import dependence (fuel, inputs), inflation pass-through, foreign debt, fixed vs floating regime, unemployment costs, whether the deficit reflects high investment; justified conclusion.

Q22Exchange rates · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a managed float exchange rate system works, and consider whether it is better than a fixed exchange rate for a small open economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a managed float, market forces set the rate but the central bank intervenes (buying or selling currency, changing interest rates) to limit large or unwanted movements. It combines some flexibility with some stability, but needs reserves and may lack credibility; a fixed rate gives more certainty but removes an adjustment tool.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of managed float and fixed exchange rate; tools of intervention (foreign currency reserves, interest rates). Analysis: if the currency falls too fast, the central bank sells foreign currency and buys domestic currency, raising demand (diagram); it can let the rate adjust gradually to shocks, helping the current account; under a fixed rate the government must defend the peg whatever happens. Evaluation: for a small open economy heavily dependent on trade, stability of a fixed rate helps traders and controls imported inflation, but defending it can exhaust reserves and requires interest rates to follow the anchor country; a managed float keeps monetary independence but may cause uncertainty and speculative pressure; judgement.

Invented data. A country can import any quantity of a good at the world price of $8. Without a tariff, domestic demand is 900,000 units and domestic supply 300,000 units. The government imposes a tariff of $2 per unit. At the new price of $10, domestic demand is 800,000 units and domestic supply 420,000 units. Assume the demand and supply curves are straight lines.

  1. (a) Calculate the quantity of imports before and after the tariff. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the government's tariff revenue. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the loss of consumer surplus and the gain in producer surplus caused by the tariff. [3]
  4. (d) Calculate the net welfare loss to the economy, and explain why it arises. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before 600,000 units; after 380,000 units
    900 − 300 = 600 thousand; 800 − 420 = 380 thousand. 1 each.
  2. (b) $760,000
    $2 × 380,000 = $760,000.
  3. (c) Consumer surplus falls by $1,700,000; producer surplus rises by $720,000
    M1 area between the two prices: ΔCS = 2 × (900 + 800) ÷ 2 = 1,700 thousand; A1; B1 ΔPS = 2 × (300 + 420) ÷ 2 = 720 thousand.
  4. (d) $220,000; less efficient domestic producers replace cheaper imports and consumers who valued the good above $8 no longer buy it.
    1 1,700 − 720 − 760 = 220 thousand; 1 production and consumption inefficiency explained.
Q88Protectionism · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a production subsidy and an import tariff each protect a domestic industry, and consider which is less costly to consumers. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) A tariff raises the domestic price of the good, so domestic output rises, imports fall and consumers pay more and buy less (loss of consumer surplus). A subsidy lowers domestic producers' costs, increasing their output at the world price; consumers still pay the world price, so it is less costly to them directly. But a subsidy is paid from taxes (opportunity cost of government spending) whereas a tariff raises revenue.
    Mark split (8): knowledge 3, analysis 3, evaluation 2. K: definitions and diagrams (world price line, domestic supply shift). A: effects on price, quantity, imports, producers, consumers, government budget. E: consumers as taxpayers fund the subsidy; deadweight losses compared; WTO rules; ease of administration in developing countries; judgement.
Q89Protectionism · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a country gains from imposing tariffs on imports from a trading partner that has already raised tariffs on its exports. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Retaliation may protect domestic jobs, raise revenue and act as a bargaining tool to get the partner to remove its tariffs. But it raises prices for consumers and import-using firms, reduces efficiency, and may escalate into a trade war, cutting trade and growth for both. Outcome depends on relative size and bargaining power, elasticities and WTO dispute processes.
    Mark bands (12): Level 3 (9–12) accurate, well-developed analysis of both sides, diagram where relevant, and a justified judgement that answers the question; Level 2 (5–8) sound but partly undeveloped analysis, or evaluation that is only asserted; Level 1 (1–4) descriptive points with little analysis. A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3. Content: tariff diagram; effects on consumers, producers, government; retaliation and game-theory reasoning (both worse off if both impose). Evaluation: large vs small country (terms of trade gain), credibility of threat, supply-chain effects, legal alternatives (WTO); justified conclusion.
Q90Protectionism · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that protectionism by high-income countries is the main barrier to development in low-income countries. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Tariffs and quotas on farm goods and textiles, agricultural subsidies, tariff escalation on processed goods and strict standards limit low-income countries' exports, earnings and industrialisation. But other barriers may matter more: weak infrastructure and institutions, low human capital, debt, conflict, corruption, narrow export base and their own protectionism. Preferential access schemes show that market access alone does not guarantee development.
    Mark bands (20): AO1/AO2 knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 — accurate, developed chains of reasoning with an accurate, correctly labelled diagram where relevant; AO3 evaluation up to 6 — weighs both sides, considers context and assumptions, and reaches a justified conclusion that answers the question. One-sided answers are capped at mid Level 2. Content: forms of protection used by high-income countries and their effects (tariff diagram, lost export revenue, world price depression from subsidised exports). Evaluation: relative importance of domestic constraints, aid and FDI, trade preferences, examples of successful exporters despite barriers, differing effects by country; justified conclusion.
