Q1Policies to correct current account imbalances · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which policy is an expenditure-switching policy to reduce a current account deficit? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a devaluation of the country's currency
    A devaluation makes imports dearer and exports cheaper, switching spending by both residents and foreigners towards domestically produced goods. B, C and D are expenditure-reducing: they cut total spending, including spending on imports.
Q2Policies to correct current account imbalances · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A North African country has a current account deficit equal to 5 % of its GDP, mainly because of high imports of consumer goods.

  1. (a) State two expenditure-reducing policies a government could use to reduce a current account deficit. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a fall in national income is likely to reduce spending on imports. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: higher income tax or sales tax; lower government spending; higher interest rates; a reduction in the money supply/credit.
    1 mark each for any two (contractionary fiscal or monetary measures).
  2. (b) With lower incomes households and firms spend less in total, and part of every unit of spending goes on imports (the marginal propensity to import), so imports fall.
    1 mark: lower income → lower total spending/consumption; 1 mark: some of that spending is on imported goods (imports depend on income, normal goods), so import spending falls.
Q3Policies to correct current account imbalances · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Extract (invented for practice): A South Asian country's imports of mobile phones rose to US$2.1 billion last year. Its current account deficit is 3.8 % of GDP. The government proposes a 25 % tariff on imported phones. Most local phone factories assemble phones from imported parts.

  1. (a) Distinguish between expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing policies. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether the proposed tariff on mobile phones is likely to reduce the current account deficit. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expenditure-switching policies change the relative price or availability of imports and domestic goods so spending moves towards domestic output (e.g. tariffs, devaluation); expenditure-reducing policies lower total spending in the economy, which reduces spending on imports (e.g. higher taxes or interest rates).
    1 mark for each correctly explained type (with or without an example).
  2. (b) It may reduce imports of phones if demand is price elastic and domestic substitutes exist, but if demand is inelastic and there are no local producers, spending on imports may fall only a little; retaliation and higher costs for businesses may also reduce exports.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: tariff raises the domestic price of imported phones → quantity of imports falls; switch to domestic assemblers; tariff revenue. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: effect depends on PED for phones (likely inelastic for a necessity), availability of domestic substitutes (domestic phones may use imported parts), retaliation against exports, smuggling, and it treats a symptom not the cause (low competitiveness).
Q4Policies to correct current account imbalances · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a rise in interest rates could reduce a current account deficit, and consider the possible costs of this policy for the domestic economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Higher interest rates reduce consumption and investment, so national income and spending on imports fall (expenditure-reducing); costs include lower growth and higher unemployment, and an appreciation that may hurt exporters.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: current account deficit; expenditure-reducing policy; meaning of interest rate rise. Analysis: higher cost of borrowing and reward for saving → lower C and I → lower national income → lower spending on imports; lower inflation may improve competitiveness over time. Evaluation: costs — slower growth, higher unemployment, lower investment reduces future capacity; inflows of hot money may cause appreciation, making exports less competitive and partly offsetting the effect; heavy cost for indebted households and firms; judgement on whether the cost is worth paying.
Q5Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Data (invented for practice): Before a 10 % devaluation, a country's exports were worth US$30 billion and its imports US$45 billion a year, both priced in US dollars. In the first year after the devaluation, the US dollar price of its exports fell by 10 % and export volumes rose by 4 %. The US dollar price of its imports was unchanged and import volumes fell by 3 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the export earnings and import spending, in US$ billion, one year after the devaluation, and state whether the trade deficit has become larger or smaller. Give your answers to two decimal places. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the trade deficit might become smaller over a longer period after the devaluation. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Exports US$28.08 bn; imports US$43.65 bn; the deficit rises from US$15.00 bn to US$15.57 bn, so it becomes larger.
    M1: exports = 30 × 0.90 × 1.04 = 28.08; M1: imports = 45 × 0.97 = 43.65; A1: deficit = 43.65 − 28.08 = 15.57, larger than 15.00.
  2. (b) In the short run demand for exports and imports is price inelastic because contracts are fixed and buyers take time to find substitutes; over time demand becomes more elastic, export volumes rise more and import volumes fall more, so earnings rise and the deficit falls (a J-curve pattern).
    1 mark: short-run demand is inelastic (contracts, habits, lack of substitutes); 1 mark: over time PED for exports and imports rises as buyers adjust; 1 mark: when the combined responsiveness is large enough, export earnings rise relative to import spending and the deficit narrows (J-curve).
Q6Policies to correct current account imbalances · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether expenditure-switching policies are more effective than expenditure-reducing policies in correcting a current account deficit. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Expenditure-switching avoids a deliberate fall in national income and targets competitiveness, but depends on price elasticities, retaliation and inflation; expenditure-reducing policies work more reliably on imports but at the cost of lower growth and higher unemployment. The more effective choice depends on the cause of the deficit.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: switching (devaluation, tariffs, quotas, export subsidies) changes relative prices so domestic and foreign buyers move to domestic goods; reducing (contractionary fiscal and monetary policy) lowers income and therefore imports; diagrams of AD shifts where useful. Evaluation: switching may fail if demand for imports and exports is price inelastic, if devaluation raises import costs and inflation, or if tariffs bring retaliation/break trade agreements; reducing works whatever the elasticities but creates unemployment and may harm investment; if the deficit is due to excess demand, reducing is appropriate; if due to low competitiveness, switching plus supply-side policy is better; justified conclusion.
Q7Exchange rates · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change would most likely cause a country's floating exchange rate to appreciate? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a rise in its interest rates relative to those in other countries
    Higher relative interest rates attract financial inflows, raising demand for the currency, so it appreciates. B, C and D each raise the supply of the currency or reduce demand for it, causing depreciation.
Q8Exchange rates · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a floating exchange rate and a fixed exchange rate. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a large increase in the number of foreign tourists visiting Thailand would affect the value of the Thai baht in a floating exchange rate system. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A floating rate is determined by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market with no government target; a fixed rate is set by the government or central bank at a chosen value and maintained by intervention (buying/selling currency, interest rates).
    1 mark for each correct description.
  2. (b) Tourists must buy baht to pay for hotels and services, so demand for the baht rises; the demand curve shifts to the right while supply is unchanged; the equilibrium exchange rate rises, so the baht appreciates (and the quantity traded increases).
    1 mark: tourists need baht → demand for baht increases; 1 mark: demand curve shifts right (axes: price of baht in foreign currency, quantity of baht); 1 mark: excess demand at the old rate; 1 mark: new higher equilibrium rate — appreciation.
Q9Exchange rates · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The exchange rate changes from US$1 = PKR 280 to US$1 = PKR 300. A Pakistani exporter keeps the rupee price of a shirt at PKR 4200. What has happened? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A The rupee has depreciated and the US dollar price of the shirt has fallen from $15.00 to $14.00.
    More rupees are needed to buy one dollar, so the rupee has depreciated. Dollar price = 4200 ÷ 280 = $15.00 before and 4200 ÷ 300 = $14.00 after. B gives the right prices but the wrong direction for the rupee.
Q10Exchange rates · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a managed float exchange rate system works, and consider whether it is better than a fixed exchange rate for a small open economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a managed float, market forces set the rate but the central bank intervenes (buying or selling currency, changing interest rates) to limit large or unwanted movements. It combines some flexibility with some stability, but needs reserves and may lack credibility; a fixed rate gives more certainty but removes an adjustment tool.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of managed float and fixed exchange rate; tools of intervention (foreign currency reserves, interest rates). Analysis: if the currency falls too fast, the central bank sells foreign currency and buys domestic currency, raising demand (diagram); it can let the rate adjust gradually to shocks, helping the current account; under a fixed rate the government must defend the peg whatever happens. Evaluation: for a small open economy heavily dependent on trade, stability of a fixed rate helps traders and controls imported inflation, but defending it can exhaust reserves and requires interest rates to follow the anchor country; a managed float keeps monetary independence but may cause uncertainty and speculative pressure; judgement.
Q11Exchange rates · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 7: Exchange rate of the Country Q dollar (data invented for practice)January: US$1 = 160 Q-dollarsDecember: US$1 = 184 Q-dollars

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in the value of the Country Q dollar measured in US dollars between January and December. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Consider how the change in the exchange rate is likely to affect inflation in Country Q. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) −13.0 % (a depreciation of about 13.0 %)
    M1: value of one Q-dollar in US$ = 1 ÷ 160 = 0.006250 in January and 1 ÷ 184 = 0.005435 in December. A1: (0.005435 − 0.006250) ÷ 0.006250 × 100 = −13.0 %. (The 15 % rise in Q-dollars per US$ is not the same as a 15 % fall in the Q-dollar.)
  2. (b) Inflation is likely to rise: imported goods, fuel and raw materials cost more in Q-dollars (cost-push), and cheaper exports raise net exports and AD (demand-pull). The size depends on the import content of consumption, how much firms pass on and whether the depreciation is expected to last.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: higher domestic price of imported consumer goods → CPI rises directly; dearer imported inputs → higher costs → AS shifts left (cost-push); exports become cheaper abroad → higher net exports → AD rises (demand-pull); possible wage-price spiral. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: effect depends on the share of imports in spending, PED for imports, profit margins absorbing costs, contracts priced earlier, and the central bank's response.
Q12Exchange rates · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a depreciation of a country's currency is always beneficial for its economy. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) No: a depreciation can raise exports, output and employment and help a current account deficit, but it also raises import prices and inflation, increases the burden of foreign-currency debt and reduces living standards; its benefits depend on elasticities, spare capacity and the cause of the fall.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home → net exports and AD rise → growth and jobs (AD/AS); current account may improve if demand is price elastic enough; competitiveness improves. Costs: imported inflation, higher costs for firms using imported inputs, lower real incomes, larger burden of debt in foreign currency, possible capital flight. Evaluation: depends on price elasticities (short-run J-curve), spare capacity, import content of exports, size of foreign debt, whether trading partners are growing, and whether the depreciation is gradual or a sudden crisis; 'always' is too strong; justified conclusion.
Q13Terms of trade · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Define the terms of trade. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the terms of trade index for the year when the export price index is 108 and the import price index is 90. [2]
  3. (c) Explain what this index value shows about the country's position compared with the base year. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The ratio of a country's index of export prices to its index of import prices, multiplied by 100.
    1 mark: export price index ÷ import price index × 100 (or the quantity of imports that a unit of exports can buy).
  2. (b) 120
    M1: 108 ÷ 90 × 100; A1: 120.
  3. (c) The terms of trade have improved by 20 %: each unit of exports now buys 20 % more imports than in the base year.
    1 mark: an improvement (index above 100); 1 mark: a given quantity of exports pays for more imports.
Q14Terms of trade · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Table 8: Price indices for Country C, a cocoa exporter (2023 = 100; data invented for practice)2023: export prices 100, import prices 1002024: export prices 120, import prices 1052025: export prices 96, import prices 112Extract: After a record harvest in other producing countries, world cocoa prices fell sharply in 2025. Country C imports most of its fuel and machinery.

  1. (a) Calculate the terms of trade index for 2024 and 2025. Give your answers to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Using the extract, explain one likely cause of the change in the terms of trade between 2024 and 2025. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2024: 114.3; 2025: 85.7
    2024: 120 ÷ 105 × 100 = 114.3 (1); 2025: 96 ÷ 112 × 100 = 85.7 (1).
  2. (b) The world price of cocoa, the main export, fell (export prices index from 120 to 96) while import prices of fuel and machinery kept rising, so the terms of trade deteriorated.

Q15Terms of trade · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Over a year, a country's index of export prices rises from 100 to 110 and its index of import prices rises from 100 to 125. What is its terms of trade index at the end of the year? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 88.0
    Terms of trade = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100 = 110 ÷ 125 × 100 = 88.0: a deterioration. B inverts the ratio (125 ÷ 110 × 100).
Q16Terms of trade · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a large rise in world oil prices would affect the terms of trade of an oil-importing country such as Pakistan. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether this change in the terms of trade will reduce living standards in the country. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Oil is a large part of its imports, so the import price index rises relative to the export price index and the terms of trade deteriorate (fall).
    1 mark: import prices rise; 1 mark: index falls/deteriorates (each unit of exports buys fewer imports).
  2. (b) Likely yes in the short run: more exports are needed to pay for the same imports, real incomes fall, fuel and transport costs raise inflation and the current account deficit widens; the effect may be smaller if demand shifts to other energy sources, if the price rise is temporary, or if export prices also rise.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: deterioration means more resources must be exported to buy the same oil; higher import bill → current account worsens/currency may depreciate; higher fuel prices → cost-push inflation → real incomes fall; firms' costs rise → output and jobs may fall. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: depends on PED for oil (inelastic → larger import bill), duration of the shock, government subsidies, rising remittances from oil-exporting Gulf countries, and whether export prices rise too.
Q17Terms of trade · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 9 marks

Data (invented for practice): A garment-exporting country lowered its export prices to win orders. Between 2024 and 2025 its export price index fell from 100 to 95, its import price index rose from 100 to 104, and the volume of its exports rose by 15 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the terms of trade index for 2025. Give your answer to one decimal place. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in the value of the country's export earnings between 2024 and 2025. Give your answer to two decimal places. [2]
  3. (c) Assess whether the deterioration in the terms of trade is harmful to this country. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 91.3
    95 ÷ 104 × 100 = 91.3 (1).
  2. (b) +9.25 %
    M1: value index = price index × volume index ÷ 100 = 95 × 115 ÷ 100 = 109.25. A1: export earnings rose by 9.25 %.
  3. (c) Not necessarily: export prices fell, but because demand for its exports is price elastic, export volumes rose by more and export earnings rose; the fall in the terms of trade may reflect greater competitiveness. It would be harmful if caused by rising import prices or falling world demand.
    Up to 4 marks for analysis: a deterioration means each unit of exports buys fewer imports; but the cause matters — here export prices fell as firms became more competitive and volumes rose 15 %, so earnings rose (calculation), helping output, jobs and the current account; if import prices rise (e.g. oil), real income falls. Up to 2 marks for evaluation: depends on the cause of the change, PED for exports and imports, the import bill (import prices rose 4 %), whether the gain is sustained; justified judgement.
Q18Terms of trade · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a country that depends on exporting primary products will always benefit from a rise in world commodity prices. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) A rise in commodity prices improves its terms of trade and usually raises export earnings, incomes and government revenue, but benefits may be temporary, unevenly shared and may cause appreciation that harms other exporters; it does not always benefit.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: higher export prices → terms of trade improve → more imports per unit of exports; export revenue rises if demand is price inelastic; current account improves; higher incomes, employment and tax revenue (royalties) → growth; supply of exports may expand. Evaluation: the gain depends on whether the price rise reflects higher world demand or a supply shortage at home (poor harvest — lower volumes); commodity prices are volatile, so dependence increases instability; currency appreciation can harm manufacturing and tourism; revenue may go to a few firms or MNCs; rising import prices may offset; it may discourage diversification; justified conclusion on 'always'.
Q19Reasons for trade and comparative advantage · Core · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between absolute advantage and comparative advantage, and consider whether free trade benefits all countries. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Absolute advantage is producing more of a good with the same resources; comparative advantage is producing at a lower opportunity cost. Trade based on comparative advantage can raise total output and welfare, but not every country or group gains equally.
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definitions of absolute and comparative advantage, opportunity cost, free trade. Analysis: a numerical or worked example showing that specialisation by comparative advantage raises total world output even when one country has absolute advantage in both goods; gains shared through the terms of trade; consumers gain from lower prices and more choice. Evaluation: gains depend on the terms of trade, transport costs and factor mobility; some industries and workers lose jobs; countries specialising in primary products face volatile prices; infant industries may never develop; benefits are not automatic for all; judgement.
Q20Reasons for trade and comparative advantage · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) One worker in Country X can produce either 10 tonnes of rice or 5 units of cloth. One worker in Country Y can produce either 6 tonnes of rice or 4 units of cloth. Which statement is correct? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Country X has an absolute advantage in both goods, but Country Y has a comparative advantage in cloth.
    X produces more of both goods per worker (absolute advantage in both). Opportunity cost of 1 unit of cloth: X = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 tonnes of rice; Y = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 tonnes of rice. Y gives up less rice, so it has the comparative advantage in cloth (and X in rice). D ignores that gains come from differences in opportunity cost.
Q21Reasons for trade and comparative advantage · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Table 9: Output per worker per week (data invented for practice)Country A: 8 tonnes of wheat or 24 units of textilesCountry B: 6 tonnes of wheat or 12 units of textiles

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of producing 1 unit of wheat in each country. [2]
  2. (b) Identify which good each country should specialise in. Explain your answer. [2]
  3. (c) State a rate of exchange of textiles for wheat at which both countries could gain from trade. [1]
  4. (d) Explain one reason why the gains from specialisation might be smaller in practice than the theory suggests. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Country A: 3 units of textiles; Country B: 2 units of textiles.
    24 ÷ 8 = 3 textiles (1); 12 ÷ 6 = 2 textiles (1).
  2. (b) Country B should specialise in wheat (lower opportunity cost: 2 textiles vs 3); Country A should specialise in textiles (opportunity cost 1/3 tonne of wheat vs 1/2 in B).
    1 mark: B wheat, A textiles; 1 mark: justified by lower opportunity cost (comparative advantage).
  3. (c) Any rate strictly between 2 and 3 units of textiles per tonne of wheat, e.g. 2.5 units of textiles for 1 tonne of wheat.
    1 mark: a rate between the two opportunity cost ratios (2 < rate < 3).
  4. (d) For example, transport costs may use up the gain; or factors are not perfectly mobile between industries, so workers cannot easily move from wheat to textiles; or opportunity costs rise as production expands (increasing costs).
    1 mark: identifies a valid limitation (transport costs, factor immobility, increasing costs, trade barriers, exchange rates); 1 mark: explains how it reduces the gain.
Q22Reasons for trade and comparative advantage · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether specialising according to comparative advantage is the best trade strategy for a low-income country. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Specialisation by comparative advantage raises efficiency and income in the short run, but a low-income country's current comparative advantage is often in primary products with volatile prices and falling terms of trade; diversification and developing new advantages may be better long-run strategies.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: comparative advantage theory; gains from specialisation and trade (higher output, economies of scale, foreign currency earnings, access to imported capital goods); compare with alternatives such as diversification, protection of infant industries, regional trade agreements. Evaluation: risks of over-specialisation (price volatility, weather, exhaustion of resources, deteriorating terms of trade); comparative advantage can change over time through investment in skills and technology (dynamic comparative advantage); protection may lead to inefficiency and retaliation; examples (e.g. garment exports, cocoa, copper); justified conclusion on 'best'.
Q23Current account of the balance of payments · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which transaction is recorded as a credit in the secondary income section of Pakistan's current account? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A money sent home by Pakistani workers employed in Saudi Arabia
    Workers' remittances are current transfers and are recorded in secondary income. B is primary income (a debit), C is foreign direct investment (financial account), D is trade in goods.
Q24Current account of the balance of payments · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the four components of the current account of the balance of payments. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why a country might have a large surplus on its current account. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income, secondary income.
    2 marks for all four; 1 mark for two or three.
  2. (b) For example: strong international competitiveness (high productivity, quality, low costs) so exports far exceed imports; high domestic saving and low consumption, so spending on imports is low; an undervalued exchange rate; large exports of a high-priced commodity such as oil.
    For each of two reasons: 1 mark for identification and 1 mark for explaining how it raises exports relative to imports (or income inflows relative to outflows).
Q25Current account of the balance of payments · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Table 10: Current account of Country N, 2025 (US$ billion; data invented for practice)Trade in goods: −27.5Trade in services: −2.9Primary income: −8.1Secondary income: +31.2

  1. (a) Calculate the current account balance for 2025. [2]
  2. (b) Consider whether a current account deficit of this kind is a problem for the country. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A deficit of US$7.3 billion
    M1: −27.5 − 2.9 − 8.1 + 31.2; A1: = −7.3, i.e. a deficit of US$7.3 billion.
  2. (b) It may be: it must be financed by borrowing or by selling assets, and the large deficit on goods shows weak competitiveness. But the deficit is small relative to the trade gap because of large remittances, and if imports are capital goods it may support future growth.
    Up to 3 marks for analysis: the deficit must be financed through the financial account (borrowing, running down reserves, FDI); persistent deficits may lead to depreciation and higher debt; large goods deficit suggests low competitiveness and reliance on remittances; primary income deficit reflects interest and profit payments abroad. Up to 1 mark for evaluation: depends on the size relative to GDP, how it is financed (FDI vs short-term loans), what is imported (capital goods vs consumer goods), and whether remittances are stable.
Q26Current account of the balance of payments · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether a persistent current account deficit is always harmful to an economy. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always: a deficit financed by long-term FDI and used to import capital goods can raise future growth, but a persistent deficit financed by short-term borrowing or reserves can lead to debt problems, currency crises and loss of jobs in import-competing industries.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: meaning of a current account deficit and how it is financed (financial account); harms: lower AD (net exports negative), job losses in exporting and import-competing industries, rising foreign debt and interest payments (primary income outflows), pressure on the exchange rate, loss of reserves, possible need for contractionary policy. Possible benefits: imports of capital goods raise productivity; consumers enjoy higher living standards; deficit may reflect strong growth and inward investment. Evaluation: depends on size relative to GDP, cause (strong demand vs weak competitiveness), type of financing, what is imported, and the exchange rate system; justified conclusion on 'always'.
Q27Protectionism · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A Central Asian government is considering whether to protect its new solar-panel industry from imports.

  1. (a) Distinguish between a tariff and an import quota. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the infant industry argument for protection. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A tariff is a tax on imported goods, raising their price; a quota is a physical limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported.
    1 mark for each correct definition.
  2. (b) A new domestic industry has high average costs because it cannot yet benefit from economies of scale or experience, so it cannot compete with established foreign firms; temporary protection lets it grow until it becomes competitive.
    1 mark: new firms have high costs/lack economies of scale and experience; 1 mark: temporary protection until it can compete, after which it is removed.
Q28Protectionism · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The world price of a good is $200 and a country can import any quantity at this price. Before a tariff, domestic demand is 70 000 units and domestic supply is 20 000 units. The government imposes a 15 % tariff. Domestic demand falls to 64 000 units and domestic supply rises to 26 000 units. What is the government's tariff revenue? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A $1 140 000
    Tariff per unit = 15 % × $200 = $30. Imports after the tariff = 64 000 − 26 000 = 38 000 units. Revenue = 38 000 × $30 = $1,140,000. B uses the imports before the tariff (50 000).
Q29Protectionism · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how an import quota affects domestic consumers and domestic producers of a good, and consider whether the gains to producers are greater than the losses to consumers. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) A quota reduces supply in the domestic market, so price rises; producers sell more at a higher price and gain producer surplus, but consumers pay more and buy less, losing consumer surplus. Consumer losses exceed producer gains because of deadweight losses, and unlike a tariff no revenue goes to the government (the quota rent goes to licence holders).
    Mark bands (8): knowledge and understanding up to 3, analysis up to 3, evaluation up to 2. Knowledge: definition of quota; consumer and producer surplus. Analysis: diagram with world price, domestic demand and supply; quota limits imports so domestic price rises above world price; domestic output rises, consumption falls; producer surplus increases, consumer surplus falls; quota rent to importers/foreign exporters; two deadweight loss triangles (production inefficiency and lost consumption). Evaluation: in standard analysis consumers lose more than producers gain; but there may be wider benefits — jobs saved, strategic industry — and the size depends on elasticities and the quota level; judgement.
Q30Protectionism · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks
  1. (a) Assess whether protectionism is justified to protect jobs in a developing country's car industry. [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Protection may save jobs in the short run and allow the industry to develop, but it raises prices for consumers and firms, protects inefficiency, invites retaliation and may cost jobs elsewhere; it is justified only if temporary and linked to a credible plan to become competitive.
    Mark bands (12): analysis up to 8 (knowledge applied, clear chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 4 (weighed judgement that answers the question). Analysis: tariffs/quotas raise import prices → demand shifts to domestically produced cars → output and employment rise in the car industry and its suppliers; infant industry and strategic arguments; tariff revenue. Costs: higher prices for consumers and businesses using vehicles; lower consumer surplus and deadweight loss; reduced incentive to cut costs; retaliation against the country's exports may cost jobs elsewhere; breaches of trade agreements. Evaluation: whether protection is temporary and conditional on productivity gains; size of the domestic market (economies of scale); alternatives such as training or attracting FDI; experiences of protected car industries that stayed uncompetitive; justified conclusion.
