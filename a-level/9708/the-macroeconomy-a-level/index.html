Q1Money and banking · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In Keynes's liquidity preference theory, which motive for holding money is most sensitive to changes in the rate of interest? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A the speculative motive
    Speculative balances are held instead of bonds; when interest rates are high, bonds are attractive and the opportunity cost of holding money is high, so speculative demand falls. Transactions demand (B) depends mainly on income.
Q2Money and banking · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A central bank in South Asia has raised its policy rate three times this year and has also lent emergency funds to a small commercial bank.

  1. (a) Distinguish between narrow money and broad money. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two functions of a central bank. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Narrow money is money that can be spent immediately: notes and coins (plus banks' reserves at the central bank or sight deposits, depending on the measure); broad money also includes less liquid bank and savings deposits that can be turned into spending power.
    1 mark: narrow = notes, coins/highly liquid; 1 mark: broad = narrow money plus time/savings deposits (less liquid).
  2. (b) Any two, e.g.: carries out monetary policy (sets the policy interest rate, controls the money supply) to meet an inflation target; acts as lender of last resort to commercial banks in a crisis; is banker to the government (manages its accounts and borrowing); supervises and regulates banks; issues notes and coins; manages foreign exchange reserves and the exchange rate.
    For each of two functions: 1 mark for identification, 1 mark for explaining what it involves or why it matters.

Market traders, taxi drivers and utility companies now accept wallet payments, and the share of payments made in cash has fallen from 70 % to 40 %.

  1. (a) Explain two characteristics of money that a digital wallet balance must have if it is to function as money. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how a shift from cash to digital payments could affect commercial banks' ability to create credit. [3]
Show answer

(a) For example: general acceptability — shops and people must be willing to accept it in payment; divisibility — it can be used for small and large amounts; durability/security — balances cannot be destroyed or easily forged; portability — payments can be made anywhere; limited supply/stable value so it holds purchasing power.
    For each of two characteristics: 1 mark for naming it, 1 mark for applying it to digital wallet balances.
  2. (b) Less cash is withdrawn and held outside the banking system, so the cash leakage falls; more deposits stay with banks and are available as reserves, so the credit multiplier and banks' ability to lend increase (subject to demand for loans and regulation).

Q5Money and banking · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the transactions and precautionary motives for holding money. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how each of these motives is likely to change when national income rises. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Transactions motive: money held to pay for day-to-day purchases between receiving income; precautionary motive: money held to meet unexpected expenses or emergencies.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Both rise: higher incomes mean more spending, so more money is needed for transactions, and people hold larger reserves for emergencies in proportion to their spending.
    1 mark: transactions demand rises with income/spending; 1 mark: precautionary demand rises with income.

  1. (a) Commercial banks keep 8 % of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. A customer deposits $40 million of new cash. Assuming no cash leaks out of the banking system, what is the maximum total increase in bank deposits? [1]

  1. (a) Calculate the new loans made in each of the first three rounds of lending, starting with Bank A. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the maximum total increase in bank deposits that could result from the original deposit. [1]

Q8Money and banking · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the extent to which commercial banks' ability to create credit is limited. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Banks can create deposits by lending a multiple of their reserves, but their ability is limited by reserve and capital requirements, cash leakages, the demand for loans, the supply of creditworthy borrowers, central bank policy and confidence. The extent of the limit varies over the economic cycle.
    Mark bands (20): knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 (accurate theory, developed chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 6 (justified judgement on the actual question, with conditions). Analysis: functions of commercial banks; process of credit creation and the credit multiplier (1 ÷ reserve ratio) with a numerical example; liquidity vs profitability conflict; limits: required reserve/liquidity ratios, capital adequacy rules, cash leakage, banks' own prudence, demand for loans, interest rates set by the central bank, competition from non-bank lenders. Evaluation: in booms limits are weak and credit may expand rapidly (asset bubbles); in recessions demand for loans and bank confidence are the main limits even with ample reserves; financial innovation and digital payments reduce cash leakage; the multiplier model oversimplifies modern banking; justified judgement on 'extent'.
Q9Money and banking · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Data (invented for practice): in an economy the money supply (M) is $200 billion, the velocity of circulation (V) is 5 and the volume of transactions (T) is 1 000 billion units of output, where MV = PT.

  1. (a) Calculate the average price level (P). [1]
  2. (b) The money supply rises by 10 %. Calculate the new price level if V and T are unchanged, and the new price level if V falls to 4.5 at the same time. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why monetarists argue that V and T are broadly constant in the short run, and why Keynesians disagree. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.0
    P = MV ÷ T = 200 × 5 ÷ 1 000 = 1.0 (1).
  2. (b) 1.1 if V is unchanged; 0.99 if V falls to 4.5.
    1 mark: 220 × 5 ÷ 1 000 = 1.1; 1 mark: 220 × 4.5 ÷ 1 000 = 0.99.
  3. (c) Monetarists: V depends on slowly changing payment habits and institutions; T (real output) is determined by supply-side factors and tends towards full employment; so changes in M mainly change P. Keynesians: V is unstable – extra money may be held idle (speculative demand, liquidity trap) so V falls; when there is spare capacity, higher spending raises T (output) rather than P.
    1 mark: monetarist view of V; 1 mark: monetarist view of T; 1 mark: Keynesian view of V; 1 mark: Keynesian view of T (spare capacity).
Q10Money and banking · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Data (invented for practice): the assets of a commercial bank, as a percentage of total assets: cash in its vaults 5 %; balances at the central bank 3 %; short-term government bills 12 %; advances (loans) to customers 70 %; long-term investments 10 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of the bank's assets held in liquid form (cash, balances at the central bank and short-term bills). [1]
  2. (b) Explain the conflict between liquidity and profitability that a commercial bank faces. [4]
  3. (c) Explain how a rise in the bank's reserve requirement would affect its ability to create credit. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20 %
    5 + 3 + 12 = 20 % (1).
  2. (b) Liquid assets (cash, central bank balances, bills) can meet customer withdrawals quickly but earn little or no interest; advances (loans) earn high interest but cannot be turned into cash quickly and carry default risk. Holding more liquid assets reduces the risk of a run but lowers profit; lending more raises profit but increases liquidity and solvency risk.
    1 mark: liquid assets earn low returns; 1 mark: loans are profitable but illiquid/risky; 1 mark: need to meet withdrawals/confidence; 1 mark: trade-off explained.
  3. (c) The bank must hold a larger share of deposits as reserves, so it has less to lend; the credit multiplier (1 ÷ reserve ratio) falls, reducing deposit creation.
    1 mark: less excess reserves to lend; 1 mark: smaller credit multiplier.

Q12Money and banking · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the equilibrium rate of interest when the money supply is $360 billion. [2]
  2. (b) The central bank increases the money supply to $400 billion. Calculate the new equilibrium rate of interest. [1]
  3. (c) Explain what is meant by a liquidity trap and why, in a liquidity trap, a further increase in the money supply would not reduce the rate of interest. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6 %
    M1: 600 − 40r = 360 → 40r = 240; A1: r = 6 %.
  2. (b) 5 %

Q13Money and banking · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that controlling the money supply is the most effective way to control inflation. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Monetarists argue, using the quantity theory, that inflation is caused by excess money growth and is best controlled by limiting it; Keynesians argue that velocity and output are not stable, that money supply is hard to control, and that inflation has other causes (cost-push, demand from fiscal policy). Money supply control can be effective in some cases (hyperinflation, monetary financing of deficits) but is rarely the most effective tool on its own.
    Mark bands (20): knowledge, understanding and analysis up to 14 (accurate theory, developed chains of reasoning, diagram where useful), evaluation up to 6 (justified judgement on the actual question, with conditions). Analysis: MV = PT; monetarist assumptions (V and T fixed or stable) → P rises proportionately with M; transmission through interest rates and spending; Keynesian view — V varies, T can rise when there is spare capacity, money demand sensitive to interest rates; difficulty of controlling money supply when banks create credit; central banks now usually target interest rates; alternatives: interest rate policy, fiscal policy, supply-side policy, exchange rate policy. Evaluation: depends on the cause of inflation (excess money creation vs cost-push from oil or depreciation), on whether the economy is near full capacity, on central bank credibility; evidence that money and inflation are closely linked in the long run and in hyperinflations, weaker in the short run; justified conclusion.

Q15Money and banking · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that the loanable funds theory gives a better explanation of how the rate of interest is determined than the liquidity preference theory. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Loanable funds: interest rate is the price that balances the supply of savings (plus new credit) with the demand for borrowing for investment; a rise in thriftiness lowers interest; links to real factors (productivity of capital, time preference). Liquidity preference (Keynes): interest is the reward for parting with liquidity; set where money demand (transactions, precautionary, speculative) equals money supply fixed by the central bank; explains how monetary policy changes rates and the liquidity trap. Evaluation: loanable funds suits the long run and real factors; liquidity preference suits the short run and the central bank's role in setting policy rates; in practice central banks set a policy rate and banks create money endogenously, so neither theory is complete; risk premia and global capital flows also matter. Conclusion: depends on time period and institutional setting; the two can be seen as complementary.
    Top band: accurate definitions, a correct, fully explained diagram where relevant, developed chains of analysis on more than one side, and evaluation that weighs the arguments and reaches a justified conclusion that answers the question; mid band: sound but partly developed analysis with limited evaluation; low band: descriptive points or assertion. Expect: both theories explained with diagrams described in words (supply/demand for loanable funds; money demand curve and vertical money supply), what shifts each curve, and an evaluated judgement on 'better'.

Q17Economic growth and sustainability · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between actual and potential economic growth. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of potential economic growth. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Actual growth is an increase in real output (real GDP) produced; potential growth is an increase in the economy's productive capacity — the maximum output it could produce.
    1 mark for each correct definition.
  2. (b) For example: investment in capital goods increases the capital stock; improvements in technology raise productivity; education and training improve the quality of labour; a larger labour force (population growth, migration, higher participation); discovery of natural resources.

Show answer

(a) The original Phillips curve suggested a stable trade-off, but the expectations-augmented view argues that in the long run unemployment returns to the natural rate whatever the inflation rate, so there is no long-run trade-off; however, hysteresis, very low inflation and wage rigidity mean the long-run curve may not be perfectly vertical.

Q58Employment and unemployment · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that technological change will inevitably increase unemployment in the long run. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: automation and artificial intelligence replace routine manual and clerical jobs; structural unemployment where workers' skills do not match new jobs; occupational and geographical immobility; regions dependent on declining industries; in lower-income countries, labour-intensive manufacturing may shrink. Data (invented for practice): at the start of a year a country had 3.0 million unemployed people and a labour force of 40.0 million. During the year 1.2 million people became unemployed, 0.9 million unemployed people found jobs and 0.2 million unemployed people stopped looking for work and left the labour force. At the end of the year the labour force was 40.2 million.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of unemployed people at the end of the year. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the unemployment rate at the start and at the end of the year. Give the end-of-year rate to 2 decimal places. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why looking at the flows into and out of unemployment gives more information than looking at the unemployment rate alone. [3]
  4. (d) Evaluate whether a stable unemployment rate means that a country's labour market is working well. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.1 million
    3.0 + 1.2 − 0.9 − 0.2 = 3.1 million (1).
  2. (b) Start 7.5 %; end 7.71 %.
    1 mark: 3.0 ÷ 40 × 100 = 7.5 %; 1 mark: 3.1 ÷ 40.2 × 100 = 7.71 %.
  3. (c) The stock changed only slightly, but 1.2 million people became unemployed and 1.1 million left unemployment; high flows with short spells suggest frictional unemployment in a dynamic labour market, while low flows with a stable stock suggest long-term (structural) unemployment; flows also show how many leave the labour force (discouraged workers) rather than find jobs.
    1 mark: large flows hidden by small change in stock; 1 mark: duration/type of unemployment; 1 mark: outflows to inactivity vs jobs.
  4. (d) Possibly well: stable rate near the natural rate, steady flows into jobs, low long-term unemployment. Q60Employment and unemployment · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 14 marks

Data (invented for practice): a country's labour force is 30 million in both years.Year 1: unemployment rate 9 %; 20 % of the unemployed had been out of work for more than 12 monthsYear 5: unemployment rate 7 %; 38 % of the unemployed had been out of work for more than 12 months

  1. (a) Calculate the number of long-term unemployed people in Year 1 and in Year 5, and the percentage change. Give the percentage to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the rise in long-term unemployment may mean that the natural rate of unemployment has increased. [3]
  3. (c) Evaluate the policies a government could use to reduce long-term unemployment. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1: 540 000; Year 5: 798 000; an increase of 47.8 %.
    M1: unemployed 0.09 × 30 m = 2.7 m and 0.07 × 30 m = 2.1 m; M1: 0.2 × 2.7 m = 540 000 and 0.38 × 2.1 m = 798 000; A1: (798 000 − 540 000) ÷ 540 000 × 100 = 47.8 %.
  2. (b) Long-term unemployed lose skills and motivation and face employer discrimination, so they no longer compete effectively for jobs; they do not restrain wage demands, so inflation can rise at a higher unemployment rate (higher NAIRU); this is hysteresis – a temporary downturn has a lasting effect.
    1 mark: loss of employability; 1 mark: less downward pressure on wages/NAIRU; 1 mark: hysteresis explained.
  3. (c) Supply-side: retraining and education matched to vacancies; job-search support and subsidised employment (wage subsidies for hiring the long-term unemployed); relocation help; reforms to benefits so work pays. Demand-side: keeping AD high helps, but long-term unemployed may not be hired without other support. Evaluation: costs and time lags; deadweight (subsidies paid for people who would be hired anyway); effectiveness depends on cause (skills mismatch vs regional decline); the evidence that the overall rate fell suggests AD is not the main problem. Judgement: targeted active labour-market policies are likely to be most effective.
    Up to 3 marks: analysis of supply-side/active policies; up to 2 marks: role and limits of demand-side policy; up to 3 marks: evaluation and justified conclusion using the data.
