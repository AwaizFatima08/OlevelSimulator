Q1Economic development and living standards · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Invented data for a country in one year: GDP is $380 billion. Income earned in the country by foreign-owned firms and sent abroad is $20 billion. Income earned by the country's residents from assets they own abroad is $8 billion. The population is 240 million.

  1. (a) Calculate the country's gross national income (GNI). [2]
  2. (b) Calculate GNI per head. Give your answer to the nearest dollar. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why GNI per head is a better indicator than GDP per head of the income available to the residents of this country. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $368 billion
    GNI = GDP + net primary income from abroad (M1) = 380 − 20 + 8 = $368 billion (A1).
  2. (b) $1,533
    368,000 million ÷ 240 million = $1,533 (B1).
  3. (c) GNI excludes income that flows abroad to foreign owners (e.g. repatriated profits) and includes residents' income from abroad.
    1 mark: GDP measures output produced in the country, whoever owns it. 1 mark: $20 billion of income goes to foreign firms, so GDP overstates residents' income.
Q2Economic development and living standards · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which indicator is NOT used in calculating the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)?A years of schooling of household membersB child mortality in the householdC household income per personD access to clean drinking water [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C household income per person
    The MPI measures deprivation in health, education and living standards using non-income indicators (nutrition, child mortality, schooling, school attendance, cooking fuel, sanitation, water, electricity, housing, assets). Income is deliberately excluded. A, B and D are all MPI indicators.
Q3Economic development and living standards · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by the Measure of Economic Welfare (MEW) and why it might give a different view of living standards from real GDP per head. [4]
  2. (b) Explain one difficulty in calculating the MEW. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) MEW adjusts GDP by adding the value of non-market activities that raise welfare and subtracting activities that do not add to welfare.
    1 mark: MEW starts from GDP/GNP. 1 mark: adds value of leisure, household production (unpaid work) and the informal economy. 1 mark: subtracts 'regrettables' such as defence and commuting, and environmental damage. 1 mark: so two countries with the same GDP per head can have very different MEW, e.g. if one has longer working hours or more pollution.
  2. (b) Valuing non-market items (leisure, unpaid care, pollution) in money terms is subjective.
    1 mark difficulty; 1 mark explanation (choice of values / data availability makes comparisons unreliable).
Q4Economic development and living standards · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Invented population data for a low-income country: aged 0–14: 72 million; aged 15–64: 150 million; aged 65 and over: 9 million.

  1. (a) Calculate the dependency ratio (dependants per 100 people of working age). [2]
  2. (b) Explain two economic characteristics, other than population structure, commonly found in low-income countries. [4]
  3. (c) Explain how a fall in the birth rate could give this country a 'demographic dividend'. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 54.0
    (72 + 9) ÷ 150 × 100 (M1) = 54.0 (A1).
  2. (b) e.g. large share of employment in agriculture/primary sector; large informal sector; low savings and investment; low productivity; high dependence on primary exports.
    2 marks each for two explained characteristics.
  3. (c) The working-age share rises relative to dependants, raising output per head, savings and investment.
    1 mark: dependency ratio falls as fewer children are born. 1 mark: more workers per dependant can raise income per head and savings, if jobs are available.
Q5Economic development and living standards · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether HDI is a better measure of living standards than real GNI per head. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) HDI is broader because it includes health and education, but it still omits inequality, freedom and the environment; the better measure depends on the purpose.
    Analysis (up to 14): real GNI per head – adjusts for inflation and population, PPP allows comparison, measures material living standards; limitations – ignores distribution, informal sector, non-market output, leisure, environmental damage, quality of goods, exchange-rate issues. HDI – geometric mean of life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and GNI per head; reflects capabilities; shows countries with high income but poor health/education; limitations – only three dimensions, averages hide inequality (inequality-adjusted HDI exists), ignores freedom, security, environment; arbitrary weights and goalposts; slow to change. Other measures – MPI, MEW, happiness indices. Evaluation (up to 6): judgement – HDI generally a better indicator of overall living standards, but GNI still essential for income comparisons; use several indicators.
Q6Economic development and living standards · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

The Human Development Index uses these formulas:life expectancy index = (life expectancy − 20) ÷ (85 − 20)education index = average of (mean years of schooling ÷ 15) and (expected years of schooling ÷ 18)income index = (ln GNI per head − ln 100) ÷ (ln 75,000 − ln 100), with GNI per head in PPP$Invented data for a country: life expectancy 67.5 years; mean years of schooling 8.0; expected years of schooling 11.0; GNI per head PPP$5,800.

  1. (a) Calculate the life expectancy index, the education index and the income index. Give each to 3 decimal places. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the country's HDI as the geometric mean of the three indices. Give your answer to 3 decimal places. [2]
  3. (c) Identify which dimension contributes least to the country's HDI and suggest one policy that could raise it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Life expectancy index 0.731; education index 0.572; income index 0.613
    LEI = (67.5 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.731 (B1). EI = ½ × (8 ÷ 15 + 11 ÷ 18) = ½ × (0.533 + 0.611) = 0.572 (B1). II = ln(5800 ÷ 100) ÷ ln(75,000 ÷ 100) = ln 58 ÷ ln 750 = 0.613 (B1).
  2. (b) 0.635
    HDI = (0.7308 × 0.5722 × 0.6134)^(1/3) (M1) = 0.635 (A1).
  3. (c) Education (index 0.572); e.g. increase spending on schools, teacher training or conditional cash transfers for school attendance.
    1 mark education identified; 1 mark appropriate policy.
Q7Economic development and living standards · Challenge · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a year, a country's nominal GDP rises by 12 %, the general price level rises by 8 % and the population rises by 2 %. What is the approximate change in real GDP per head?A a rise of about 1.7 %B a rise of about 3.7 %C a rise of about 6.0 %D a rise of about 10.0 % [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a rise of about 1.7 %
    Real GDP growth = 1.12 ÷ 1.08 − 1 = 3.7 %; per head = 1.037 ÷ 1.02 − 1 ≈ 1.7 %. B ignores population growth; C adds population growth instead of subtracting it.
Q8Economic development and living standards · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 14 marks

Invented data for two countries.Country P: GNI per head (PPP$) 14,000; HDI 0.70; life expectancy 68 years; mean years of schooling 6.5; Gini coefficient 0.52; oil is 60 % of exportsCountry Q: GNI per head (PPP$) 9,000; HDI 0.76; life expectancy 74 years; mean years of schooling 10.0; Gini coefficient 0.34

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage by which Country P's GNI per head exceeds that of Country Q. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Using the data, explain why Country Q has a higher HDI than Country P despite its lower GNI per head. [4]
  3. (c) Evaluate whether HDI shows that living standards are higher in Country Q than in Country P. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) 55.6 %
    (14,000 − 9,000) ÷ 9,000 × 100 (M1) = 55.6 % (A1).
  2. (b) Q has better health and education outcomes, which raise the other two dimensions of the HDI.
    1 mark: HDI combines income, health and education. 1 mark: Q's life expectancy is 6 years higher. 1 mark: Q's mean years of schooling are 3.5 years higher. 1 mark: the higher health and education indices more than offset Q's lower income index; e.g. P's income may be concentrated in an oil sector with little spending on public services.
  3. (c) Probably, but HDI omits inequality, freedom, environment and other factors; the MPI and inequality data could give a different picture.
    Analysis (up to 6): HDI is broader than GNI per head and captures capabilities; but it is an average – Gini data show P is much more unequal (0.52 vs 0.34), so typical households in P may be worse off than the GNI figure suggests, strengthening the view that Q is better off; HDI ignores political freedom, environmental quality, working hours, housing, security; equal weighting of the three dimensions is arbitrary; data quality. Evaluation (up to 2): judgement, e.g. evidence supports higher living standards in Q, especially given the lower inequality, but a fuller view needs MPI and other indicators.
Q9Economic development and living standards · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that rapid population growth is the main obstacle to economic development in low-income countries. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rapid population growth can hold back income per head and strain services, but it can also provide a labour force and markets; institutions, investment and human capital may matter more.
    Analysis (up to 14): Malthusian view – population outpaces food and resources; diluted capital per worker; high dependency ratio; pressure on schools, health, housing; unemployment and urban slums; lower savings. Counter-arguments – demographic transition and demographic dividend when the working-age share rises; larger domestic markets and economies of scale; young, adaptable labour force; technology raises food output. Other obstacles – low savings and investment, poor infrastructure, weak institutions and corruption, debt, dependence on primary exports, conflict, low human capital, inequality. Evaluation (up to 6): population growth is a consequence as well as a cause of poverty (birth rates fall as incomes, education of women and health improve); 'main' obstacle requires a comparison and a reasoned judgement.
Q10Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between bilateral aid and multilateral aid. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why tied aid may bring fewer benefits to the receiving country than untied aid. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bilateral aid is given directly by one government to another; multilateral aid is channelled through international organisations such as the World Bank or UN agencies.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Tied aid must be spent on goods/services from the donor country, which may be more expensive or unsuitable, and does not support local producers.
    1 mark: definition of tied aid. 1 mark: recipient cannot buy from the cheapest supplier, so less value is obtained. 1 mark: goods may not suit local needs or use inappropriate technology. 1 mark: money flows back to donor firms rather than local businesses/jobs.

(a) Which is an example of foreign direct investment (FDI) in Pakistan?A a Pakistani worker in Dubai sends money home to his familyB a foreign pension fund buys a small number of shares in a Karachi-listed bankC a foreign car maker builds and operates a new assembly plant near LahoreD a foreign government gives a grant to build schools in Balochistan [1]

Q12Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Invented data for a copper-exporting country in 2025: external debt service payments (interest and principal) were $9.6 billion and export earnings were $32 billion.

  1. (a) Calculate the debt service ratio (debt service as a percentage of export earnings) in 2025. [1]
  2. (b) Export earnings are forecast to fall by 20 % in 2026 because of a fall in copper prices, while debt service payments stay the same. Calculate the new debt service ratio. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a rising debt service ratio may reduce the country's prospects for development. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 30.0 %
    9.6 ÷ 32 × 100 = 30.0 % (B1).
  2. (b) 37.5 %
    Exports = 32 × 0.8 = $25.6 billion (M1); 9.6 ÷ 25.6 × 100 = 37.5 % (A1).
  3. (c) More foreign currency goes on interest and repayments, leaving less for imports of capital goods and for spending on health and education.
    1 mark: opportunity cost – foreign exchange and government revenue diverted to creditors. 1 mark: less investment, capital imports, social spending. 1 mark: risk of default, higher borrowing costs, IMF conditions, falling investor confidence.
Q13Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain two possible benefits to a low-income host country of a multinational company (MNC) setting up a factory there. [4]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by transfer pricing and why it can reduce the benefits to the host country. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Jobs and incomes; transfer of technology and skills; tax revenue; exports and foreign currency; investment in infrastructure.
    2 marks each for two explained benefits.
  2. (b) MNCs set the prices of goods traded between their own subsidiaries so that profits are recorded in low-tax countries; the host country loses tax revenue.
    1 mark: definition – prices charged in trade between branches of the same MNC. 1 mark: overcharging the host-country subsidiary for inputs/fees, or undercharging for its output. 1 mark: profits appear in a low-tax country. 1 mark: host government loses corporate tax revenue.
Q14Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether foreign direct investment by multinational companies is more beneficial to low-income countries than official development aid. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) FDI brings capital, technology and jobs without debt, but is driven by profit and may concentrate in extractive sectors; aid can target poverty and public goods but has its own problems; they complement each other.
    Analysis (up to 14): FDI – capital inflow, jobs, technology and management skills, exports, tax revenue, multiplier effects; costs – profit repatriation, transfer pricing, enclave production with weak linkages, environmental damage, influence over government, footloose firms, goes to countries with resources or large markets, not the poorest. Aid – grants and concessional loans, can fund health, education and infrastructure (public goods) that attract FDI later; humanitarian relief; problems – tied aid, corruption, dependency, loans add to debt, donor priorities, Dutch-disease effects. Evaluation (up to 6): depends on the country's institutions, sector of FDI, type of aid (project vs budget support); FDI more beneficial for middle-income countries with infrastructure; aid more important for the poorest; reasoned judgement.
Q15Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 10 marks

Invented data for a small mineral-exporting country in one year:GDP: $50 billionprofits of foreign-owned MNCs sent abroad: $3.5 billionincome received by residents from assets abroad: $0.5 billionMNC employment: 40,000 workers, total wages $1.2 billionaverage wage in the rest of the economy: $8,000 per workerMNC exports: $6 billion; corporate tax paid by MNCs: $0.9 billion

  1. (a) Calculate the country's GNI and GNI as a percentage of GDP. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the average annual wage paid by the MNC per worker, in dollars. [1]
  3. (c) Evaluate, using the data, whether the MNCs bring more benefits than costs to the country. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) GNI $47 billion; 94 % of GDP
    50 − 3.5 + 0.5 = 47 (M1); 47 ÷ 50 × 100 = 94 % (A1).
  2. (b) $30,000
    $1.2 billion ÷ 40,000 = $30,000 (B1).
  3. (c) The MNCs create well-paid jobs, exports and tax revenue, but large profit outflows and possible environmental costs reduce the net gain.
    Analysis (up to 5): benefits – 40,000 jobs at wages ($30,000) far above the national average ($8,000); $6 billion exports earn foreign currency; $0.9 billion tax revenue; skills transfer. Costs – $3.5 billion profits repatriated reduces GNI (GNI is only 94 % of GDP); dependence on a few foreign firms; possible environmental damage and use of non-renewable resources; little linkage to local suppliers if inputs are imported; possible transfer pricing. Evaluation (up to 2): reasoned judgement, e.g. net benefits appear positive, but depend on reinvestment, linkages and government regulation.
Q16Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether debt relief is the most effective way to promote economic development in heavily indebted low-income countries. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Debt relief frees resources for spending on development, but its effectiveness depends on governance and on what is done with the savings; trade access, FDI and institutional reform may be more important.
    Analysis (up to 14): burden of debt – high debt service ratio, diversion of foreign exchange and tax revenue, debt overhang deters investment, austerity cuts social spending; debt relief reduces payments, allows spending on health, education and infrastructure, improves credit rating. Problems – moral hazard (may encourage future over-borrowing), funds may be wasted through corruption, conditions attached may impose unpopular reforms, relief is a one-off and does not address causes (dependence on primary exports, low productivity, volatile commodity prices). Alternatives – fairer trade and market access, diversification, FDI, aid targeted at human capital, institutional reform, microfinance. Evaluation (up to 6): relief is a necessary condition in some cases but rarely sufficient; reasoned judgement on 'most effective'.
Q17Relationships between countries (aid, FDI, MNCs, debt) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain the main roles of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank in relation to low-income countries. [4]
  2. (b) Discuss why the conditions attached to IMF loans are often controversial. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) IMF: short- to medium-term loans for balance-of-payments problems, surveillance, technical advice. World Bank: long-term loans and grants for development projects (infrastructure, health, education) and poverty reduction.
    2 marks each: IMF (1 role identified, 1 developed); World Bank (1 role identified, 1 developed).
  2. (b) Conditions such as cutting budget deficits, removing subsidies, raising interest rates and privatisation can restore stability but may raise poverty and unemployment in the short run.
    Analysis (up to 3): conditions aim to correct the causes of the crisis and ensure repayment; but austerity reduces aggregate demand and social spending, removal of fuel/food subsidies hurts the poor, loss of policy independence, 'one-size-fits-all' criticisms. Judgement (1): e.g. conditions may be needed for credibility but should be phased and protect vulnerable groups.
Q18Exchange rate systems and effects · Core · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a floating exchange rate, a fixed exchange rate and a managed float. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which a central bank could prevent its currency from falling below a fixed exchange rate. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Floating: set by market demand and supply with no target. Fixed: the central bank holds the currency at an official rate. Managed float: mostly market-determined, but the central bank intervenes to influence it.
    1 mark for each correct description.
  2. (b) Buy its own currency using foreign exchange reserves; raise interest rates to attract capital inflows; (also capital controls or borrowing from the IMF).
    2 marks each: method identified (1) and explained in terms of demand for the currency (1).
Q19Exchange rate systems and effects · Core · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A Pakistani textile firm sells shirts for Rs 5,600 each. The exchange rate changes from Rs 280 = $1 to Rs 300 = $1 (invented data).

  1. (a) Calculate the dollar price of the shirt before and after the change in the exchange rate, and the percentage change. Give the percentage to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the rupee cost of a $50 machine part before and after the change. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the depreciation might not raise the textile exporter's profits as much as expected. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before $20.00; after $18.67; a fall of 6.7 %
    5600 ÷ 280 = $20.00 (B1); 5600 ÷ 300 = $18.67 (B1); (18.67 − 20) ÷ 20 = −6.7 % (B1).
  2. (b) Rs 14,000 before; Rs 15,000 after
    50 × 280 = 14,000 (B1); 50 × 300 = 15,000 (B1).
  3. (c) Its imported inputs (machinery, dyes, fuel) become more expensive, raising costs; foreign demand may be price inelastic.
    1 mark point (dearer imported inputs / inelastic demand / competitors' currencies also falling); 1 mark development.
Q20Exchange rate systems and effects · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Invented data: a burger costs Rs 900 in Pakistan and $5.50 in the USA. The market exchange rate is Rs 280 = $1.

  1. (a) Calculate the exchange rate implied by purchasing power parity (PPP) for the burger. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage by which the rupee is undervalued against the dollar on this measure. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one reason why a single-product comparison may not give a reliable estimate of the PPP exchange rate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 163.6 per $
    900 ÷ 5.50 (M1) = Rs 163.6 per $ (A1).
  2. (b) 41.6 %
    At the market rate the burger costs 900 ÷ 280 = $3.21 in Pakistan; 3.21 ÷ 5.50 = 0.584 (M1); undervalued by 1 − 0.584 = 41.6 % (A1).
  3. (c) The burger includes non-traded inputs (rent, wages) whose prices differ; local tastes, taxes and competition differ; one product does not represent the whole basket.
    1 mark reason; 1 mark explanation.
Q21Exchange rate systems and effects · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) According to the Marshall–Lerner condition, a depreciation of a currency will improve a country's current account balance ifA the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than oneB the price elasticity of demand for exports is less than oneC the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is less than oneD the income elasticity of demand for imports is greater than one [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than one
    If the sum of PEDs (in absolute value) exceeds 1, export revenue rises and import spending falls by enough to improve the balance. C describes the case in which the balance worsens, as at the start of the J-curve.
Q22Exchange rate systems and effects · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether a fixed exchange rate system is better than a floating exchange rate system for a developing economy. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) A fixed rate gives certainty and can control inflation, but needs large reserves and removes monetary policy independence; a floating rate adjusts automatically but can be volatile; the better choice depends on the economy.
    Analysis (up to 14): fixed – certainty for traders and investors, reduces speculation (if credible), imposes discipline and can anchor inflation; costs – need for large foreign currency reserves, loss of independent monetary policy (interest rates set to defend the peg), risk of speculative attack and forced devaluation, misalignment can harm competitiveness. Floating – automatic correction of current account imbalances, no need for reserves, monetary policy free to target domestic goals; costs – volatility and uncertainty, depreciation raises import prices (imported inflation, cost of fuel and food), raises the burden of foreign-currency debt, speculation. Managed float as a middle way. Evaluation (up to 6): depends on reserves, inflation history, openness, credibility of the central bank, size of foreign-currency debt, commodity dependence; reasoned judgement.
Q23Exchange rate systems and effects · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Invented data: a country's currency depreciated by 15 % at the start of Quarter 1. Its current account balance ($ billion) was:Quarter 0: −2.0Quarter 1: −2.6Quarter 2: −2.9Quarter 3: −2.1Quarter 4: −1.2Quarter 5: −0.4

  1. (a) Identify the quarter in which the current account deficit was largest, and calculate the improvement from that quarter to Quarter 5. [2]
  2. (b) Use the J-curve effect to explain the pattern in the data. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason, other than the depreciation, why the current account might have improved. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Quarter 2 (−$2.9 billion); improvement of $2.5 billion
    B1 Quarter 2. −0.4 − (−2.9) = $2.5 billion (B1).
  2. (b) In the short run demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, so the deficit widens; over time demand becomes more elastic and the Marshall–Lerner condition is met, so the balance improves.
    1 mark: depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. 1 mark: in the short run contracts and habits mean quantities change little (PEDs sum < 1), so import spending rises and the deficit worsens (Q1–Q2). 1 mark: over time buyers switch, PEDs rise and the sum exceeds 1. 1 mark: the deficit narrows (Q3–Q5), giving the J shape.
  3. (c) A recession in the country reducing import demand / rise in world prices of its exports / faster growth in trading partners.
    B1.
Q24Globalisation and trade integration · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a free trade area, a customs union and a common market. [3]
  2. (b) Explain one problem that can arise in a free trade area that does not arise in a customs union. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Free trade area: no tariffs between members, each sets its own external tariffs. Customs union: free trade area plus a common external tariff. Common market: customs union plus free movement of labour and capital.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Trade deflection: imports enter through the member with the lowest external tariff and are moved to other members; rules of origin are needed.
    1 mark: trade deflection. 1 mark: rules of origin/border checks needed, adding cost.
Q25Globalisation and trade integration · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following is a feature of a customs union but not of a free trade area?A removal of tariffs on trade between member countriesB a common external tariff on imports from non-membersC free movement of labour between member countriesD a single currency used by all members [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B a common external tariff on imports from non-members
    Both arrangements remove internal tariffs (A), but only a customs union adds a common external tariff. Free movement of labour (C) is a feature of a common market; a single currency (D) of a monetary union.
Q26Globalisation and trade integration · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Invented data for a country ($ billion):2015: exports 12, imports 18, GDP 402025: exports 15, imports 20, GDP 50

  1. (a) Calculate the trade openness ratio ((exports + imports) as a percentage of GDP) for 2015 and 2025. [2]
  2. (b) A newspaper said the data show the country 'became less involved in globalisation'. Explain why this conclusion may be wrong. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2015: 75 %; 2025: 70 %
    (12 + 18) ÷ 40 × 100 = 75 % (B1); (15 + 20) ÷ 50 × 100 = 70 % (B1).
  2. (b) Trade grew in absolute terms (from $30 billion to $35 billion); the ratio fell only because GDP grew faster; other aspects of globalisation (FDI, migration, technology, capital flows) are not shown.
    1 mark: exports and imports both rose in value. 1 mark: the ratio fell because GDP rose by 25 % while trade rose by about 17 %. 1 mark: globalisation also includes FDI, capital and labour flows not in the data. 1 mark: real vs nominal values / exchange-rate changes may distort figures.
Q27Globalisation and trade integration · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Country A imports a good. It can buy the good from Country B at $10 per unit or from Country C at $8 per unit. Before joining a customs union, Country A charges a 50 % tariff on imports from all countries. Country A then forms a customs union with Country B, keeping a 50 % common external tariff on goods from Country C (invented data).

  1. (a) Explain from which country Country A imports the good before and after joining the customs union. [2]
  2. (b) State whether this is trade creation or trade diversion, and calculate the extra resource cost per unit to the world of buying from the new supplier. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why consumers in Country A may still gain even though the government of Country A loses. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before: from Country C ($8 + $4 tariff = $12, cheaper than B at $10 + $5 = $15). After: from Country B ($10, no tariff, cheaper than C at $12).
    1 mark each, with prices shown.
  2. (b) Trade diversion; extra resource cost $2 per unit
    B1 trade diversion: imports switch from a more efficient non-member to a less efficient member. M1 cost per unit B $10 − C $8; A1 $2 per unit.
  3. (c) Consumers pay $10 instead of $12, but the government loses the $4 per unit tariff revenue it used to collect.
    1 mark consumers' price falls by $2. 1 mark government loses tariff revenue of $4 per unit, which is greater than consumers' gain per unit on existing imports.
Q28Globalisation and trade integration · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that globalisation has reduced inequality between countries. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Globalisation has helped many emerging economies converge, but some low-income and commodity-dependent countries have fallen further behind; effects vary.
    Analysis (up to 14): features of globalisation – trade liberalisation, FDI and MNCs, capital flows, migration, technology transfer, global supply chains. Arguments for reduced inequality between countries – rapid growth in China, India, Vietnam and others through export-led manufacturing; technology and capital flows to lower-income countries; comparative advantage; remittances. Arguments against – benefits concentrated in countries with infrastructure, skills and stable institutions; least developed countries remain dependent on primary exports with volatile prices and declining terms of trade; capital flight and financial crises; brain drain; MNC profits repatriated; protection by high-income countries in agriculture. Distinguish inequality between countries vs within countries. Evaluation (up to 6): weighted judgement, e.g. average gap between countries has narrowed, largely because of a few large Asian economies, while some countries have been left behind; depends on policy and institutions.
Q29Policies to correct the balance of payments · Core · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which policy is an expenditure-reducing policy for correcting a current account deficit?A a devaluation of the currencyB a tariff on imported carsC an increase in income tax ratesD a subsidy to exporters [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C an increase in income tax rates
    Higher income tax reduces disposable income and total spending, including spending on imports. A, B and D are expenditure-switching policies, because they change relative prices to move spending from imports to domestic goods.
Q30Policies to correct the balance of payments · Standard · Paper 4 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing policies for correcting a current account deficit, giving one example of each. [4]
  2. (b) Evaluate whether expenditure-reducing policies are a suitable way to correct a current account deficit in a country with high unemployment. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Switching policies change relative prices so that spending moves from imports to domestic output (e.g. devaluation, tariffs); reducing policies lower total demand so that imports fall (e.g. higher interest rates, tax rises, spending cuts).
    1 mark each definition, 1 mark each example.
  2. (b) They reduce imports but at the cost of lower output and higher unemployment; supply-side or switching policies may be more appropriate.
    Analysis (up to 4): lower AD reduces imports according to the marginal propensity to import; may also lower inflation and improve competitiveness; but output and employment fall further, with multiplier effects; conflict between external balance and full employment; if the MPM is small, a large fall in income is needed for a small improvement. Evaluation (up to 2): judgement – unsuitable as the main policy in a recession; better to use expenditure-switching or supply-side policies, unless the deficit is caused by excess demand.
