Q2Market structures and price discrimination · Core · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Describe the kinked demand curve model of oligopoly, explaining the assumptions about how rival firms react. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the model predicts that prices in oligopoly will be stable even when costs change. [3]
  3. (c) State one limitation of the kinked demand curve model. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rivals match price cuts but do not follow price rises, so demand is elastic above the current price and inelastic below it, producing a kink.
    1 mark: assumption that rivals do not follow a price rise – demand above the kink is price elastic (firm loses many customers). 1 mark: rivals follow a price cut – demand below the kink is price inelastic (little gain in sales). 1 mark: kink at the current price. 1 mark: MR curve has a vertical discontinuity (gap) below the kink.
  2. (b) Because MC can shift up or down within the gap in the MR curve without changing the profit-maximising output and price.
    1 mark: profit maximisation where MR = MC. 1 mark: the discontinuity in MR means MC can rise or fall within the gap. 1 mark: output and price stay the same – price rigidity.
  3. (c) It does not explain how the initial price was set (or: evidence shows oligopoly prices do change; ignores non-price competition and collusion).
    B1.
Q3Market structures and price discrimination · Core · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain two conditions that make collusion between firms in an oligopoly more likely. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why each member of a cartel has an incentive to cheat on the agreement. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Few firms, so agreements are easy to make and monitor; similar costs and products, so firms can agree on one price; also high barriers to entry, stable demand, and weak competition law enforcement.
    For each of two conditions: 1 for the condition, 1 for why it helps collusion. Conditions: small number of firms; similar costs/homogeneous products; barriers to entry stop new firms undercutting; easy to observe rivals' prices (cheating detected); stable market demand; weak competition authority; a dominant firm that can act as price leader.
  2. (b) The cartel price is above each member's marginal cost, so a firm that secretly sells more at, or slightly below, the agreed price raises its own profit, at the expense of the other members; if all do this the price falls.
    1: cartel restricts output to raise price above MC; 1: one firm can raise its own profit by selling extra units/undercutting; 1: gain to cheater comes at the expense of others, so the agreement is unstable (prisoners' dilemma).

Q7Market structures and price discrimination · Standard · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not always: monopoly can bring higher prices and lower output, but economies of scale, dynamic efficiency and regulation may benefit consumers.
    Knowledge and understanding / analysis (up to 14): diagram comparing monopoly with perfect competition – monopoly sets MR = MC, higher price, lower output, P > MC (allocative inefficiency), possibly not at minimum AC (productive inefficiency), deadweight loss, transfer of consumer surplus to producer; X-inefficiency without competition; less choice.   1. (a) Evaluate whether firms in oligopoly are more likely to compete through non-price competition than through price. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Interdependence makes price competition risky (price wars, kinked demand), so oligopolists often prefer branding, advertising, quality and loyalty schemes; but price competition does occur when firms have cost advantages, products are homogeneous, a firm wants market share, or collusion breaks down.

Q10Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 10 marks

A rail company sells peak-time and off-peak tickets on one route (invented data). Peak demand is P = 100 − 2Q and off-peak demand is P = 60 − Q, where P is the fare in dollars and Q is thousands of tickets per week. Marginal cost is constant at $20 per ticket in both markets.

  1. (a) Calculate the profit-maximising quantity and price in each market. [4]
  2. (b) Calculate the price elasticity of demand (as a positive number) in each market at these prices, and explain how this relates to the prices charged. [3]
  3. (c) Calculate the firm's total profit from the two markets, ignoring fixed costs. [1]
  4. (d) State two conditions that make it possible for the company to practise this price discrimination. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Peak: Q = 20 thousand, P = $60; off-peak: Q = 20 thousand, P = $40
    Peak: MR = 100 − 4Q = 20 so Q = 20 (M1), P = 100 − 40 = 60 (A1). Off-peak: MR = 60 − 2Q = 20 so Q = 20 (M1), P = 60 − 20 = 40 (A1).
  2. (b) Peak 1.5; off-peak 2.0; the higher price is charged where demand is less price elastic.
    For P = a − bQ, PED = P ÷ (a − P). Peak: 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5 (B1). Off-peak: 40 ÷ 20 = 2.0 (B1). The firm charges more in the market with lower PED (B1).
  3. (c) $1200 thousand
    (60 − 20) × 20 + (40 − 20) × 20 = 800 + 400 = 1200 (B1).
  4. (d) Some monopoly power; markets can be separated (ticket valid only at that time) with no resale; different PEDs in the two markets.
    1 mark each for any two.

Q14Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 10 marks

A regional water company is the only supplier in its area (invented data). Its total cost is TC = 60 + 4Q and its demand curve is P = 40 − 2Q, where Q is millions of cubic metres a year, P is dollars per cubic metre and TC is in $ million. So MC = 4, AC = 60/Q + 4 and MR = 40 − 4Q.

  1. (a) Calculate the profit-maximising output, price and profit. [3]
  2. (b) The regulator requires the company to set price equal to marginal cost. Calculate the output and the profit or loss. [2]
  3. (c) The regulator instead requires price to equal average cost. Calculate the output and price, each to two decimal places. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why this firm is a natural monopoly and why marginal-cost pricing is difficult to sustain. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q = 9 million m³, P = $22 per m³, profit = $102 million.
    MR = MC: 40 − 4Q = 4 → Q = 9 (1); P = 40 − 18 = 22 (1); profit = 22 × 9 − (60 + 36) = 198 − 96 = 102 (1).
  2. (b) Q = 18 million m³ at P = $4; loss of $60 million.
    40 − 2Q = 4 → Q = 18 (1); TR = 72, TC = 60 + 72 = 132, loss = 60 (equal to the fixed cost) (1).
  3. (c) Q = 16.14 million m³, P = $7.72.
    40 − 2Q = 60/Q + 4 → 2Q² − 36Q + 60 = 0 → Q² − 18Q + 30 = 0 (1); Q = 9 + √51 = 16.14 (larger root), P = 40 − 32.28 = 7.72 (1).

    1: very high fixed/sunk costs; 1: AC falls over the whole range of market output, so one firm has lowest cost; 1: MC < AC so P = MC gives a loss (here the whole fixed cost) and needs a subsidy, or the firm will not invest.
Q15Market structures and price discrimination · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A cinema is the only one in a small town. Demand for tickets to a screening is P = 50 − Q, where P is the ticket price in dollars and Q is the number of tickets. Marginal cost is constant at $10 per ticket and there are no fixed costs (invented data).

  1. (a) If the cinema charges a single price, calculate its output, price, profit and the consumer surplus. [3]
  2. (b) If the cinema could practise first-degree (perfect) price discrimination, calculate its output, profit and the consumer surplus. [3]
  3. (c) Explain why output under perfect price discrimination is allocatively efficient even though consumers are worse off. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q = 20 tickets, P = $30, profit = $400, consumer surplus = $200.
    MR = 50 − 2Q = 10 → Q = 20, P = 30 (1); profit = (30 − 10) × 20 = 400 (1); CS = ½ × 20 × (50 − 30) = 200 (1).
  2. (b) Q = 40 tickets, profit = $800, consumer surplus = $0.
    Each ticket sold at the buyer's maximum price, so MR = demand; sell until P = MC: 50 − Q = 10 → Q = 40 (1); profit = ½ × 40 × (50 − 10) = 800 (1); consumer surplus = 0 (1).
  3. (c) Output rises to where price of the last unit equals marginal cost, so there is no deadweight loss; but all the surplus goes to the producer instead of consumers.
    1: last unit sold at P = MC (output 40 = competitive output), so no deadweight welfare loss; 1: the whole surplus is transferred to the producer, so it is efficient but not equitable.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by limit pricing, using these figures. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the incumbent's supernormal profit per month at this price. Average cost includes normal profit. [2]
  3. (c) Distinguish between limit pricing and predatory pricing. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The incumbent sets its price ($9.50) below the average cost of a potential entrant ($10) but above its own average cost ($8), so entry would be loss-making for the newcomer while the incumbent still earns supernormal profit.
    1: price set to deter entry; 1: price below entrant's AC ($10), so entrant would make a loss; 1: above incumbent's AC ($8), so incumbent still profitable (uses its economies of scale).
  2. (b) $300 000.
    (9.50 − 8.00) × 200 000 (1) = $300 000 (1).
  3. (c) Limit pricing keeps price low enough to stop rivals entering while the incumbent remains profitable; predatory pricing sets price below the predator's own cost (often below AVC) for a time to force an existing rival out, then raises price. Predatory pricing is usually illegal.
    1: limit pricing — deters entry, still profitable; 1: predatory — price below own cost to drive out existing rivals, with later price rises; 1: legal/competition policy difference or short-run losses accepted by predator.
