  1. (a) In an open economy with a government, the marginal propensity to save is 0.1, the marginal tax rate is 0.15 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.25. Government spending rises by $20bn. By how much will national income eventually rise?A $20bnB $40bnC $80bnD $200bn [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B $40bn
    Multiplier = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. ΔY = 2 × 20 = $40bn. D uses only the MPS (1 ÷ 0.1 = 10), ignoring taxes and imports.

  2. (b) Calculate the value of the multiplier. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the increase in government spending needed to close the output gap. [2]

  2. (b) 2.5

Q5Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In which situation is a cut in interest rates least likely to raise aggregate demand?A Interest rates are already close to zero and households and firms expect prices to fall.B Firms have strong business confidence and many profitable projects.C Households have large variable-rate mortgages.D The exchange rate is floating and exports are price elastic. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Interest rates are already close to zero and households and firms expect prices to fall.
    Near-zero rates with deflationary expectations is a liquidity trap: people hold extra money rather than spend it, and real interest rates stay high, so monetary policy has little effect. B, C and D are conditions in which a rate cut works strongly (investment, disposable income, depreciation).
Q6Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 3 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) According to the Laffer curve, which statement is correct?A Any cut in the income tax rate will raise tax revenue.B Tax revenue is highest when the tax rate is 100%.C If the tax rate is above the revenue-maximising rate, a cut in the rate can increase tax revenue.D Tax revenue rises at a constant rate as the tax rate rises. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C If the tax rate is above the revenue-maximising rate, a cut in the rate can increase tax revenue.
    The Laffer curve shows revenue rising and then falling as the rate rises, because high rates discourage work and encourage avoidance. Only above the revenue-maximising rate does a cut raise revenue. A is the common misreading: below that rate a cut reduces revenue.

Q9Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how progressive income taxes and unemployment benefits act as automatic stabilisers. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether automatic stabilisers are likely to be effective in an economy with a large informal sector. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) In a downturn incomes fall, so tax revenue falls more than proportionately and benefit payments rise automatically, cushioning disposable income and AD; in a boom tax revenue rises and benefits fall, restraining AD. No new decision is needed.
    1 recession: tax revenue falls; 1 benefit spending rises → supports AD; 1 boom: opposite effect dampens AD; 1 automatic, without discretionary changes.
  2. (b) Less effective: few workers pay income tax or receive unemployment benefits, so revenue and transfers change little with the cycle; the stabilising effect is weak, so discretionary policy is needed. But indirect taxes still vary with spending and cash-transfer programmes may help.
    Analysis (up to 3): narrow tax base, low benefit coverage, small size of government; Consideration (1+): sales taxes, targeted transfers, judgement.
Q10Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a cut in interest rates may affect the exchange rate and the current account of the balance of payments. [4]
  2. (b) Consider why the effect of the interest rate cut on the current account may be small. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Lower rates reduce returns on deposits in the currency → hot money outflows → lower demand for the currency → depreciation; exports become cheaper and imports dearer, which may improve the current account (if Marshall–Lerner holds), but higher domestic spending also raises imports.
    1 capital outflow/less demand for currency; 1 depreciation; 1 exports more price competitive, imports dearer; 1 net effect on current account depends on elasticities/higher income raising imports.
  2. (b) Demand for exports and imports may be price inelastic in the short run (J-curve); higher incomes from cheaper borrowing raise imports; other countries may also cut rates; exchange rates are affected by many other factors.
    1 reason; 1 explained; 1 judgement/second reason.

Q12Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

The quantity theory of money: MV = PT. In an invented economy the money supply M is $400bn, the velocity of circulation V is 5 and the volume of transactions T is 1,000 billion units.

  1. (a) Calculate the average price level P. [1]
  2. (b) The money supply rises by 10% while V and T are unchanged. Calculate the new price level and the percentage change. [2]
  3. (c) Instead, the money supply rises by 10% while the volume of transactions rises by 4%. Calculate the percentage change in the price level, to one decimal place. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why monetarists assume that V and T are roughly constant in the short run, and one criticism of this assumption. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2
    1 P = MV ÷ T = 400 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 2.
  2. (b) 2.2; +10%
    M1 440 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 2.2. A1 +10%.
  3. (c) +5.8%
    M1 P = 440 × 5 ÷ 1,040 = 2.1154. A1 (2.1154 − 2) ÷ 2 = 5.8%.
  4. (d) V depends on slowly changing payment habits and institutions; T is fixed at full-employment output (determined by supply-side factors). Critics: V changes, for example when people hoard money in recessions or with new payment technology, and output is not at full employment, so more money can raise T rather than P.
    1 V determined by habits/institutions; 1 T determined by real factors/full employment; 1 valid criticism (unstable velocity, spare capacity, Keynesian view).
Q13Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether supply-side policies are more effective than demand-side policies in achieving sustained economic growth. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Supply-side policies raise productive capacity (LRAS), allowing non-inflationary sustained growth, but are slow, costly and uncertain. Demand-side policies act faster to close negative output gaps but cannot raise capacity and risk inflation or debt if used alone. Supply-side policies are more effective for sustained growth, with demand management needed for stability.

One-sided answers are capped at Level 2. Analysis: AD/AS diagrams — AD shift (actual growth, inflation near capacity), LRAS shift (potential growth); market-based supply-side policies (tax cuts, deregulation, privatisation, labour market reform) and interventionist ones (education, infrastructure, R&D); fiscal and monetary demand management. Evaluation: time lags; cost to the budget; effectiveness depends on country (infrastructure gaps, governance); some policies (infrastructure spending) work on both sides; demand needed to use new capacity; inequality from market-based reforms. Q16Effectiveness of fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Invented data. A government is choosing between raising its spending by $10bn and cutting income tax by $10bn. Households spend 0.8 of any increase in disposable income on consumption in the first round. The marginal propensity to withdraw from national income is 0.5.

  1. (a) Calculate the eventual change in national income from the increase in government spending. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the eventual change in national income from the income tax cut. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the tax cut has a smaller effect than the same increase in government spending. [2]
  4. (d) Consider one reason why the tax cut might have an even smaller effect than calculated. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) +$20bn
    M1 multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. A1 10 × 2 = $20bn.
  2. (b) +$16bn
    M1 initial rise in consumption = 0.8 × 10 = $8bn (the rest is saved or spent on imports, treated as already part of the withdrawals). A1 8 × 2 = $16bn.
  3. (c) All of the extra government spending is an injection into the circular flow in the first round, whereas part of a tax cut is withdrawn before it is spent.
    1 G adds directly to AD; 1 only part (MPC) of the tax cut is spent in the first round.
  4. (d) e.g. households expect future tax rises to repay borrowing and save the tax cut (Ricardian equivalence); low confidence; much spending goes on imports; benefit mostly to high earners with low MPC.
    1 reason; 1 explained.

Q22Policy objectives and conflicts · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain, using a long-run Phillips curve, why monetarists believe that expansionary demand-side policy cannot permanently reduce unemployment below the natural rate. [4]

Show answer
  1. (a) Expansion moves the economy up the short-run curve: unemployment falls and inflation rises.     Analysis (up to 3): supply-side measures; risk of accelerating inflation; credibility of low-inflation policy. Consideration (1+): Keynesian view/hysteresis/slow adjustment; judgement.

Q25Policy objectives and conflicts · Standard · Paper 4 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a depreciation of the currency intended to reduce a current account deficit may conflict with the objective of low inflation. [4]
  2. (b) Consider whether this conflict is likely to be serious for an economy that imports most of its energy. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Depreciation makes imports dearer in domestic currency (imported fuel, food, raw materials) → cost-push inflation; exports become cheaper, so net exports and AD rise → demand-pull pressure; workers may demand higher wages.

Q27Policy objectives and conflicts · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether a government can achieve low inflation and low unemployment at the same time. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) In the short run demand-side policy faces a trade-off (short-run Phillips curve), but both can be achieved if unemployment is at or above a low natural rate and inflation expectations are anchored; supply-side policies that lower the natural rate and raise productivity make both possible; supply shocks make it harder.

One-sided answers are capped at Level 2. Analysis: short-run Phillips curve and AD/AS; demand-pull trade-off; long-run vertical Phillips curve; natural rate; supply-side policies shifting LRAS right and the LRPC left; credibility of an independent central bank anchoring expectations. Q28Policy objectives and conflicts · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate the view that economic growth should be the most important macroeconomic objective for a low-income country. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Growth raises incomes, creates jobs, funds public services and reduces poverty, so it is often a priority; but without price stability, external balance and fair distribution, growth may be unsustainable (boom–bust, inflation, debt crises) or fail to reach the poor; environmental sustainability also matters. Growth is necessary but not sufficient; priority depends on current conditions.

Q29Policy objectives and conflicts · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 20 marks
  1. (a) Evaluate whether reducing a large budget deficit is compatible with achieving economic growth and low unemployment. [20]
Show answer
  1. (a) Short run: cutting spending or raising taxes reduces AD, growth and employment (multiplier). Long run: lower borrowing may reduce interest rates, crowding in, restore confidence and avoid debt crises, supporting growth. Compatibility depends on the state of the economy, the method of deficit reduction and the response of monetary policy.

One-sided answers are capped at Level 2. Analysis: contractionary fiscal policy and AD/AS; multiplier; cyclical vs structural deficits; crowding out/crowding in; debt sustainability and interest costs. Evaluation: timing (in recession vs boom); composition (cutting investment harms long-run growth; cutting wasteful subsidies or broadening the tax base may not); expansionary fiscal contraction debate; accompanying monetary easing or depreciation; credibility with lenders; distributional effects. Q30Policy objectives and conflicts · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

In an invented economy the expectations-augmented Phillips curve is:actual inflation (%) = expected inflation (%) + 10 − 2 × unemployment (%)

  1. (a) Calculate the inflation rate when unemployment is 4% and expected inflation is 3%. [1]
  2. (b) Workers now expect inflation to be the rate you calculated in (a). The government keeps unemployment at 4%. Calculate the new inflation rate. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the rate of unemployment at which actual inflation equals expected inflation. [1]
  4. (d) Explain what these results show about the trade-off between inflation and unemployment. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5%
    1 3 + 10 − 8 = 5%.
  2. (b) 7%
    M1 expected inflation = 5. A1 5 + 10 − 8 = 7%.
  3. (c) 5%
    1 10 − 2U = 0 → U = 5%.
  4. (d) Holding unemployment below 5% (the natural rate) gives ever-rising inflation (5%, then 7%, ...) as expectations adjust; the trade-off exists only in the short run, and the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at 5%.
    1 inflation accelerates when U is held below the natural rate; 1 because expectations adjust upward each period; 1 so no permanent trade-off/vertical LRPC at 5%.
