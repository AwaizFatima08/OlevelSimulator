Q1Mode of action & specificity · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which statement describes the induced-fit hypothesis of enzyme action? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B The active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, so that it fits the substrate more closely.
    B: in induced fit the active site is not exactly complementary at first; binding of the substrate causes a small change in the shape of the active site, giving a closer fit and straining bonds in the substrate. A describes the lock-and-key hypothesis. D is wrong because enzymes remain specific.
Q2Mode of action & specificity · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Catalase is found inside the cells of potato tubers, liver and yeast. It breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism, into water and oxygen:2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

  1. (a) State whether catalase is an intracellular or an extracellular enzyme. Name one enzyme that is extracellular. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how catalase increases the rate of this reaction. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why a cell needs only a small amount of catalase to deal with the hydrogen peroxide it produces. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Intracellular; extracellular example: salivary amylase / trypsin / pancreatic lipase (any one).
    B1 intracellular. B1 any correct extracellular enzyme (one secreted out of the cell that made it).
  2. (b) Hydrogen peroxide binds to the active site forming an enzyme–substrate complex; this lowers the activation energy; products are released and the enzyme is unchanged and reused.
    1 mark each for any three of: substrate binds to the complementary active site; enzyme–substrate complex forms; activation energy is lowered; bonds in the substrate are strained / substrate held in a position that favours reaction; enzyme–product complex forms and products leave the active site; enzyme is not used up and can bind another substrate molecule.
  3. (c) Each catalase molecule is not used up and has a very high turnover, so it can break down many substrate molecules per second.
    B1 enzyme reused / high turnover number.

Q4Mode of action & specificity · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Tears and saliva contain lysozyme, an enzyme that hydrolyses glycosidic bonds in peptidoglycan, the main substance in bacterial cell walls. Lysozyme is made in cells of the tear glands and secreted onto the surface of the eye.

  1. (a) State what is meant by an enzyme. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why lysozyme hydrolyses bonds in bacterial cell walls but has no effect on the cellulose cell walls of plant cells. [2]
  3. (c) Describe how lysozyme forms an enzyme–substrate complex. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest how lysozyme in tears helps to protect the eye. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A globular protein that acts as a biological catalyst: it speeds up a (metabolic) reaction without being used up or permanently changed.
    B1 (globular) protein. B1 biological catalyst / speeds up reaction and is not used up.
  2. (b) Lysozyme's active site is complementary only to its substrate in peptidoglycan; cellulose has a different shape, so it cannot bind to the active site and no enzyme–substrate complex forms.
    B1 active site complementary to (part of) peptidoglycan / enzyme is specific. B1 cellulose a different shape, does not fit / bind, no E–S complex.
  3. (c) The substrate collides with and enters the active site; it is held there by temporary bonds (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds) with R groups of amino acids in the active site.
    B1 substrate collides with / binds to active site. B1 held by temporary bonds (H bonds / ionic) with R groups of the active site.
  4. (d) It weakens bacterial cell walls so bacteria burst (lysis) and die, which helps prevent eye infections.
    B1 bacteria killed / burst because cell wall weakened → fewer infections.
Q5Mode of action & specificity · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Statements P to T describe stages in the action of an enzyme.P The products are released from the active site.Q A substrate molecule collides with the active site.R An enzyme–product complex forms.S An enzyme–substrate complex forms.T Bonds in the substrate are put under strain, lowering the activation energy.

  1. (a) Put statements P to T in the order in which they happen during an enzyme-catalysed reaction. [2]
  2. (b) State what happens to the enzyme after step P. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction depends on how often enzyme and substrate molecules collide. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Q, S, T, R, P
    2 marks all correct; 1 mark if one pair is reversed.
  2. (b) The enzyme is unchanged and its active site is free to bind another substrate molecule.
    B1 unchanged / reused / binds another substrate.
  3. (c) An enzyme–substrate complex can only form when a substrate molecule collides with an active site; more collisions per unit time give more enzyme–substrate complexes and so more product formed per unit time.
    B1 E–S complex forms only after collision with active site. B1 more (successful) collisions per unit time → more E–S complexes → faster rate.

  1. (a) Calculate how many substrate molecules one enzyme molecule converts in 2.5 minutes. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why one enzyme molecule can convert so many substrate molecules. [2]
  3. (c) State one way in which enzymes differ from inorganic catalysts. [1]

  1. (a) 675 000 (6.75 × 10⁵) molecules
    M1 2.5 × 60 = 150 s. A1 4500 × 150 = 675 000.
  2. (b) The enzyme is not used up or changed by the reaction; once the products leave, the active site is free to bind another substrate molecule.

  3. (c) Enzymes are proteins (so they are specific / denatured by high temperature or extreme pH / work at body temperature).
    B1 any one: protein; highly specific; denatured by heat or pH; work at mild temperatures.
Q7Mode of action & specificity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Sucrase is an enzyme produced by cells lining the small intestine. It hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose. It does not hydrolyse lactose, even though lactose is also a disaccharide.

  1. (a) Explain why sucrase can hydrolyse sucrose but cannot hydrolyse lactose. [3]
  2. (b) Name the type of reaction catalysed by sucrase and the bond that is broken. [2]
  3. (c) Outline one difference between the lock-and-key hypothesis and the induced-fit hypothesis. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The active site of sucrase has a specific shape complementary to sucrose; this shape depends on the enzyme's tertiary structure (its sequence of amino acids and their R groups); lactose has a different shape, so it cannot fit/bind and no enzyme–substrate complex forms.
    1 mark each for any three of: active site has a specific/complementary shape to sucrose; shape is determined by the tertiary structure / bonds between R groups / primary structure; lactose is not complementary / does not fit the active site; so no enzyme–substrate complex can form with lactose.
  2. (b) Hydrolysis; (1,2) glycosidic bond.
    B1 hydrolysis. B1 glycosidic bond.
  3. (c) In lock-and-key the active site is a fixed shape exactly complementary to the substrate; in induced fit the active site changes shape slightly when the substrate binds, so that it fits more closely.
    B1 lock and key: rigid/fixed active site, exact fit. B1 induced fit: active site (and enzyme) changes shape on binding the substrate.
Q8Mode of action & specificity · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In a reaction without a catalyst, the activation energy is 75 kJ mol⁻¹. With an enzyme, the activation energy is 29 kJ mol⁻¹. The products have 18 kJ mol⁻¹ less energy than the reactants.Which statement is correct? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A The enzyme lowers the activation energy by 46 kJ mol⁻¹ and the energy released by the reaction stays at 18 kJ mol⁻¹.
    A: 75 − 29 = 46 kJ mol⁻¹ lower; an enzyme does not change the energy of reactants or products, so the overall energy change is unchanged. B wrongly adds the reduction to the energy released; C confuses the new value with the reduction.
Q9Mode of action & specificity · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Hexokinase catalyses the first reaction of glycolysis in many cells:glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADPWhen glucose enters the active site, the two lobes of the enzyme move towards each other and the active site closes around the glucose.

  1. (a) Name the model of enzyme action that this observation supports. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how the change in shape of the active site helps hexokinase to catalyse the reaction. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest an advantage of the active site closing around glucose before the phosphate group is transferred from ATP. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why the lock-and-key hypothesis cannot fully account for the behaviour of hexokinase. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Induced fit
    B1 induced fit.
  2. (b) The active site moulds more closely around the substrates, so more temporary bonds form between R groups and the substrates; this strains bonds and holds glucose and the phosphate group of ATP close together in the correct orientation, lowering the activation energy.
    1 mark each for any three of: closer fit / more bonds between R groups and substrate; strain / distortion of bonds; glucose and ATP held close together / correctly orientated; activation energy lowered.
  3. (c) Water is excluded from the active site, so ATP is not simply hydrolysed (wasting energy); the phosphate group is transferred to glucose instead.
    B1 water excluded / kept out. B1 so ATP not hydrolysed / energy not wasted; phosphate goes to glucose.
  4. (d) The lock-and-key hypothesis treats the active site as rigid, but in hexokinase the active site changes shape when the substrate binds.
    B1 lock-and-key = rigid / fixed active site; here shape changes.
Q10Mode of action & specificity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In germinating barley grains used for malting, amylase hydrolyses starch to maltose. Maltase then hydrolyses maltose to glucose. Both starch and maltose contain α-1,4 glycosidic bonds between α-glucose units.

  1. (a) Amylase and maltase both break α-1,4 glycosidic bonds. Explain why they are still described as specific enzymes. [2]
  2. (b) Name the type of reaction catalysed by both enzymes and the molecule that must be present for it. [2]
  3. (c) A student tested the barley extract with Benedict's solution to show that maltase was active. Explain why a positive Benedict's test does not show this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each enzyme's active site is complementary to the shape of its own substrate: amylase binds a section of the long starch chain, while maltase's active site fits only the disaccharide maltose, so maltase cannot bind starch and amylase does not bind maltose effectively.
    B1 active site of each complementary to its own substrate's shape. B1 maltose (disaccharide) and starch (long polysaccharide chain) have different shapes, so each only binds one.
  2. (b) Hydrolysis; water
    B1 hydrolysis. B1 water.
  3. (c) Both maltose and glucose are reducing sugars, so Benedict's solution gives a positive result whether or not maltase has converted maltose into glucose.
    B1 maltose is a reducing sugar (as well as glucose). B1 so a positive result could be due to maltose alone / cannot tell which sugar is present.
Q11Mode of action & specificity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose. Starch contains α-glucose units joined by α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds. Cellulose contains β-glucose units joined by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds. Humans secrete amylase but do not make cellulase. Buffaloes and cattle digest cellulose with cellulase made by microorganisms in their stomachs.

  1. (a) Explain why human amylase cannot hydrolyse cellulose. [3]
  2. (b) Textile mills in Faisalabad use cellulase to give cotton fabric a smoother finish. Suggest why cellulase does not damage woollen garments, which are made of the protein keratin. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cellulose is made of β-glucose, in which alternate glucose units are inverted, so the chain and its β-1,4 bonds have a different shape from starch; cellulose is not complementary to the active site of amylase, so it cannot bind and no enzyme–substrate complex forms.
    B1 β-glucose / β-1,4 bonds / alternate units inverted. B1 different shape, not complementary to amylase active site. B1 no E–S complex / bond not hydrolysed.
  2. (b) Cellulase is specific: its active site is complementary only to β-1,4 glycosidic bonds in cellulose. Keratin is a protein with peptide bonds and a different shape, so it does not bind to the active site.
    B1 enzyme specific / active site complementary to cellulose only. B1 keratin a protein / peptide bonds / different shape, cannot bind.
Q12Mode of action & specificity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A single base substitution in the gene for an enzyme replaces one amino acid with another. The changed amino acid is in a part of the enzyme that is far from the active site. The enzyme made from the mutated gene has only about 5 % of its normal activity.

  1. (a) Explain how a change in an amino acid far from the active site can reduce the activity of the enzyme. [3]
  2. (b) A second mutation replaces an amino acid inside the active site with a different amino acid of similar size and charge. The enzyme's activity does not change. Suggest why. [2]
  3. (c) Explain, in terms of activation energy, why enzymes allow metabolic reactions to take place quickly at 37 °C. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The primary structure changes, so a different R group is present; this changes the bonds (hydrogen, ionic, disulfide, hydrophobic interactions) that hold the tertiary structure; the protein folds differently so the shape of the active site changes and the substrate fits less well, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
    1 mark each for any three of: change in primary structure / different R group; different bonds form or bonds lost (hydrogen / ionic / disulfide / hydrophobic interactions); tertiary structure changes / folds differently; active site shape changes; substrate no longer complementary / fewer enzyme–substrate complexes.
  2. (b) The new R group has similar properties, so it forms the same bonds/interactions; the shape and charge of the active site are unchanged, so the substrate still binds.
    B1 similar R group forms the same bonds / interactions. B1 active site shape (and charge) still complementary to substrate.
  3. (c) Enzymes lower the activation energy, so at 37 °C a much larger proportion of substrate molecules have enough energy to react; without the enzyme a high temperature would be needed.
    B1 enzymes lower activation energy. B1 so reactions occur fast at body temperature without needing high temperatures (which would denature proteins / damage cells).
Q13Mode of action & specificity · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

An enzyme from a bacterium that lives in hot springs at 80 °C is made of two identical polypeptide chains. Its active site is formed from R groups belonging to both chains. When the two chains are separated without any other change, neither chain shows any activity.

  1. (a) Name the level of protein structure that involves more than one polypeptide chain. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the separated chains have no activity. [2]
  3. (c) This enzyme has more disulfide bonds and ionic bonds than a similar enzyme from a bacterium that lives at 30 °C. Explain how this allows the enzyme to stay active at 80 °C. [3]
  4. (d) Suggest why this enzyme has a low rate of activity at 30 °C. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Quaternary structure
    B1 quaternary.
  2. (b) The active site is made from R groups of both chains, so a single chain has an incomplete active site that is not complementary to the substrate; the substrate cannot bind and no enzyme–substrate complexes form.

  2. (b) Explain why using a lower concentration of hydrogen peroxide reduces the risk but may affect the investigation. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hydrogen peroxide at 6 % is an irritant/harmful to eyes and skin — use the lowest concentration that works (e.g. 3 %) and wear eye protection.     B1

  1. (a) Explain why heating over a Bunsen flame is unsuitable here. [2]
  2. (b) Identify two other hazards in this experiment and give a precaution for each. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest a safer way to warm the tubes. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The phenolphthalein solution contains ethanol, which is highly flammable — the vapour could ignite near a flame; heating directly also gives uneven, uncontrolled temperature (and contents may spit).

  2. (b) Sodium carbonate solution: irritant to eyes — wear eye protection. Lipase solution: allergen/irritant — avoid skin contact, gloves. Phenolphthalein: harmful (possible carcinogen in large amounts) — use only a few drops, avoid contact, wash hands. Hot water bath — tongs.

    B1

Protease activity is measured by the biuret test: biuret reagent contains sodium hydroxide and copper(II) sulfate. The student also uses a centrifuge to remove undigested solid protein before testing.

  1. (a) Identify the hazards of biuret reagent and give precautions, including first aid if it splashes into the eye. [3]
  2. (b) Describe two precautions when using the centrifuge. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sodium hydroxide is corrosive/irritant to eyes and skin (alkali can cause serious eye damage); copper sulfate is harmful/irritant. Q225Skill: risk assessment · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 5 marks

A student plans to compare catalase activity in leaves of trees growing beside a busy highway near Islamabad and in a park 1 km away. She will collect the leaves herself, then grind them with sand and test the extracts with 3 % hydrogen peroxide in the school laboratory.

  1. (a) Identify hazards in the fieldwork part of the investigation and give suitable precautions. [3]
  2. (b) Identify one hazard in the laboratory part and give a precaution. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why a risk assessment should consider people other than the student. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Traffic: risk of being hit by vehicles — work away from the road edge, wear a high-visibility vest, go with a partner. Plants (thorns, sap, insects/stinging insects, allergies) — gloves, long sleeves. Air pollution / exhaust fumes — limit time spent near the road. Permission/lone working — tell someone where you are going, get permission from the landowner/park authority.
    1 mark for each hazard with a matching precaution, max 3.
  2. (b) Sand and pestle grinding can splash extract/sand into eyes — eye protection; or hydrogen peroxide irritant — goggles; or dust/pollutants on leaves — wash leaves and hands.
    B1
  3. (c) Others nearby (classmates, the public, drivers) could be harmed by the activity, e.g. spills on the floor, broken glass, or a student stepping into traffic, so precautions must protect them too.
    B1
