Q11Diseases, pathogens & transmission · Standard · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a vaccine against seasonal influenza must be updated every year. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The influenza virus changes its surface antigens over time (antigenic drift), so antibodies made against last year's strain no longer bind well to the new strain; a new vaccine must contain antigens of the predicted strain.

Q12Diseases, pathogens & transmission · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Name the vector of malaria and describe how it transmits the disease. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why it has been difficult to develop a fully effective vaccine against malaria. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why malaria cases in parts of Pakistan rise sharply in the months after the monsoon. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The female Anopheles mosquito; when she takes a blood meal from an infected person she takes in Plasmodium gametocytes (sexual stages); they develop in the mosquito and the infective stages move to her salivary glands; she injects them in saliva when she bites another person.
    B1 female Anopheles mosquito; B1 takes up Plasmodium in blood from an infected person; B1 parasites injected (with saliva / anticoagulant) into the blood of the next person bitten.
  2. (b) Plasmodium is a eukaryote with many stages in its life cycle, each with different antigens; it spends time inside liver cells and red blood cells, hidden from the immune system; antigens vary between strains.

    B1 stagnant water / pools → more mosquito breeding sites (larvae); B1 warm and humid conditions → more mosquitoes / faster development of Plasmodium in the mosquito.

Q14Diseases, pathogens & transmission · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the stages of the life cycle of Plasmodium in a human host, including where it multiplies. [4]
  2. (b) Heterozygous individuals with the sickle cell allele have partial protection against severe malaria. Explain how this protection arises. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Sporozoites injected by the mosquito enter liver cells and multiply; merozoites are released from the liver into the blood; they invade red blood cells and multiply asexually; the infected red cells burst, releasing more merozoites, which infect other red cells; some develop into gametocytes that can be taken up by a mosquito.
    Up to 4 marks, 1 each for any four of: sporozoites enter liver cells and multiply; merozoites are released into the blood; merozoites invade red blood cells and multiply asexually; infected red cells burst, releasing merozoites; some merozoites develop into gametocytes that are taken up by a mosquito.
  2. (b) Parasites develop poorly in the red blood cells of heterozygotes, so fewer parasites multiply and the disease is less severe.
    B1 Plasmodium reproduces less well in red cells that contain sickle haemoglobin (abnormal shape); B1 so fewer parasites survive and the infection is less severe / heterozygote advantage.
Q15Diseases, pathogens & transmission · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A wedding meal in Multan was followed by an outbreak of typhoid fever, caused by the bacterium Salmonella Typhi. Investigators recorded what each of the 150 guests ate. 60 guests ate the rice, and 36 of them became ill. 90 guests did not eat the rice, and 12 of them became ill.

  1. (a) Calculate the attack rate (percentage of people who became ill) among guests who ate the rice. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the relative risk of illness for guests who ate the rice compared with guests who did not. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two reasons why this result does not prove that the rice was the source of the infection. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 60 %
    M1 36 ÷ 60 × 100; A1 = 60 %.
  2. (b) 4.5
    M1 attack rate in those who did not eat rice = 12 ÷ 90 = 13.3 %; M1 relative risk = 60 ÷ 13.3 (or 0.60 ÷ 0.133); A1 = 4.5.
  3. (c) Any two of: guests who ate rice may also have eaten other contaminated foods; guests may not remember accurately what they ate (recall bias); the groups may differ in other ways; laboratory tests on the rice or on stool samples are needed to confirm the source; numbers are small so chance is possible.
    1 mark each for any two.

Q19Antibiotics & resistance · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Penicillinase is an enzyme produced by some bacteria. Describe how penicillinase makes a bacterium resistant to penicillin. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Penicillinase hydrolyses (breaks) the β-lactam ring of penicillin, so the antibiotic is inactivated before it can reach and block the enzymes that make the cell wall.
    B1 enzyme hydrolyses / breaks the β-lactam ring of penicillin; B1 penicillin is inactivated so it cannot inhibit cell wall synthesis (peptidoglycan cross-linking).
Q20Antibiotics & resistance · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Why is penicillin not effective against measles? [1]

  1. (a) B Viruses have no peptidoglycan cell wall for penicillin to act on.
    Penicillin stops bacteria building peptidoglycan cell walls; viruses have no cell wall or metabolism of their own, so penicillin has no target. C is wrong because penicillinase is made by some bacteria, not viruses.
Q21Antibiotics & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how penicillin kills bacteria that are growing and dividing. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why penicillin does not harm human cells. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Penicillin inhibits the enzymes that form cross-links between peptidoglycan chains in the cell wall; new wall is weak; water enters by osmosis and the weakened wall cannot withstand the pressure, so the cell bursts (lysis).
