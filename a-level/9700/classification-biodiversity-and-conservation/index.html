Q1Conservation & threats · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Give three reasons why it is important to maintain biodiversity. [3]
  2. (b) The Indus river dolphin lives only in the Indus river system and is endangered. Its range is split into sections by barrages built for irrigation. Suggest three threats to this species, other than barrages. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any three: moral/ethical duty to other species and future generations; economic value – food, timber, crops, ecotourism; source of medicines and new genes for crop breeding; ecosystem services – pollination, nutrient recycling, flood control, oxygen; stability of food webs/ecosystems; aesthetic/cultural value.
    1 mark each for three different reasons.
  2. (b) Any three: low water flow in the dry season because water is taken for irrigation; pollution from industry, sewage and agricultural chemicals; accidental capture in fishing nets (bycatch); dolphins stranded in irrigation canals; reduced fish prey from overfishing; noise/boat traffic; small, isolated populations losing genetic diversity.
    1 mark each for any three suitable threats.
Q2Conservation & threats · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how national parks such as Khunjerab National Park help to conserve endangered species. [3]
  2. (b) Astola Island became Pakistan's first marine protected area. Suggest two activities that might be restricted in a marine protected area and explain how one of them benefits marine species. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Habitat is protected from development, farming and logging; hunting is banned or controlled; whole ecosystems are protected, so food chains and interactions are kept; species can be monitored and studied.
    1 mark each for any three of: habitat protected; hunting/poaching controlled; whole ecosystem/all species protected; monitoring/research; controlled tourism raises money.
  2. (b) Fishing (especially trawling or net fishing); collecting corals/shells; anchoring boats; dumping waste/pollution; development of the coastline. E.g. banning fishing lets fish populations recover and breed; this provides food for other species and fish spread to surrounding areas.
    1 mark each for two restricted activities. 1 mark for a valid explanation of benefit.
Q3Conservation & threats · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The Asiatic cheetah once lived across south-west Asia, including parts of Pakistan and India. Today only a very small population survives, in Iran.

  1. (a) State four reasons why populations and species may become extinct. [4]
  2. (b) Cheetahs feed mainly on gazelles. Suggest how the hunting of gazelles by people could contribute to the decline of the cheetah. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Climate change; competition (e.g. from introduced/invasive species or livestock); hunting/poaching by humans; degradation and loss of habitat.
    1 mark each for any four of: climate change; competition (for food, space – e.g. from other predators, invasive species or livestock); hunting/overexploitation by humans; habitat loss/destruction; habitat degradation/fragmentation; (accept disease, pollution).
  2. (b) Less prey for cheetahs, so they starve or breed less successfully; cheetahs may then kill livestock and be killed by herders.
    1 mark each for any two of: reduced food supply/prey; fewer cubs survive/lower reproduction; cheetahs travel further, more contact with people/roads; attack livestock so are persecuted.
Q4Conservation & threats · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Coral reefs, such as those in the Red Sea and around Astola Island, contain a very high biodiversity. Reef-building corals contain single-celled photosynthetic algae inside their cells.

  1. (a) Explain how a rise in sea temperature caused by climate change can lead to the death of corals. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the death of reef-building corals reduces the number of fish species on the reef. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why making a reef into a marine protected area cannot fully protect it from climate change. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) High temperature causes corals to expel their algae (bleaching); the coral loses the sugars made by algal photosynthesis; if the high temperature lasts, the coral starves and dies.
    B1 corals expel/lose algae (bleaching); B1 algae supply most of the coral's food/organic compounds from photosynthesis; B1 coral starves/is weakened and more likely to die from disease if high temperatures persist.
  2. (b) Corals provide the physical habitat – shelter, breeding sites and food – for many fish; without them niches are lost, so many species leave or die out.
    1 mark each: loss of habitat/shelter/breeding sites; loss of food (coral polyps or organisms that live on coral) / fewer niches.
  3. (c) Sea temperature rise is caused by global greenhouse gas emissions, which cannot be controlled within the boundary of the protected area.
    B1 the cause (warming/acidification) is global and not affected by local rules on fishing or development.
Q5Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Outline the roles of zoos in the conservation of endangered species such as the snow leopard. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why a captive population started from only a few founder animals may have problems after several generations. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Captive breeding to increase numbers; maintaining genetic diversity using studbooks and exchanging animals between zoos (avoiding inbreeding); reintroduction of animals into the wild; research into behaviour, nutrition and reproduction; education of the public and fund-raising for conservation in the wild; keeping a safe population if wild populations are lost.
    1 mark each for any four of: captive breeding; studbooks/exchange to keep genetic diversity; reintroduction; research; education/raising awareness; fund-raising; safe 'reserve' population; assisted reproduction (IVF, AI).
  2. (b) Small gene pool / low genetic diversity; inbreeding increases the chance of offspring being homozygous for harmful recessive alleles; this reduces fertility and survival (inbreeding depression) and the population is less able to adapt to change or disease.
    1 mark: low genetic diversity / small gene pool. 1 mark: inbreeding → more homozygous recessive harmful alleles expressed. 1 mark: reduced fitness/fertility or less able to adapt (e.g. to disease).
Q6Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how in vitro fertilisation (IVF) and embryo transfer could be used to produce offspring of a rare antelope, using a female of a common, closely related species as a surrogate mother. [4]
  2. (b) Explain the advantages of using a surrogate of a common species and of keeping frozen embryos and sperm in a 'frozen zoo'. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Give the rare female hormones to stimulate many eggs to mature (superovulation); collect eggs (from ovaries); collect sperm from a rare male (or use frozen sperm); mix eggs and sperm in a dish (in vitro) for fertilisation; grow embryos for a few days in culture; implant embryo into the uterus of the surrogate female of the common species, whose cycle has been synchronised with hormones.
    1 mark each for any four of: hormones to stimulate egg production; eggs collected; sperm collected / thawed from frozen store; fertilisation in a dish/outside the body; embryos cultured; embryos implanted into surrogate; surrogate's cycle synchronised / prepared with hormones.
  2. (b) Rare females are not put at risk by pregnancy and can produce more eggs/offspring; more offspring per year from each rare female; frozen material can be stored for many years and transported between countries instead of animals; genetic diversity is preserved and can be reintroduced into future populations, even from animals that have died.
    1 mark each for any three of: protects rare female / more offspring; many embryos per rare female; long-term storage; easy transport between zoos/countries; preserves genetic diversity for the future; use material from dead animals.
Q7Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe how seeds are prepared and stored in a seed bank. [3]
  2. (b) Many farmers in northern Pakistan have replaced old local wheat varieties with modern high-yielding varieties. Explain why seeds of the old local varieties are being collected and stored. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Seeds are cleaned and checked; dried to a low water content (about 5 %); sealed in airtight containers; stored at low temperature (about −20 °C); samples are germinated regularly to check viability, and new seed is grown when viability falls.
    1 mark each for any three of: dried to low water content; airtight containers; low temperature (e.g. −20 °C); germination tests at intervals; regrow to collect fresh seed; records kept.
  2. (b) Old varieties contain alleles not found in modern varieties (genetic diversity); some may give resistance to diseases, drought, cold or salinity; these alleles could be bred into future crop varieties as the climate or pathogens change; once the varieties are no longer grown they may be lost for ever.
    1 mark: preserves genetic diversity / alleles. 1 mark: useful traits (e.g. drought or disease resistance). 1 mark: for future breeding / prevent loss (extinction of variety).
Q8Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Outline the role of CITES in conservation. [2]
  2. (b) In one mountain area of Pakistan, a small number of permits to hunt old male markhor are sold each year to foreign hunters. Most of the money goes to the local community, which employs game guards. Suggest how this scheme can help the markhor population to increase. [3]
  3. (c) The markhor's IUCN Red List category improved from Endangered to Near Threatened. State what the IUCN Red List is. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) CITES is an international agreement that regulates or bans international trade in endangered species and products made from them; species are listed in appendices according to how threatened they are (e.g. Appendix I – commercial trade banned; others need permits).
    1 mark: international agreement controlling trade in endangered species / their products. 1 mark: species listed in appendices with different levels of restriction / permits needed.
  2. (b) Local people gain income from markhor, so they value live animals and stop poaching; game guards protect markhor from illegal hunting; only a few old males past their prime breeding age are taken, so the breeding population is hardly affected; money can also be used for habitat protection and community projects.
    1 mark each for any three of: income gives incentive to conserve; reduced poaching / guards employed; only few old males removed so breeding not affected; funds habitat protection; community support for conservation.
  3. (c) A list that assesses and classifies species according to their risk of extinction (e.g. Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable).
    1 mark.
Q9Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The Arabian oryx, a desert antelope, became extinct in the wild in 1972. Animals bred in captivity were later released back into the wild in Oman and other countries.

  1. (a) Outline the steps that should be taken before captive-bred animals are released into the wild. [4]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages of returning captive-bred oryx to the wild rather than keeping the species only in zoos and captive breeding centres. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Make sure the original cause of decline (e.g. hunting) has been removed; check the habitat is suitable with enough food and water; screen animals for disease; choose animals with as much genetic diversity as possible; acclimatise them in a fenced area (soft release); plan monitoring.
    1 mark each for any four of: remove/control the threat that caused extinction (poaching); assess/restore habitat – food, water, space; health checks/quarantine so disease is not introduced; select genetically diverse, unrelated animals (studbook); acclimatise in an enclosure/soft release so they learn to find food; educate/involve local people; fit radio collars/plan monitoring.
  2. (b) In the wild the oryx again fills its niche in the desert ecosystem (grazing, food for predators, seed dispersal); natural selection acts on the wild population so it stays adapted to wild conditions and keeps natural behaviours; a wild population can grow much larger than captive space allows, giving more genetic diversity and less risk from a single disease outbreak or disaster in one zoo.
    1 mark each for any two of: restores the species' role/niche in the ecosystem; natural selection continues so adaptations (and wild behaviour) are kept, not lost by adaptation to captivity; larger population/more genetic diversity than captive space allows; cheaper long-term than captivity; not all eggs in one basket – reduced risk from disease/disaster in one centre.
Q10Conservation & threats · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Cane toads were introduced to north-eastern Australia in 1935 to control beetles that damaged sugar cane. They did not control the beetles but spread across a large part of the country. Their skin contains a poison that kills many native predators that try to eat them.

  1. (a) State what is meant by an invasive alien species. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why cane toads have reduced the populations of some native predators and of some native frogs. [3]
  3. (c) It has been suggested that a disease organism that kills toads should be introduced. Suggest one risk of this method. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A species introduced by humans to an area outside its natural range that spreads and harms native species, ecosystems or the economy.
    B1 non-native/introduced by humans and spreads causing harm.
  2. (b) Predators that eat toads are poisoned and die; toads compete with native frogs for food and breeding sites; toads have no natural predators or diseases in Australia, so their numbers are very large.
    1 mark each: native predators not adapted to the toxin die after eating toads; competition with native frogs for food/space/breeding ponds; no natural predators/parasites to control toad numbers so they reach very high densities (and eat native invertebrates).
  3. (c) The disease organism might also infect and kill native frogs or other species.
    B1 non-target species harmed / the control organism itself becomes invasive / cannot be removed once released.
Q11Conservation & threats · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

Prosopis juliflora, a shrub from Central and South America, was introduced to Pakistan to provide fuel wood and to stabilise sand. It has since spread over large areas of grazing land and river banks in Sindh.

  1. (a) Suggest why Prosopis juliflora has been able to spread so rapidly in Sindh. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how the spread of an invasive species such as P. juliflora can reduce biodiversity. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest two methods that could be used to control P. juliflora and give one disadvantage of one of them. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) No natural predators, herbivores or diseases that control it in its new range; it tolerates drought and salty soils better than native plants; it produces many seeds that are spread by livestock eating the pods; it outcompetes native plants for water and light.
    1 mark each for any three of: lack of natural predators/pathogens; tolerance of drought/salinity; large seed production; seeds dispersed by animals; outcompetes native species (deep roots for water, shading).
  2. (b) It outcompetes native plants, which decline or disappear locally; animals that depend on those plants for food or habitat also decline, so species diversity (and the variety of habitats) falls.
    1 mark: native plants outcompeted / lost. 1 mark: knock-on loss of animals that depend on them / habitat becomes uniform.
  3. (c) Mechanical removal (uprooting/cutting); herbicides; using the wood for fuel/charcoal to encourage harvesting; introducing a biological control agent (seed-eating insect) after testing; replanting native species. Disadvantage: e.g. herbicides may kill native plants or pollute water; cutting is labour-intensive and stumps resprout; a biological control agent might attack native species.
    1 mark each for two methods. 1 mark for a valid disadvantage of one of them.
Q12Conservation & threats · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the elephant population in a fenced reserve may need to be controlled. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of culling and of contraception as methods of controlling the population. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) With no natural predators and no way to migrate out of the reserve, the population grows beyond the carrying capacity; elephants destroy trees and vegetation, reducing habitat and food for other species (and for themselves), lowering biodiversity.
    1 mark: population exceeds carrying capacity / no predators or migration. 1 mark: overgrazing / habitat damage reduces biodiversity.
  2. (b) Culling: reduces numbers quickly; meat/ivory/hide may provide income; but it is unpopular/ethically controversial, disturbs social groups and causes stress to remaining animals. Contraception (e.g. immunocontraceptive vaccine darted into females): humane and reversible, keeps social groups intact; but slow to reduce numbers (elephants are long-lived), must be repeated, expensive and needs each female to be identified and treated.
    1 mark each for any four, with at least one advantage and one disadvantage for each method: culling fast / income vs ethical objection / social disruption; contraception humane / reversible / groups intact vs slow effect / repeated treatment / cost / labour.
Q13Conservation & threats · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Forty captive-bred gazelles were released into a protected desert reserve in 2014. The population was counted every two years.Year: 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022, 2024Number: 40, 52, 71, 66, 94, 118

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in population from release (2014) to 2024. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest reasons for the fall in numbers between 2018 and 2020. [2]
  3. (c) All 40 animals released came from one captive population. Discuss whether this reintroduction can be considered a long-term success. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 195 %
    M1: (118 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100. A1: 195 %.
  2. (b) Drought/reduced food or water; disease; predation; poaching; animals moving out of the reserve.
    1 mark each for any two.
  3. (c) Success: population has nearly tripled and is breeding in the wild. Concerns: all descended from 40 related animals, so genetic diversity is low – risk of inbreeding and less ability to adapt to disease or climate change; numbers fluctuate and the population is still small; long-term success needs continued monitoring, protection and perhaps introduction of unrelated animals.
    1 mark: evidence of success (increase, breeding). 1 mark: low genetic diversity / founder effect / inbreeding risk. 1 mark: still small/fluctuating, needs further protection or new genetic stock.
Q14Conservation & threats · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A seed bank stores seeds of a wild relative of wheat collected in Balochistan. Seeds are dried and kept at −20 °C. Every 5 years a sample of 200 seeds is removed and germinated.Years in storage: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20Seeds germinated (out of 200): 188, 183, 176, 161, 142

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage germination after 0 years and after 20 years. [2]
  2. (b) The seed bank regrows a fresh batch of seeds when the percentage germination falls below 85 % of its value at the start. Use the data to state the first test at which this happens. Show your working. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the seeds are dried and kept at −20 °C. [2]
  4. (d) Seeds of mango cannot be stored in this way. Suggest why, and suggest how the genetic diversity of mango varieties could be conserved instead. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 94.0 % and 71.0 %
    M1 germinated ÷ 200 × 100; A1 94.0 % and 71.0 %.
  2. (b) 20 years: 85 % of 94.0 % = 79.9 %; at 15 years germination is 80.5 % (above), at 20 years it is 71.0 % (below).
    M1 0.85 × 94.0 = 79.9 %; A1 year 20 (80.5 % at year 15 is still above the limit).
  3. (c) Low water content and low temperature slow metabolism/enzyme activity, so stored food is used up very slowly and seeds stay viable for longer; drying also prevents ice crystals damaging cells and stops fungal growth.
    1 mark each for any two of: reduces respiration/metabolic rate/enzyme activity; reserves last longer so seeds remain viable; dry seeds do not form ice crystals that damage cells when frozen; prevents growth of fungi/bacteria/germination in store.
  4. (d) Mango seeds are killed by drying (they need a high water content); conserve as living trees in field gene banks/orchards, or freeze embryos or tissue in liquid nitrogen (cryopreservation).
    B1 seeds die/lose viability when dried (recalcitrant); B1 field gene bank of living trees / tissue culture / cryopreservation of embryos in liquid nitrogen.
Q15Conservation & threats · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A large forest in South Asia has been cleared for farms and roads, leaving four separate forest fragments. Leopards live in all four fragments but cannot cross the farmland safely. Geneticists measured genetic diversity (mean heterozygosity) in the leopards: 0.62 in a large, unbroken forest elsewhere and 0.41 in the fragments.

  1. (a) Calculate how much lower, as a percentage, genetic diversity is in the fragments than in the unbroken forest. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why small, isolated populations such as these have a high risk of extinction. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest how planting forest corridors between the fragments would help to conserve the leopards. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 33.9 %
    M1 (0.62 − 0.41) ÷ 0.62 × 100; A1 33.9 %.

  1. (a) Use the data to evaluate the two hypotheses. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest how the mesh itself could have affected the results, and how this could be tested. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) H2 is strongly supported: excluding crabs more than doubles survival (34 → 81 % at low salinity, 30 → 76 % at high salinity, an increase of about 46–47 percentage points); H1 is only weakly supported: high salinity lowers survival by only 4–5 percentage points, with or without crabs; crabs are the main cause of death, though salinity may have a small effect that needs a statistical test.
    B1 crab exclusion large increase, with figures; B1 H2 supported; B1 salinity small effect, with figures; B1 H1 weakly supported/may not be significant – need replicates/statistics.
  2. (b) Mesh may shade seedlings or reduce wave damage/trap sediment; test with a 'partial cage' control – mesh with gaps large enough for crabs to enter, so the only difference is crab access.
    B1 mesh alters other conditions (shading, water flow, sediment); B1 control of mesh with openings/roof-only cage.

Frogs from two neighbouring valleys look identical but their mating calls are different. A biologist proposes the hypothesis that they are two separate species.

  1. (a) Make three predictions that would be expected to be true if the hypothesis is correct. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a result that agrees with a prediction does not prove the hypothesis. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Females will respond (move towards) only to calls of males from their own valley; in mixed captive groups they will not interbreed, or hybrids will be infertile/inviable; DNA sequences (e.g. mitochondrial genes) will differ more between the valleys than within each valley, by an amount similar to that between other recognised frog species.
    1 mark each for any three testable predictions: call preference/behavioural isolation; no interbreeding or infertile hybrids; greater genetic difference between than within populations; no gene flow where ranges meet.
  2. (b) The result could have another explanation (e.g. call differences due to local learning or temperature, or populations recently separated but still able to interbreed); further evidence could still contradict the hypothesis, so it is supported, not proved.
    B1 alternative explanations possible; B1 hypotheses can only be supported/falsified, not proved.

A botanist has records of the altitudes at which an alpine flower was found on a mountain in Gilgit-Baltistan in 1975. She will survey the mountain again this year to test the hypothesis that climate warming has caused the species to move to higher altitudes.

  1. (a) State a null hypothesis and two predictions that follow from the hypothesis. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest two reasons, other than climate warming, why the survey might find the flower at higher altitudes. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one additional piece of evidence that would strengthen the link with climate warming. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Null: there is no difference between the altitudes at which the flower is found in 1975 and now. Predictions: the lowest altitude at which the species grows is higher now than in 1975; the mean (or upper limit) altitude of the population is higher now.
    B1 null; 1 mark each for two predictions about lower limit, mean or upper limit.
  2. (b) Grazing or land use change has removed it from lower slopes; the new survey may search higher areas more thoroughly (different sampling effort/methods); trampling or collecting at lower, more accessible sites.
    1 mark each for any two.
  3. (c) Temperature records showing warming over the same period, or similar upward shifts in many other species on the same and other mountains.

In green turtles, eggs incubated at higher sand temperatures produce more female hatchlings. A conservation team is concerned that shade from new beach buildings will cool some nesting sites.

  1. (a) State a hypothesis about the effect of shading on the sex ratio of hatchlings and a prediction that could be tested at the hatchery. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the team should record sand temperature in every nest, not just whether it is shaded. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why, for conservation, a population producing almost only female hatchlings could be a problem. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hypothesis: shaded nests have a lower sand temperature, so they produce a lower proportion of female hatchlings. Prediction: if half of a set of clutches is incubated under shade cloth, those nests will have a lower mean temperature and fewer female hatchlings than unshaded nests.
    B1 hypothesis linking shade, temperature and sex ratio; B1 testable prediction.
  2. (b) Temperature is the actual factor that determines sex; shade may affect other factors (moisture), and temperature varies between nests for other reasons, so recording it allows the relationship between temperature and sex ratio to be analysed directly.
    B1 temperature is the causal variable; B1 shade is only an indirect/variable proxy – allows correlation analysis.
  3. (c) Too few males for mating in future, reducing reproductive success and genetic diversity.
