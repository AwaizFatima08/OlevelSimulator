Q1Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the structure of an antibody molecule. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how antibodies cause bacteria to clump together (agglutination) and why this helps to protect the body. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Four polypeptide chains: two heavy and two light; held together by disulfide bonds; variable regions at the tips form two antigen-binding sites; constant region (same in all antibodies of that class); hinge region gives flexibility
    1 mark each for any four of: four polypeptides; two heavy and two light chains; disulfide bonds; variable region(s); antigen-binding sites (two); constant region; hinge region; Y-shape.
  2. (b) Each antibody has two antigen-binding sites so it can bind to antigens on two different bacteria, linking many together; clumps cannot spread easily and are easier for phagocytes to engulf
    1 mark: two binding sites link several bacteria. 1 mark: clumped bacteria easily engulfed by phagocytes / cannot spread/enter cells.
Q2Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which cells secrete antibodies?A plasma cellsB T-helper cellsC memory T-cellsD macrophages [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A plasma cells
    Plasma cells are differentiated B-lymphocytes that make and secrete antibodies. T-helper cells (B) release cytokines that stimulate B-lymphocytes, but do not make antibodies themselves.
Q3Lymphocytes & antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Complete the comparison of B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes by stating, for each type: (i) where it matures, (ii) one way in which it helps to destroy pathogens. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) B-lymphocytes: mature in bone marrow; become plasma cells that secrete antibodies (humoral response). T-lymphocytes: mature in the thymus; T-helper cells release cytokines / T-killer (cytotoxic) cells destroy infected cells
    1 mark each: B – bone marrow; B – antibodies via plasma cells; T – thymus; T – cytokines from T-helper cells or killing infected cells by T-killer cells.
Q4Lymphocytes & antibodies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how B-lymphocytes respond during the primary immune response to a bacterium that the body has never met before. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Only a B-lymphocyte with receptors (antibody) complementary to the antigen binds to it (clonal selection); stimulated by cytokines from T-helper cells; divides by mitosis to form a clone (clonal expansion); some cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies; others become memory cells
    1 mark each for any five of: specific B-cell receptor binds antigen; clonal selection; activation by T-helper cells/cytokines; mitosis/clonal expansion; plasma cells; secrete antibodies specific to antigen; memory cells formed; response is slow (several days).
Q5Lymphocytes & antibodies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A volunteer received an injection of a harmless antigen on day 0 and the same antigen again on day 28. Antibody concentration in the blood (arbitrary units, a.u.):day 0: 0 | day 7: 10 | day 14: 40 | day 21: 15 | day 28: 12 | day 35: 350 | day 42: 400

  1. (a) Compare the antibody response to the first exposure with the response to the second exposure. Use the data in your answer. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate how many times greater the peak antibody concentration was after the second exposure. [1]
  3. (c) Explain the difference between the two responses. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Second response is faster (high levels within 7 days of the second exposure v about 14 days); much larger (peak 400 v 40 a.u.); lasts longer / antibody stays high
    1 mark each for any three, at least one with data: faster; greater; longer-lasting; quoted values (e.g. 400 v 40, peak at day 42 v day 14).
  2. (b) 10 times
    400 ÷ 40 = 10.
  3. (c) Memory cells were made during the first response; on second exposure memory B-cells recognise the antigen quickly; they divide rapidly to produce many plasma cells, so more antibody is made sooner
    1 mark each: memory cells formed in primary response; memory cells remain in blood/long-lived; rapid clonal expansion/differentiation into plasma cells on second exposure.
Q6Lymphocytes & antibodies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Outline the roles of T-helper cells and T-killer (cytotoxic) cells in the immune response. [4]
  2. (b) HIV infects and destroys T-helper cells. Explain why people with untreated HIV infection become ill with other infections such as tuberculosis. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) T-helper cells: bind to antigen presented by macrophages; release cytokines; stimulate B-cells to divide/become plasma cells and stimulate macrophages/T-killer cells. T-killer cells: recognise antigens on the surface of infected cells; attach and release substances (e.g. perforin/toxins) that kill the infected cell
    Max 2 for T-helper: recognise antigen-presenting cells; secrete cytokines; activate B-cells/T-killer cells/phagocytes. Max 2 for T-killer: bind infected/cancer cells with matching antigen; secrete toxins/perforin making holes; destroy infected cells.
  2. (b) Fewer T-helper cells so B-cells are not stimulated to make antibodies and T-killer cells are not activated; the immune system cannot respond effectively to other pathogens (opportunistic infections)
    1 mark: less activation of B-cells/T-killer cells/phagocytes. 1 mark: weakened immune response so other pathogens not destroyed.
Q7Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by an autoimmune disease. [2]
  2. (b) In myasthenia gravis, antibodies bind to acetylcholine receptors on the muscle cell membranes at neuromuscular junctions. Suggest how this causes muscle weakness. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The immune system fails to distinguish self from non-self; it makes antibodies/T-cells against the body's own antigens and attacks its own cells
    1 mark: immune system attacks body's own cells/tissues. 1 mark: self antigens treated as non-self / lymphocytes not removed during maturation.
  2. (b) Antibodies block receptor binding sites so acetylcholine cannot bind; receptors may also be destroyed/removed; fewer sodium ion channels open so less depolarisation of the muscle membrane; fewer action potentials so muscle contracts weakly
    1 mark each for any three of: antibodies block/compete with acetylcholine; receptors destroyed/fewer receptors; less depolarisation (fewer Na⁺ channels open); threshold not reached / fewer impulses in muscle fibre; weaker contraction.
Q8Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain why an antibody that binds to an antigen on the surface of the measles virus cannot bind to an antigen on the mumps virus. [3]
  2. (b) Some antibodies neutralise bacterial toxins. Suggest how binding of an antibody to a toxin molecule prevents it harming cells. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The antigen-binding site (variable region) has a specific shape determined by its amino acid sequence; it is complementary only to the shape of the measles antigen; the mumps antigen has a different shape so it cannot fit/bind
    1 mark each: variable region/antigen-binding site; specific amino acid sequence gives specific tertiary structure/shape; complementary to one antigen only (mumps antigen different shape). Do not credit 'active site' or 'receptor'.
  2. (b) The antibody covers the part of the toxin that binds to cell receptors / changes its shape, so it cannot attach to or enter body cells; antibody–toxin complexes are removed by phagocytes
    1 mark: blocks toxin's binding site/prevents entry into cells. 1 mark: complex engulfed by phagocytes.
Q9Lymphocytes & antibodies · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) During their development, some lymphocytes are found to have receptors that bind to the body's own (self) antigens. What happens to these lymphocytes?A They are destroyed or made inactive.B They develop into memory cells.C They divide to form a clone of plasma cells.D They become T-helper cells. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A They are destroyed or made inactive.
    Lymphocytes that react with self antigens are removed (by programmed cell death) so that the immune system does not attack the body. If they became memory or plasma cells (B, C), an autoimmune response would result.

(a) Classify each example as natural active, natural passive, artificial active or artificial passive immunity:(i) a baby receives antibodies in its mother's breast milk(ii) a child is given oral polio vaccine drops(iii) a man recovers from measles and does not catch it again(iv) a farmer in Sindh bitten by a snake is given an injection of antivenom [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) natural passive; (ii) artificial active; (iii) natural active; (iv) artificial passive
    1 mark each.
Q11Vaccination & types of immunity · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which would give long-term immunity to measles?A an injection of an attenuated (weakened) measles virus vaccineB an injection of antibodies against measlesC antibodies crossing the placenta from an immune motherD a course of antibiotics [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A an injection of an attenuated (weakened) measles virus vaccine
    A vaccine stimulates an active immune response, producing memory cells that last for years. Injected or placental antibodies (B, C) give passive immunity, which lasts only weeks because no memory cells are made; antibiotics do not affect viruses.
Q12Vaccination & types of immunity · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how vaccination provides protection against a disease. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The vaccine contains antigens (dead/weakened pathogen, toxoid or parts of the pathogen); it stimulates a primary immune response; B- and T-lymphocytes specific to the antigen form clones and memory cells; on infection memory cells give a fast secondary response, destroying the pathogen before symptoms appear
    1 mark each for any four of: vaccine contains antigens; specific lymphocytes activated/clonal selection; antibodies produced; memory cells formed; secondary response faster and greater on infection; pathogen destroyed before causing disease.
Q13Vaccination & types of immunity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A district health office ran a measles vaccination campaign. Before the campaign there were 840 cases per year. Two years later, with 95 % of children vaccinated, there were 63 cases per year.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage decrease in measles cases. [2]
  2. (b) Some children in the district were too young or too ill to be vaccinated, but their risk of catching measles still fell. Explain why. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest two reasons why a few vaccinated children still caught measles. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 92.5 %
    M1: 840 − 63 = 777. A1: 777 ÷ 840 × 100 = 92.5 %.
  2. (b) Herd immunity: so many people are immune that the virus has few susceptible hosts; chains of transmission are broken; so unvaccinated individuals are less likely to meet an infected person
    1 mark each: herd immunity (named or described); fewer infected people/carriers to pass on the virus; transmission chain broken so contact with infected people unlikely.
  3. (c) Some people do not respond fully to the vaccine (e.g. poor immune response, malnutrition); vaccine may have been stored incorrectly (cold chain broken); only one dose received; vaccinated too young while maternal antibodies were present
    1 mark each for any two sensible reasons.
Q14Vaccination & types of immunity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Smallpox was eradicated worldwide by 1980. Explain the features of smallpox and its vaccine that made eradication possible. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why it has been much more difficult to develop a highly effective vaccine against malaria. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The virus had stable antigens (did not mutate much), so one vaccine worked everywhere; the vaccine was live, effective and gave long-lasting immunity; infected people were easy to identify (clear symptoms) so cases could be isolated and contacts vaccinated (ring vaccination); no animal reservoir / only infected humans; vaccine was heat-stable/freeze-dried and easy to give
    1 mark each for any four of: little antigenic variation; effective live vaccine; long-lasting immunity; easy to identify cases; ring vaccination/isolation; no animal reservoir; heat-stable vaccine.
  2. (b) Plasmodium is a eukaryote with many antigens and several stages in its life cycle; it hides inside liver cells and red blood cells, and shows antigenic variation, so antibodies cannot reach it easily
    1 mark each for any two of: different antigens at different life-cycle stages; intracellular (liver/red blood cells) so hidden from antibodies; antigenic variation; many strains.
Q15Vaccination & types of immunity · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A newborn baby has antibodies against tetanus that came from its mother across the placenta. Why does this protection last for only a few weeks?A The baby makes no memory cells and the antibodies are gradually broken down.B The baby's memory cells die soon after birth.C The antibodies are destroyed by the baby's plasma cells.D The antibodies are digested in the baby's stomach. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A The baby makes no memory cells and the antibodies are gradually broken down.
    This is natural passive immunity: the antibodies were made by the mother, so the baby has no memory cells for tetanus and the antibodies are broken down. B is wrong because no memory cells were formed in the first place.
Q16Vaccination & types of immunity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A new influenza vaccine is produced every year. Explain why. [3]
  2. (b) Some vaccines contain mRNA coding for a viral surface protein, enclosed in lipid droplets. Explain how such a vaccine leads to immunity. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The influenza virus mutates frequently, changing its surface antigens (antigenic variation/drift); memory cells and antibodies from earlier vaccines are not complementary to the new antigens; so a new vaccine matching the current strains is needed
    1 mark each: mutation/antigenic variation; new antigen shapes not recognised by existing memory cells/antibodies; vaccine updated to match strains expected that season.
  2. (b) Lipid droplets fuse with/enter body cells; the mRNA is translated by the cell's ribosomes to make the viral protein (antigen); this protein is displayed on the cell surface/released, triggering a primary immune response with production of antibodies and memory cells
    1 mark each: mRNA enters cells; translated to produce viral antigen; antigen presented/displayed and activates lymphocytes, forming memory cells (and antibodies).
Q17Vaccination & types of immunity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) A boy in Multan is bitten by a dog that may have rabies. Doctors inject both rabies antibodies and rabies vaccine. Explain the reason for giving both. [4]
  2. (b) The rabies virus travels slowly along nerves from the bite to the brain, which can take weeks. Suggest why this makes vaccination after the bite effective. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The antibodies give immediate (artificial passive) protection by binding to/neutralising the virus straight away; but they are broken down within weeks and no memory cells form; the vaccine stimulates artificial active immunity, but antibodies take a week or more to appear; the vaccine gives long-term protection through memory cells
    1 mark each: antibodies act immediately (passive); antibodies short-lived/no memory cells; vaccine stimulates own immune response (active) but slower; vaccine gives memory cells/long-term protection.
  2. (b) There is time for the primary immune response to produce antibodies and T-cells before the virus reaches the brain; so the virus is destroyed before it causes disease
    1 mark: long incubation period gives time for primary response/antibody production. 1 mark: virus neutralised before reaching the central nervous system.
Q18Monoclonal antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced using the hybridoma method. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inject a mouse with the antigen; mouse makes B-lymphocytes/plasma cells specific to the antigen; remove B-cells from the spleen; fuse them with myeloma (cancer) cells to form hybridoma cells; select hybridomas making the required antibody; clone these cells and culture them; collect and purify antibody
    1 mark each for any five of: antigen injected into mouse; B-lymphocytes/plasma cells taken from spleen; fused with myeloma cells; using polyethylene glycol/electric shock; hybridoma cells; screened for correct antibody; cloned/cultured (divide indefinitely); antibody harvested.
Q19Monoclonal antibodies · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Why are B-lymphocytes fused with myeloma cells when producing monoclonal antibodies?A Myeloma cells can divide indefinitely.B Myeloma cells make large quantities of antibody of their own.C Myeloma cells provide the specificity of the antibody.D Myeloma cells prevent the mouse rejecting the antibody. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Myeloma cells can divide indefinitely.
    B-lymphocytes do not divide in culture; fusing them with cancerous myeloma cells gives hybridomas that both divide continuously and secrete the specific antibody. The specificity comes from the B-lymphocyte, not the myeloma cell (C).
Q20Monoclonal antibodies · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A home pregnancy test strip detects the hormone hCG in urine. Near the bottom of the strip are mobile monoclonal antibodies against hCG, each attached to a coloured bead. Further up are a test line of immobilised antibodies against hCG and, above it, a control line of immobilised antibodies.

  1. (a) Explain how a positive result appears at the test line. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why a coloured line should always appear at the control line, whether or not the woman is pregnant. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) hCG in the urine binds to the mobile antibodies, which carry coloured beads/dye; the hCG–antibody complex moves up the strip with the urine; at the test line, immobilised antibodies that are also specific to hCG bind the complex; coloured beads build up so a coloured line appears
    1 mark each: hCG binds to mobile (labelled) antibody; complex moves up strip; immobilised antibody at test line binds hCG (at a different site); colour accumulates at test line.
  2. (b) Immobilised antibodies at the control line bind to the mobile antibody itself (not to hCG); so mobile antibodies with coloured beads always collect there, showing the urine has moved up the strip and the test has worked
    1 mark: control antibodies bind the mobile antibodies (with or without hCG). 1 mark: shows test is working / liquid has flowed past test line.
Q21Monoclonal antibodies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Trastuzumab is a monoclonal antibody used to treat some breast cancers. It binds to a receptor protein (HER2) found in very large numbers on the surface of these cancer cells. Explain why trastuzumab binds to the cancer cells but rarely to other cells. [2]
  2. (b) Monoclonal antibodies for treatment are usually 'humanised'. Explain why antibodies made entirely by mouse cells are not suitable for repeated treatment. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why patients need repeated doses of trastuzumab over several months. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Its antigen-binding sites are complementary to HER2 only; normal cells have few HER2 receptors, so little binding occurs
    1 mark: specific/complementary binding site to HER2. 1 mark: HER2 present in large numbers only on these cancer cells.
  2. (b) Mouse antibodies have mouse-specific (constant region) amino acid sequences that act as foreign antigens; the patient's immune system makes antibodies against them, destroying them / causing an allergic reaction, so later doses work less well
    1 mark: mouse antibodies are non-self/antigenic. 1 mark: patient produces an immune response (antibodies) that removes them or causes side-effects.
  3. (c) Antibodies are proteins that are broken down in the body over time; cancer cells continue to be present and divide, so the antibody concentration must be kept high
    1 mark: antibodies broken down/removed (no memory cells, passive). 1 mark: need to keep binding to cancer cells as they continue to divide.
Q22Monoclonal antibodies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A rapid test for dengue fever uses a monoclonal antibody against a dengue virus protein (NS1). In a hospital in Lahore it was tested on 400 patients. A laboratory method showed that 150 patients were infected and 250 were not.Of the 150 infected patients, 138 tested positive.Of the 250 uninfected patients, 10 tested positive.

  1. (a) Calculate (i) the percentage of infected patients correctly identified by the test (its sensitivity), (ii) the percentage of uninfected patients correctly identified as negative (its specificity). [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why some uninfected patients tested positive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) 92 %; (ii) 96 %
    (i) 138 ÷ 150 × 100 = 92 % (2 marks; M1 correct division). (ii) uninfected testing negative = 250 − 10 = 240; 240 ÷ 250 × 100 = 96 % (1 mark).
  2. (b) The monoclonal antibody may bind to a similar antigen from another related virus (cross-reaction), e.g. a protein of shape similar to NS1; or contamination/misreading of a faint line
    1 mark: antibody binds a different antigen of similar shape (cross-reactivity). 1 mark: e.g. related virus such as Zika / or a technical error.
Q23Monoclonal antibodies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which statement about a sample of one type of monoclonal antibody is correct?A All the molecules have identical antigen-binding sites.B The molecules are made by many different clones of B-lymphocytes.C The molecules bind to several different antigens.D The molecules have different constant regions. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A All the molecules have identical antigen-binding sites.
    Monoclonal antibodies come from a single clone of hybridoma cells, so every molecule is identical and binds the same antigen. B describes polyclonal antibodies produced in a normal immune response.
Q24Monoclonal antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A sandwich ELISA test detects a virus antigen in blood serum. A monoclonal antibody against the antigen is fixed to the bottom of a plastic well. The serum is added, then the well is washed. A second monoclonal antibody, which binds to a different part of the antigen and is linked to an enzyme, is added; the well is washed again. Finally a colourless substrate is added that the enzyme converts to a coloured product.

  1. (a) Explain why the wells must be washed after the sample is added, and again after the second antibody is added. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the intensity of colour indicates the amount of antigen in the sample. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the two antibodies used must bind to different parts (epitopes) of the antigen. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest a suitable control well for this test. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Washing removes any unbound antigen/proteins, and then any unbound enzyme-linked antibody; otherwise unbound enzyme would cause a colour change even when no antigen is present (false positive)
    1 mark: removes unbound molecules (sample proteins/second antibody). 1 mark: prevents colour change not caused by antigen / false positive.
  2. (b) The second antibody can only stay in the well if it is bound to antigen held by the first antibody; more antigen means more enzyme bound, so more substrate is converted to coloured product
    1 mark: second (enzyme-linked) antibody binds only where antigen is captured. 1 mark: more enzyme → more coloured product.
  3. (c) If both bound the same site, the first (fixed) antibody would block it so the second antibody could not bind
    Second antibody must bind while the antigen is held by the first.
  4. (d) A well with a sample known to contain no antigen (e.g. buffer or serum from an uninfected person), treated in the same way
    Negative control with no antigen (or a positive control with known antigen).
Q25Monoclonal antibodies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

After a single dose, the concentration of a humanised therapeutic antibody in a patient's blood was 160 µg cm⁻³. Its concentration halves every 21 days.

  1. (a) Calculate the concentration remaining 63 days after the dose. [2]
  2. (b) The same antibody made entirely by mouse cells had a much shorter half-life in patients after the first few doses. Suggest why. [2]
  3. (c) Outline how scientists 'humanise' a mouse monoclonal antibody. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20 µg cm⁻³
    M1: 63 ÷ 21 = 3 half-lives. A1: 160 × (½)³ = 20 µg cm⁻³.
  2. (b) Patient's immune system recognised the mouse antibody as foreign/non-self; antibodies against it were produced and the complexes were removed by phagocytes, so it disappeared faster
    1 mark: mouse protein is antigenic / triggers immune response. 1 mark: anti-mouse antibodies bind and remove it (phagocytosis) more quickly.
  3. (c) Genetic engineering: keep the mouse variable regions (antigen-binding sites) but replace the constant regions (and most other parts) with human antibody sequences
    1 mark: variable/antigen-binding region from mouse retained. 1 mark: rest/constant region changed to human sequence (by altering the genes).
Q26Phagocytes & the immune response · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a neutrophil destroys a bacterium by phagocytosis. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Neutrophil is attracted to the bacterium (by chemicals/chemotaxis); receptors on its membrane bind to the bacterium (or to antibodies on it); it engulfs the bacterium by endocytosis, forming a phagocytic vacuole; lysosomes fuse with the vacuole and hydrolytic enzymes digest the bacterium
    1 mark each for any four of: chemotaxis/moves towards bacterium; attachment/recognition; engulfing by cell surface membrane/pseudopodia (endocytosis); phagocytic vacuole (phagosome); lysosomes fuse; hydrolytic enzymes digest/hydrolyse bacterium.
Q27Phagocytes & the immune response · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which row correctly compares neutrophils and macrophages?A neutrophils are short-lived and arrive first; macrophages are long-lived and act as antigen-presenting cellsB neutrophils are long-lived and present antigens; macrophages are short-lived and arrive firstC neutrophils secrete antibodies; macrophages engulf bacteriaD neutrophils have a round nucleus; macrophages have a lobed nucleus [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A neutrophils are short-lived and arrive first; macrophages are long-lived and act as antigen-presenting cells
    Neutrophils are the first, short-lived phagocytes to reach an infection; macrophages live longer and present antigens to lymphocytes. Neither secretes antibodies (C), and it is the neutrophil that has a lobed nucleus (D).
Q28Phagocytes & the immune response · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State where phagocytes are produced. [1]
  2. (b) A blood smear is viewed with a light microscope. State one visible feature that identifies a cell as a neutrophil. [1]
  3. (c) Explain how macrophages help to activate the specific immune response. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bone marrow
    Bone marrow.
  2. (b) Lobed (multi-lobed) nucleus / granular cytoplasm
    Lobed nucleus (accept 3–5 lobes) or granules in cytoplasm.
  3. (c) Macrophages engulf and partly digest pathogens; they display the pathogen's antigens on their cell surface membrane (antigen presentation); T-helper cells with complementary receptors bind and are activated, then stimulate B-cells/T-killer cells
    1 mark each: phagocytosis/digestion of pathogen; antigens displayed on the surface (antigen-presenting cell); activation of specific T-helper lymphocytes (which then activate B-cells).
Q29Phagocytes & the immune response · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A patient with a bacterial skin infection had blood tests. Neutrophil counts (× 10⁹ per dm³):0 h: 4.0 | 6 h: 9.5 | 24 h: 11.2 | 48 h: 10.1 | 96 h: 5.3

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in neutrophil count between 0 h and 6 h. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the number of neutrophils in the blood increases rapidly after infection. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the neutrophil count falls again after 48 h. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 138 %
    M1: (9.5 − 4.0) ÷ 4.0 × 100. A1: 137.5 → 138 %.
  2. (b) Chemicals (cytokines) released from infected tissue/immune cells stimulate the bone marrow to release (and make) more neutrophils; more phagocytes are needed to engulf the bacteria
    1 mark: chemical signals (cytokines/histamine) released at infection. 1 mark: bone marrow releases/produces more neutrophils.
  3. (c) Bacteria have been destroyed so fewer signals; neutrophils are short-lived and die
    Infection controlled / neutrophils short-lived.
Q30Phagocytes & the immune response · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Mycobacterium tuberculosis is engulfed by macrophages in the lungs but is not killed. It prevents lysosomes from fusing with the phagocytic vacuole. Explain how this allows the bacterium to survive. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why antibodies are not effective at destroying Mycobacterium tuberculosis once it is inside macrophages. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Hydrolytic enzymes from lysosomes are not released into the vacuole; so the bacterium is not digested; it can remain/divide inside the macrophage
    1 mark each: no lysosomal enzymes in vacuole; bacterium not hydrolysed/digested; survives and multiplies inside macrophage (protected).
  2. (b) Antibodies are large proteins that stay outside cells/in blood and tissue fluid; they cannot cross the cell surface membrane to reach bacteria inside the vacuole
    1 mark: antibodies cannot enter cells. 1 mark: bacteria hidden from antibodies inside the macrophage (needs T-cell response instead).
