Q1Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which tissue is present in the wall of the trachea but absent from the wall of a bronchiole? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B cartilage
    Bronchioles have no cartilage; their walls are mainly smooth muscle and elastic fibres, and larger bronchioles are still lined by ciliated epithelium. Smooth muscle (A) and elastic fibres (D) occur in both.
Q2Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is the function of the elastic fibres in the walls of the airways and alveoli? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B to stretch during inspiration and recoil during expiration, helping to push air out
    Elastic fibres stretch as the lungs fill and recoil to help expel air. Contraction (A) is done by smooth muscle; keeping the trachea open (D) is the role of cartilage.
Q3Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the cartilage differs in the trachea, the bronchi and the bronchioles. [3]
  2. (b) Explain the function of the cartilage in the trachea. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Trachea: C-shaped rings of cartilage. Bronchi: irregular plates/blocks of cartilage. Bronchioles: no cartilage.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) It supports the trachea and keeps it open, preventing it collapsing when air pressure inside falls during inspiration.
    1 mark: support / keeps airway open. 1 mark: prevents collapse when pressure falls (inspiration).
Q4Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe how goblet cells and ciliated cells work together to protect the lungs from infection. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why there are no goblet cells or cilia in the alveoli. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Goblet cells secrete mucus, which traps dust, bacteria and viruses; cilia on ciliated cells beat in a coordinated (wave-like) way; they move the mucus up towards the throat (larynx) where it is swallowed, so pathogens are removed before reaching the alveoli.
    1 mark: goblet cells secrete mucus that traps particles/pathogens. 1 mark: cilia beat (in waves/synchrony). 1 mark: mucus moved up to throat and swallowed / away from alveoli.
  2. (b) A layer of mucus or cilia would increase the diffusion distance (and ciliated cells are thicker than squamous cells), slowing gas exchange; mucus could also fill the small air spaces.
    1 mark: would increase diffusion distance / thicker barrier. 1 mark: would reduce rate of gas exchange / block air spaces.

Q6Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the cilia in the trachea move mucus. [2]
  2. (b) Whooping cough is caused by bacteria that release a toxin which damages ciliated cells. Suggest why people with whooping cough cough violently and often. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The cilia beat (move back and forth) in a coordinated, wave-like rhythm; this sweeps the mucus upwards towards the larynx/throat.
    1 mark: cilia beat in coordinated waves. 1 mark: move mucus upward / towards throat.
  2. (b) Cilia no longer move mucus, so mucus builds up in the airways; coughing is the only way to remove it.
    1 mark: mucus accumulates because cilia damaged. 1 mark: coughing needed to clear the mucus/airways.
Q7Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which type of tissue forms most of the lining of the alveoli? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B squamous epithelium
    Alveoli are lined by very thin, flattened squamous epithelial cells, giving a short diffusion distance. Ciliated epithelium (A) lines the trachea, bronchi and larger bronchioles but not the alveoli.
Q8Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A child inhales a small bead that completely blocks the right main bronchus. Suggest and explain the effect on gas exchange in the alveoli of the right lung. [2]
  2. (b) During exercise, the smooth muscle in the walls of the bronchioles relaxes. Describe and explain the effect on air flow to the alveoli. [2]
  3. (c) Name the tissue that allows the alveoli to return to their original size during expiration. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Alveoli of the right lung are no longer ventilated; oxygen in them is used up / carbon dioxide builds up, so the concentration gradients disappear and little or no gas exchange occurs there (the left lung still works)
    1 mark: no fresh air reaches right-lung alveoli / no ventilation. 1 mark: gradients for O₂ and CO₂ fall → gas exchange stops/greatly reduced.
  2. (b) Bronchioles widen (lumen diameter increases); less resistance so more air reaches the alveoli per minute
    1 mark: airways dilate/widen. 1 mark: lower resistance → increased air flow/ventilation.
  3. (c) Elastic fibres
    1 mark.
Q9Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In the human lungs, each airway divides into two smaller airways. The trachea is generation 0, the two main bronchi are generation 1, and so on. Calculate the number of airways in generation 16. [2]
  2. (b) Each generation-16 airway has a diameter of 0.50 mm; the trachea has a diameter of 18 mm. Calculate how many times greater the total cross-sectional area of all generation-16 airways is than the cross-sectional area of the trachea. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest how this affects the speed at which air moves in the smallest airways. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 65 536
    M1: number = 2ⁿ. A1: 2¹⁶ = 65 536.
  2. (b) 50.6 times
    M1: area ∝ d², so ratio = 65 536 × (0.50 ÷ 18)². A1: 50.6.
  3. (c) Air moves much more slowly (the same volume flows through a much larger total cross-section), so near the alveoli gases move mainly by diffusion
    1 mark: much slower air flow in small airways.

Q14Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In cystic fibrosis the mucus in the airways is much thicker and stickier than normal. Suggest why people with cystic fibrosis suffer from frequent lung infections. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why people with cystic fibrosis may also become breathless. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cilia cannot move the thick mucus (mucociliary escalator fails); mucus and trapped bacteria stay in the airways; bacteria multiply in the warm, moist mucus, causing infection.
    1 mark: cilia unable to move/clear thick mucus. 1 mark: bacteria/pathogens trapped and not removed. 1 mark: bacteria multiply in the mucus / reach lower airways.
  2. (b) Mucus blocks or narrows bronchioles, so less air reaches the alveoli; less oxygen diffuses into the blood (smaller surface area for gas exchange).
    1 mark: mucus blocks/narrows airways. 1 mark: less ventilation of alveoli / less gas exchange.

  2. (b) Calculate how many times thicker the cartilage is than the epithelium. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. [1]

Q17Gas exchange system: structure & tissues · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In a rare inherited condition, enzymes released by white blood cells in the lungs break down elastic fibres faster than normal. Suggest and explain how this would affect breathing out. [3]
  2. (b) Alveolar walls damaged in this way may break down so that several alveoli merge into one larger air space. Explain why this reduces the rate of gas exchange. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fewer elastic fibres, so the walls of the alveoli and airways recoil less; less air is pushed out during expiration; air remains trapped in the alveoli (so the concentration gradient for gas exchange is reduced); breathing out needs more effort.
    1 mark: less elastic recoil. 1 mark: less air expelled / air trapped / harder to exhale. 1 mark: stale air reduces concentration gradient / gas exchange or extra muscular effort needed.
  2. (b) Merging alveoli reduces the total surface area available for diffusion, so less oxygen can diffuse into the blood per unit time.
    1 mark: smaller (total) surface area. 1 mark: less diffusion of O₂/CO₂ per unit time.

  3. (c) Carbon dioxide still diffuses out of the blood as quickly as oxygen diffuses in, even though its gradient is much smaller. Suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inspired air mixes with the air that remains in the lungs (residual air / air in airways) which has less oxygen; oxygen is continuously diffusing from alveolar air into the blood.
    1 mark: mixing with stale/residual air. 1 mark: O₂ constantly removed by diffusion into blood.
  2. (b) Oxygen: 8.0 kPa; carbon dioxide: 0.8 kPa
    1 mark each: 13.3 − 5.3 = 8.0 kPa; 6.1 − 5.3 = 0.8 kPa.
  3. (c) Carbon dioxide is much more soluble in water (in the moist alveolar lining and plasma) than oxygen, so it diffuses through the barrier faster for a given gradient.
    1 mark: CO₂ much more soluble.
Q54Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In emphysema, the walls between neighbouring alveoli break down and the alveoli merge into larger air spaces.

  1. (a) Calculate the diameter of the single large air space formed when 10 spherical alveoli of diameter 0.20 mm merge into one sphere with the same total volume. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Using surface area = πd², calculate the percentage decrease in surface area for gas exchange. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Using your answer to (b), explain why a person with emphysema has low blood oxygen during exercise even though their breathing rate increases. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.431 mm
    M1: volume ∝ d³, so new d³ = 10 × 0.20³. A1: d = 0.20 × ∛10 = 0.431 mm.
  2. (b) 53.6 %
    M1: before 10 × π × 0.20² = 1.257 mm²; after π × 0.431² = 0.583 mm². A1: (1.257 − 0.583) ÷ 1.257 × 100 = 53.6 %.
  3. (c) Surface area for gas exchange is much smaller, so less oxygen diffuses into the blood per second; during exercise oxygen demand rises (and blood passes the alveoli faster), so faster breathing cannot make up for the lost surface area
    1 mark: reduced surface area → less diffusion per second. 1 mark: higher O₂ demand in exercise not met / ventilation cannot compensate.

Of each breath, 150 cm³ stays in the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles (where no gas exchange happens) and does not reach the alveoli.Breathing pattern 1: 500 cm³ per breath, 12 breaths per minute.Breathing pattern 2: 250 cm³ per breath, 24 breaths per minute.

  1. (a) Show that both patterns move the same volume of air into the lungs per minute. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the volume of fresh air reaching the alveoli per minute for each pattern. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage decrease in alveolar ventilation for pattern 2 compared with pattern 1. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [1]
  4. (d) Explain why rapid, shallow breathing is less effective for gas exchange. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both 6000 cm³ per minute
    500 × 12 = 6000; 250 × 24 = 6000 cm³ min⁻¹.
  2. (b) Pattern 1: 4200 cm³ min⁻¹; pattern 2: 2400 cm³ min⁻¹
    B1: (500 − 150) × 12 = 4200. B1: (250 − 150) × 24 = 2400.
  3. (c) 42.9 %
    (4200 − 2400) ÷ 4200 × 100 = 42.9 %.
  4. (d) A larger fraction of each breath only fills the airways, so less fresh air reaches the alveoli; alveolar oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, so concentration gradients with the blood are less steep and diffusion is slower
    1 mark: less fresh air to alveoli. 1 mark: smaller gradients → slower diffusion of O₂ in / CO₂ out.

Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in partial pressure) ÷ thickness of the exchange surface.Athlete: surface area 70 m²; partial pressure difference 8 kPa; thickness 0.5 µmPatient with lung disease: surface area 45 m²; partial pressure difference 6 kPa; thickness 0.8 µm

  1. (a) Calculate how many times faster oxygen would diffuse into the blood of the athlete than into the blood of the patient. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest which ONE of the three changes in the patient has the greatest effect on the rate, and justify your answer with a calculation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.32 times
    M1: athlete 70 × 8 ÷ 0.5 = 1120. M1: patient 45 × 6 ÷ 0.8 = 337.5. A1: 1120 ÷ 337.5 = 3.32.
  2. (b) The increase in thickness: it reduces the rate by a factor of 0.8 ÷ 0.5 = 1.60, compared with 70 ÷ 45 = 1.56 for the smaller surface area and 8 ÷ 6 = 1.33 for the smaller partial pressure difference
    B1: increase in thickness (factor 1.60). B1: compared with surface area (1.56) and gradient (1.33).
Q57Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

An alveolar capillary is 0.75 mm long. At rest, blood flows through it at a mean speed of 1.0 mm per second.

  1. (a) Calculate the time a red blood cell spends in this capillary at rest. [1]
  2. (b) During hard exercise the blood flows three times faster. Calculate the new time in the capillary. [1]
  3. (c) Oxygen loading of a red blood cell is complete about 0.25 s after it enters the capillary in a healthy person. Explain why a person with thickened alveolar walls may have normal blood oxygen at rest but low blood oxygen during exercise. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.75 s
    0.75 mm ÷ 1.0 mm s⁻¹ = 0.75 s.
  2. (b) 0.25 s
    0.75 ÷ 3 = 0.25 s.
  3. (c) Thicker walls slow diffusion, so loading takes longer than 0.25 s; at rest there is still enough time (0.75 s) to load fully; during exercise the time falls to 0.25 s, which is not enough, so blood leaves partly oxygenated
    1 mark: diffusion slower / takes longer. 1 mark: at rest transit time long enough. 1 mark: exercise transit time too short → incomplete loading.
Q58Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

At rest, 5.0 dm³ of blood flows through the lungs per minute. Blood leaving the lungs contains 200 cm³ of oxygen per dm³, and blood arriving at the lungs contains 150 cm³ of oxygen per dm³.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of oxygen taken up by the blood in the lungs per minute at rest. [2]
  2. (b) During intense exercise, blood flow through the lungs rises to 20 dm³ per minute and the oxygen content of blood arriving at the lungs falls to 50 cm³ per dm³. Assuming blood leaving the lungs still contains 200 cm³ per dm³, calculate the factor by which oxygen uptake increases. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how features of the alveoli and their blood supply make this large increase in oxygen uptake possible. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 250 cm³ per minute
    M1: 200 − 150 = 50 cm³ per dm³. A1: 50 × 5.0 = 250 cm³ min⁻¹.
  2. (b) 12 times (3000 cm³ per minute)
    M1: (200 − 50) × 20 = 3000 cm³ min⁻¹. A1: 3000 ÷ 250 = 12.
  3. (c) Very large total surface area of alveoli (and capillaries) — extra capillaries open; short diffusion distance (squamous epithelium and endothelium one cell thick); steeper concentration gradient because returning blood has less oxygen and ventilation increases
    1 mark each for any three: large surface area; short diffusion distance; steeper gradient (lower venous O₂); increased ventilation; increased blood flow carries O₂ away.
Q59Alveoli & gas exchange · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Some cells in the alveolar walls secrete surfactant, a mixture of phospholipids and proteins that spreads over the water film lining the alveoli and lowers its surface tension. Babies born very early may not yet make enough surfactant.

  1. (a) Explain why the alveoli of a premature baby lacking surfactant tend to collapse after each breath. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the baby's blood oxygen concentration is low. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the baby has to use much more energy to breathe. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Without surfactant the surface tension of the water film is high; the water molecules pull the alveolar walls inwards (cohesion), so the alveoli collapse when air leaves at expiration
    1 mark: high surface tension (cohesion between water molecules). 1 mark: pulls walls together / alveoli collapse.
  2. (b) Collapsed alveoli are not ventilated, so the surface area available for gas exchange is reduced; less oxygen diffuses into the blood
    1 mark: reduced surface area / fewer alveoli open. 1 mark: less diffusion of O₂.
  3. (c) Each breath must re-inflate collapsed alveoli against high surface tension, which needs greater muscle effort
    1 mark.

  1. (a) Calculate the alveolar surface area per kg of body mass for each species. Give your answers to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Describe the relationship between body mass and alveolar surface area per kg. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest an explanation for this relationship. [3]
Show answer
