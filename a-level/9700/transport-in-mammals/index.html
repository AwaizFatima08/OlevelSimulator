Q7Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how most carbon dioxide produced by respiring cells is carried in the blood. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how the hydrogen ions formed in this process cause oxygen to be released from haemoglobin. [2]

Q8Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

When fully saturated, 100 cm³ of blood carries 20.0 cm³ of oxygen. Percentage saturation of haemoglobin:Lungs (pO₂ 13 kPa): 97 %Resting tissue (pO₂ 5 kPa): 70 %Exercising muscle (pO₂ 2.5 kPa): 35 % on the normal curve; 20 % when the high CO₂ in the muscle is taken into account.

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of oxygen released to resting tissues by 100 cm³ of blood. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate how much more oxygen is released by 100 cm³ of blood in exercising muscle, when the Bohr shift is taken into account, than in resting tissue. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the saturation at 13 kPa is hardly affected by the Bohr shift, but the saturation at 2.5 kPa is reduced from 35 % to 20 %. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.4 cm³
    M1 (97 − 70) = 27 % of maximum. A1 0.27 × 20.0 = 5.4 cm³.
  2. (b) 10.0 cm³ more
    M1 exercising, shifted curve: (97 − 20) % × 20.0 = 15.4 cm³. A1 15.4 − 5.4 = 10.0 cm³.
  3. (c) At 13 kPa the curve is flat (plateau), so a shift to the right makes little difference and haemoglobin still loads fully in the lungs; at low partial pressures the curve is steep, so the lower affinity causes a large fall in saturation and much more unloading.
    B1 lungs: plateau / high pO₂, loading still nearly complete. B1 tissues: steep part of curve, small change in affinity gives large change in saturation.
Q9Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Why does the oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin have an S (sigmoid) shape? [1]

Q10Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

P₅₀ is the partial pressure of oxygen at which haemoglobin is 50 % saturated. Values from one laboratory study (rounded):human 3.6 kPallama (lives in the Andes at about 4,000 m) 2.9 kPahouse mouse 5.3 kPa

  1. (a) Explain what the lower P₅₀ value of the llama shows about its haemoglobin compared with human haemoglobin. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why this is an advantage to a llama living at 4,000 m. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the haemoglobin of a mouse has a higher P₅₀ than human haemoglobin. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Llama haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen; its dissociation curve lies to the left of the human curve.
    B1 higher affinity; B1 curve to the left / 50 % saturated at a lower pO₂.
  2. (b) The partial pressure of oxygen in the air and alveoli is low at altitude; llama haemoglobin can still load oxygen and become (almost) fully saturated in the lungs.
    B1 low pO₂ in alveoli at altitude; B1 haemoglobin still loads / is highly saturated, so enough O₂ is carried.
  3. (c) The mouse is small with a large surface area to volume ratio, so it loses heat quickly and has a high metabolic rate; it needs a high rate of aerobic respiration; haemoglobin with a lower affinity unloads oxygen more readily in the tissues.
    1 mark each for: large SA:V / loses heat fast; high rate of respiration / metabolic rate; lower affinity so O₂ released more easily to tissues (at higher pO₂).
Q11Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A woman in Lahore has iron-deficiency anaemia. Her blood contains 87 g of haemoglobin per dm³. A healthy woman of the same age has 145 g dm⁻³. When fully saturated, 1 g of haemoglobin combines with 1.34 cm³ of oxygen.

  1. (a) Calculate the maximum volume of oxygen that 1 dm³ of blood can carry for the healthy woman and for the patient. Give your answers to the nearest whole number. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage reduction in the patient's haemoglobin concentration compared with the healthy woman. [1]
  3. (c) The patient becomes tired and breathless when climbing stairs. Explain why. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Healthy woman 194 cm³; patient 117 cm³
    M1 145 × 1.34 = 194 cm³; A1 87 × 1.34 = 117 cm³.
  2. (b) 40.0 %
    (145 − 87) ÷ 145 × 100 = 40.0 %.
  3. (c) Less haemoglobin, so less oxygen carried to muscles; less aerobic respiration / less ATP; more anaerobic respiration so lactate builds up; breathing rate rises to try to obtain more oxygen.
    1 mark each for any three: less O₂ carried / delivered; less aerobic respiration / ATP; anaerobic respiration / lactate / oxygen debt; increased breathing / heart rate to compensate.
Q12Blood cells, haemoglobin & gas transport · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Fetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult haemoglobin. State where its dissociation curve lies compared with the adult curve and explain why this is important for the fetus. [3]
  2. (b) Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and no mitochondria. Suggest how each of these features is an advantage for the transport of oxygen. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) To the left of the adult curve; at the low partial pressures in the placenta, fetal haemoglobin is more saturated than maternal haemoglobin; so oxygen moves from the mother's oxyhaemoglobin to the fetal haemoglobin.
    B1 left. B1 placenta has low pO₂ / mother's haemoglobin unloads there. B1 fetal haemoglobin loads oxygen released by maternal haemoglobin.
  2. (b) No nucleus: more space inside the cell for haemoglobin, so each cell carries more oxygen (and the cell can be a flexible biconcave disc). No mitochondria: the cell does not respire aerobically (it makes ATP by anaerobic respiration / glycolysis), so it does not use any of the oxygen it carries, and all of it can be delivered to the tissues.

Altitude and mean red blood cell count of residents of nine villages in northern Pakistan: / Altitude / m: 300, 900, 1500, 1900, 2300, 2700, 3100, 3500, 4000 / Red blood cell count / million per mm³: 4.8, 5.1, 4.9, 5.4, 5.3, 5.7, 5.6, 6.0, 5.9

Show answer
  1. (a) Altitude ranks 1 to 9; red blood cell count ranks 1, 3, 2, 5, 4, 7, 6, 9, 8; Σd² = 8.
