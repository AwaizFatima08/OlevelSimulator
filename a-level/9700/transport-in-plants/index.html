Q1Translocation (phloem) · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by the terms 'source' and 'sink' in translocation. [2]
  2. (b) In a sugarcane plant, state one example of a source and one example of a sink. [2]
  3. (c) Name the main carbohydrate transported in the phloem and suggest why it is transported in this form rather than as glucose. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Source: a part of the plant where assimilates (sucrose) are loaded into the phloem, e.g. a photosynthesising leaf or a storage organ being used. Sink: a part where assimilates are removed from the phloem and used or stored, e.g. a growing root tip, fruit or developing tuber.
    B1 source – where sucrose enters phloem/is produced or released; B1 sink – where sucrose leaves phloem/is used or stored.
  2. (b) Source: mature photosynthesising leaf. Sink: the stem (storage of sucrose), growing shoot tip or roots.
    B1 each.
  3. (c) Sucrose; it is non-reducing/less reactive, so it is not used up (respired) on the way, and it is soluble so it can be carried in solution.
    B1 sucrose; B1 less reactive/not metabolised in transit (accept: soluble).
Q2Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Describe how sucrose is loaded into a sieve tube element from a companion cell in a leaf. [4]
  2. (b) Explain how loading sucrose at the source causes mass flow of phloem sap towards a sink. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Companion cells use ATP to actively pump H⁺ ions out (proton pump); this sets up a H⁺ concentration gradient; H⁺ diffuses back into the companion cell through a cotransporter protein carrying sucrose with it (cotransport); sucrose then passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata (down its concentration gradient).
    1 mark each for: H⁺ pumped out using ATP; H⁺ gradient; cotransporter protein with sucrose; via plasmodesmata into sieve tube.
  2. (b) Sucrose loading lowers the water potential in the sieve tube; water enters by osmosis from the xylem/surrounding cells, raising hydrostatic pressure at the source; at the sink sucrose is removed, water leaves, so pressure is lower; sap flows down the hydrostatic pressure gradient from high to low pressure.
    1 mark each for any three of: water potential lowered; water enters by osmosis; high hydrostatic pressure at source; lower pressure at sink; flow down pressure gradient.
Q3Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which process in translocation uses ATP directly? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B pumping of hydrogen ions out of companion cells
    Proton pumps in companion cell membranes hydrolyse ATP to move H⁺ out; the H⁺ gradient then drives sucrose cotransport. Movement of water into the sieve tubes (C) is osmosis, a passive process.
Q4Translocation (phloem) · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A gardener in Mirpurkhas removed a complete ring of bark (including the phloem but not the xylem) from a branch of a mango tree. After four weeks the bark just above the ring had swollen and the fruits below the ring on that branch were small.

  1. (a) Explain the swelling above the ring. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the leaves on the branch did not wilt. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the fruits below the ring were small. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The phloem has been removed, so sucrose/assimilates moving down from the leaves cannot pass the ring; they accumulate above it, and water enters by osmosis/the tissue grows, causing swelling.
    B1 phloem removed, translocation blocked; B1 sugars accumulate above ring.
  2. (b) The xylem was not removed, so water and mineral ions still reach the leaves.
    B1.
  3. (c) Fruits are sinks; the sucrose they need for growth/storage comes from leaves above the ring through the phloem, which is now cut, so less sucrose reaches them.
    B1 fruits are sinks; B1 supply of sucrose from source cut off.
Q5Translocation (phloem) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Scientists supplied one leaf of a cotton plant with carbon dioxide containing radioactive ¹⁴C. Aphids feeding on the stem were later removed, leaving their mouthparts (stylets) in the stem. Sap collected from stylets 72 cm below the leaf first contained ¹⁴C 1.5 hours after it was supplied.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean rate of movement of the ¹⁴C in the phloem, in cm h⁻¹. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why aphid stylets are useful for studying the contents of sieve tubes. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how the structure of sieve tube elements allows rapid mass flow of sap. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 48 cm h⁻¹
    B1 72 ÷ 1.5 = 48.
  2. (b) The stylet penetrates a single sieve tube element; sap flows out under pressure without contamination from other cells, so pure phloem sap can be collected and analysed (and the flow shows the sap is under pressure).
    B1 enters one sieve tube/pure sap; B1 sap exudes under (hydrostatic) pressure.
  3. (c) Sieve tube elements have no nucleus, few organelles and only a thin layer of cytoplasm, so little resistance to flow; they are joined end to end into long tubes; the end walls are perforated as sieve plates with large pores.
    1 mark each for any three: few organelles/no nucleus; thin cytoplasm; end to end/continuous tube; sieve plates with pores.
Q6Water uptake & transpiration · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the apoplastic and symplastic pathways by which water crosses the cortex of a root. [3]
  2. (b) Explain the role of the Casparian strip in the endodermis. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Apoplast: water moves through the cell walls and spaces between cells, without crossing membranes. Symplast: water moves through the cytoplasm of cells, entering across the cell surface membrane and passing from cell to cell through plasmodesmata.
    B1 apoplast through cell walls/intercellular spaces; B1 symplast through cytoplasm; B1 via plasmodesmata.
  2. (b) The Casparian strip is a band of suberin in the endodermis cell walls that is impermeable to water; it blocks the apoplast pathway, so water (and ions) must enter the cytoplasm/cross a cell surface membrane, allowing the plant to control which ions reach the xylem.
    B1 suberin/waterproof, blocks apoplast; B1 forces water through cytoplasm/membranes, control of ion uptake.
Q7Water uptake & transpiration · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change would increase the rate of transpiration from a wheat plant? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C an increase in wind speed
    Wind removes the humid layer of air near the stomata, making the water potential gradient between the air spaces and the outside air steeper. Higher humidity (B) reduces the gradient and so reduces transpiration.
Q8Water uptake & transpiration · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how water moves up the xylem from the roots to the leaves of a tall tree such as a chinar (Platanus orientalis). [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the diameter of a tree trunk becomes slightly smaller during the hottest part of a sunny day. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls into air spaces and diffuses out through stomata (transpiration); this lowers the water potential in the leaf, so water is drawn from the xylem; water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds (cohesion), so a continuous column is pulled up under tension; adhesion of water to the lignified xylem walls helps; this is the cohesion–tension mechanism / transpiration pull.
    1 mark each for any four of: evaporation/transpiration from leaves; water potential gradient; cohesion by hydrogen bonding; tension/pull on column; adhesion to xylem walls; continuous column.
  2. (b) Transpiration is fastest then, so tension in the xylem is greatest; the water column pulls the xylem walls inwards, so the trunk narrows.
    B1 high transpiration/high tension; B1 xylem walls pulled inwards.
Q9Water uptake & transpiration · Standard · Paper 3 style · 7 marks

A student set up a potometer with a leafy shoot. The capillary tube had an internal radius of 0.5 mm. An air bubble moved 45 mm along the tube in 5 minutes. The volume of a cylinder is πr²l.

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm³ min⁻¹. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the rate of water uptake measured by the potometer is not exactly the same as the rate of transpiration. [2]
  3. (c) State two precautions when setting up the potometer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 7.07 mm³ min⁻¹
    M1 volume = π × 0.5² × 45 = 35.3 mm³; M1 ÷ 5; A1 7.07.
  2. (b) Some water taken up is used in photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid/in cell growth; some water may be produced in respiration; so uptake is not equal to loss.
    B1 water used in photosynthesis/turgor; B1 so uptake ≠ water lost by evaporation.
  3. (c) Any two of: cut the shoot under water; assemble the apparatus under water so no air enters; seal joints with petroleum jelly/check airtight; dry the leaves; allow the shoot to equilibrate before taking readings.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q10Water uptake & transpiration · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The rate of water loss from a potted maize plant was measured at 30 °C in a growth chamber at three relative humidities: 30 % – 4.6 g h⁻¹; 60 % – 2.9 g h⁻¹; 90 % – 0.8 g h⁻¹.

  1. (a) Explain the effect of relative humidity on the rate of water loss. [3]
  2. (b) At 90 % humidity, the rate of water loss fell close to zero. Suggest one disadvantage to the plant of a very low rate of transpiration. [1]
  3. (c) During a dry spell in Sindh, maize plants closed their stomata during the afternoon. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) As humidity increases, rate of water loss decreases; the air outside the leaf contains more water vapour, so the water potential gradient (difference in water vapour concentration) between the air spaces and the outside air is smaller; so water vapour diffuses out of the stomata more slowly.
    B1 trend with figures; B1 smaller water potential/vapour gradient; B1 slower diffusion through stomata.
  2. (b) Less mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) carried to leaves in the transpiration stream / less evaporative cooling of leaves.
    B1.
  3. (c) Advantage: reduces water loss, so the plant does not wilt. Disadvantage: less CO₂ diffuses in, so the rate of photosynthesis falls (and leaves may overheat).
    B1 each.
Q11Structure of xylem & phloem · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which feature is found in mature xylem vessel elements? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C lignified walls with pits
    Mature xylem vessel elements are dead, empty tubes with lignified walls and pits for lateral movement of water. Sieve plates (B) are found in phloem sieve tubes.
Q12Structure of xylem & phloem · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A student looked at a transverse section of a young sunflower stem and a transverse section of a young sunflower root under a light microscope.

  1. (a) Describe the arrangement of the xylem and phloem in the stem. [2]
  2. (b) Describe the arrangement of the xylem and phloem in the root. [2]
  3. (c) Name the tissue in the stem that lies between the epidermis and the vascular bundles. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) In vascular bundles arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem; in each bundle xylem is on the inside (towards the centre) and phloem on the outside.
    B1 ring of vascular bundles; B1 xylem inner, phloem outer.
  2. (b) In a central vascular cylinder (stele); xylem forms a central star/X shape with phloem in groups between the arms of the xylem.
    B1 central; B1 xylem star/cross shape with phloem between arms.
  3. (c) Cortex (parenchyma; collenchyma may be present just below epidermis)
    B1 cortex.
Q13Structure of xylem & phloem · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Compare the structure of a mature xylem vessel element with a sieve tube element. Give three differences. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how lignin in xylem walls helps the transport of water. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Xylem: dead/no cytoplasm; lignified walls; no end walls (continuous tube); pits; no companion cells. Sieve tube: living with thin cytoplasm (no nucleus); cellulose walls not lignified; sieve plates with pores; associated with companion cells; plasmodesmata.
    1 mark for each correct paired difference, up to three.
  2. (b) Lignin strengthens the walls so the vessels do not collapse under the tension of the water column; it also makes walls waterproof and helps adhesion of water.
    B1 prevents collapse under tension; B1 waterproof/adhesion.
