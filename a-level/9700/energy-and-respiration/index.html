Q1Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State where glycolysis takes place in a cell. [1]
  2. (b) Describe the main stages of glycolysis. [4]
  3. (c) Explain why glycolysis would stop if there were no NAD available. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cytoplasm (cytosol).
    B1 cytoplasm.
  2. (b) Glucose is phosphorylated using 2 ATP to form hexose (fructose) bisphosphate; this splits (lysis) into two triose phosphate molecules; each triose phosphate is oxidised by removal of hydrogen, which reduces NAD; each is converted to pyruvate, producing 2 ATP per triose phosphate by substrate-level phosphorylation (net gain 2 ATP per glucose).
    1 mark each for any four of: phosphorylation of glucose using 2 ATP; hexose bisphosphate formed; splits into 2 triose phosphates; dehydrogenation / oxidation of triose phosphate; NAD reduced (2 reduced NAD per glucose); pyruvate formed; 4 ATP produced, net gain of 2 ATP; substrate-level phosphorylation.
  3. (c) NAD is needed to accept hydrogen when triose phosphate is oxidised, so this step could not happen.
    B1 no hydrogen acceptor for oxidation of triose phosphate.
Q2Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State how pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix. [1]
  2. (b) Describe what happens to pyruvate in the link reaction. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why this stage is called the link reaction. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) By active transport (across the mitochondrial membranes).
    B1 active transport.
  2. (b) Pyruvate is decarboxylated (CO₂ removed) and dehydrogenated (hydrogen removed, reducing NAD); the remaining 2-carbon acetyl group combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A.
    B1 decarboxylation / CO₂ released; B1 dehydrogenation / NAD reduced; B1 acetyl group + coenzyme A → acetyl CoA.
  3. (c) It links glycolysis (in the cytoplasm) to the Krebs cycle (in the matrix).
    B1 connects glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.
Q3Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A simplified Krebs cycle is described in words.Step 1: acetyl coenzyme A (2C) combines with compound W (4C) to form a 6-carbon compound.Step 2: the 6-carbon compound loses CO₂ and hydrogen, forming a 5-carbon compound.Step 3: the 5-carbon compound loses CO₂ and hydrogen, forming a 4-carbon compound; ATP is also made.Step 4: the 4-carbon compound is converted back to W, with further removal of hydrogen.

  1. (a) Name compound W and the 6-carbon compound formed in step 1. [2]
  2. (b) Identify the steps in which decarboxylation occurs and state what is meant by dehydrogenation. [2]
  3. (c) The Krebs cycle does not use oxygen directly. Explain why it stops when oxygen is not available. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) W = oxaloacetate; 6-carbon compound = citrate.
    B1 oxaloacetate; B1 citrate.
  2. (b) Steps 2 and 3 (6C → 5C and 5C → 4C); dehydrogenation is the removal of hydrogen atoms from a substrate, which are accepted by NAD or FAD.
    B1 steps 2 and 3; B1 removal of hydrogen (by dehydrogenase enzymes) – accepted by NAD/FAD.
  3. (c) Oxygen is the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain; without it reduced NAD and FAD cannot be reoxidised, so there is no NAD / FAD to accept hydrogen in the Krebs cycle.
    B1 oxygen is the final electron / hydrogen acceptor in the ETC; B1 NAD and FAD not regenerated so dehydrogenation in the Krebs cycle stops.
Q4Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Succinate dehydrogenase is a Krebs cycle enzyme, bound to the inner mitochondrial membrane, that removes hydrogen from succinate and reduces FAD.

  1. (a) Malonate has a similar shape to succinate. Suggest how malonate inhibits succinate dehydrogenase. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why adding malonate to isolated mitochondria reduces their uptake of oxygen. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest how the effect of malonate could be reversed in the experiment. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is a competitive inhibitor: it binds to the active site of succinate dehydrogenase, so succinate cannot bind and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
    B1 binds to the active site (competitive inhibition); B1 prevents succinate binding / fewer E–S complexes.
  2. (b) The Krebs cycle slows / stops, so less reduced FAD (and NAD) is produced; fewer electrons enter the electron transport chain; less oxygen is needed as the final electron acceptor.
    B1 Krebs cycle slows / succinate not converted to fumarate; B1 less reduced FAD / NAD produced; B1 fewer electrons passed to oxygen at the end of the ETC, so less oxygen reduced to water.
  3. (c) Add a high concentration of succinate.
    B1 increase succinate concentration (out-competes inhibitor).
Q5Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The products of each stage of aerobic respiration per molecule of glucose (net values) are:glycolysis: 2 reduced NAD, 0 reduced FAD, 2 ATP, 0 CO₂link reaction: 2 reduced NAD, 0 reduced FAD, 0 ATP, 2 CO₂Krebs cycle (two turns): 6 reduced NAD, 2 reduced FAD, 2 ATP, 4 CO₂

  1. (a) Complete the totals for one molecule of glucose: number of reduced NAD, reduced FAD, ATP (by substrate-level phosphorylation) and CO₂ produced. [2]
  2. (b) Assume that each reduced NAD gives 2.5 ATP and each reduced FAD gives 1.5 ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. Calculate the maximum yield of ATP from one molecule of glucose. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why the actual yield of ATP in a cell is usually lower than this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) reduced NAD 10; reduced FAD 2; ATP 4; CO₂ 6
    B1 NAD 10 and FAD 2; B1 ATP 4 (net) and CO₂ 6.
  2. (b) 32 ATP
    M1 10 × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 = 28; A1 + 4 = 32.
  3. (c) Some protons leak back across the inner membrane without passing through ATP synthase; energy is used to transport pyruvate, ADP and phosphate into the mitochondrion (and ATP out); some reduced NAD from glycolysis passes its hydrogen to carriers giving less ATP.
    1 mark each for any two of: proton leak across inner membrane; energy (ATP / proton gradient) used for active transport of pyruvate, ADP, Pi, ATP; reduced NAD from cytoplasm gives fewer ATP; some energy released as heat.
Q6Glycolysis, link reaction & Krebs cycle · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Yeast cells were supplied with glucose in which all the carbon atoms were the radioactive isotope ¹⁴C, in well-aerated conditions. Samples were taken every few seconds and analysed for radioactive compounds.

  1. (a) Suggest the order in which radioactivity would first appear in pyruvate, acetyl coenzyme A, citrate and the 5-carbon compound of the Krebs cycle. Explain your answer. [2]
  2. (b) When the experiment was repeated with yeast in the absence of oxygen, radioactivity appeared in pyruvate, ethanal, ethanol and CO₂, but not in citrate. Explain these results. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pyruvate → acetyl coenzyme A → citrate → 5-carbon compound; glucose is broken down to pyruvate in glycolysis, pyruvate forms acetyl CoA in the link reaction, the acetyl group joins oxaloacetate to form citrate (6C), and citrate is then decarboxylated and dehydrogenated to the 5-carbon compound.
    B1 correct order; B1 explanation linked to glycolysis → link reaction → Krebs cycle (citrate formed before it is converted to the 5C compound). Note: labelled CO₂ would appear as early as acetyl CoA, from the link reaction.
  2. (b) Without oxygen the ETC stops so reduced NAD is not reoxidised in mitochondria; the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop, so no citrate; pyruvate is decarboxylated to ethanal (releasing labelled CO₂) and ethanal is reduced to ethanol by reduced NAD, regenerating NAD for glycolysis.
    1 mark each for any three of: no oxygen as final acceptor → Krebs cycle / link reaction stop; so no citrate; pyruvate decarboxylated to ethanal (labelled CO₂); ethanal reduced to ethanol using reduced NAD; NAD regenerated so glycolysis continues.
Q7Oxidative phosphorylation & mitochondria · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how the structure of a mitochondrion is related to its function in aerobic respiration. [4]
  2. (b) Heart muscle cells have mitochondria with many more cristae than mitochondria in skin cells. Suggest why. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inner membrane folded into cristae → large surface area for electron carriers and ATP synthase; inner membrane impermeable to protons so a gradient can be maintained; small intermembrane space so proton concentration builds up quickly; matrix contains enzymes for the link reaction and Krebs cycle, and contains NAD, FAD and its own DNA and ribosomes to make some proteins.
    1 mark each for any four of: cristae give large surface area; for ETC / ATP synthase; inner membrane impermeable to H⁺; small intermembrane space for high H⁺ concentration; matrix enzymes for link reaction and Krebs cycle; matrix has coenzymes / NAD / FAD; own DNA and 70S ribosomes; outer membrane has transport proteins for pyruvate.
  2. (b) Heart muscle contracts continuously and needs a lot of ATP, so it needs more ETC and ATP synthase.
    B1 more ATP needed for constant contraction → more sites for oxidative phosphorylation.
Q8Oxidative phosphorylation & mitochondria · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe how the energy in reduced NAD is used to synthesise ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Reduced NAD gives hydrogen to the first carrier on the inner membrane; hydrogen splits into H⁺ and an electron; electrons pass along a chain of carriers in a series of redox reactions; energy released is used to pump H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space; a proton gradient forms; H⁺ diffuse back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis); this provides energy to combine ADP and Pi; oxygen is the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and H⁺ to form water.
    1 mark each for any six of: NAD reoxidised / hydrogen released at inner membrane (cristae); H → H⁺ + e⁻; electrons passed along carriers (ETC), redox reactions; energy released pumps H⁺ into intermembrane space; proton (electrochemical) gradient; H⁺ flow back through ATP synthase by facilitated diffusion / chemiosmosis; ADP + Pi → ATP; oxygen final electron acceptor; forms water.
Q9Oxidative phosphorylation & mitochondria · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) The complete oxidation of one mole of glucose releases 2870 kJ. Hydrolysis of one mole of ATP releases 30.5 kJ. Using a yield of 32 ATP per glucose, calculate the efficiency of aerobic respiration. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) State what happens to the rest of the energy. [1]
  3. (c) Anaerobic respiration in muscle produces only 2 ATP per glucose. Calculate the efficiency of anaerobic respiration, assuming the same 2870 kJ per mole of glucose, and suggest why this comparison is not entirely fair. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 34.0 %
    M1 32 × 30.5 = 976 kJ; A1 976/2870 × 100 = 34.0 %.
  2. (b) It is released as heat.
    B1 heat (helps maintain body temperature in mammals and birds).
  3. (c) 2.1 %; much of the energy is still in lactate, which can be oxidised later, so the energy is not all lost.
    B1 2 × 30.5/2870 × 100 = 2.1 %; B1 lactate still contains chemical energy / can be converted back to pyruvate and respired.
Q10Oxidative phosphorylation & mitochondria · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The compound 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) makes the inner mitochondrial membrane permeable to protons. It was once sold as a slimming drug but caused deaths.

  1. (a) Explain why DNP reduces the production of ATP. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why the rate of oxygen uptake by the cells increased when DNP was added, and why the body temperature of people who took DNP rose. [3]
  3. (c) Brown fat cells in newborn babies contain a protein that lets protons pass through the inner membrane. Suggest the advantage of this. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Protons leak back into the matrix through the membrane instead of through ATP synthase, so the proton gradient is lost and ATP synthase cannot make ATP.
    B1 H⁺ return without passing through ATP synthase / proton gradient dissipated; B1 so less chemiosmosis / ADP not phosphorylated.
  2. (b) The electron transport chain continues (or speeds up) because there is no build-up of protons to slow it, so more oxygen is used as final acceptor; energy from electron transport is released as heat instead of being used to make ATP; cells respire more substrate to try to meet ATP demand.
    B1 ETC still runs / faster because back-pressure of proton gradient removed; B1 more oxygen reduced to water; B1 energy released as heat rather than trapped in ATP (more substrate respired).
  3. (c) Generates heat to maintain body temperature.
    B1 produces heat (thermoregulation) instead of ATP.
Q11Oxidative phosphorylation & mitochondria · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Cyanide binds to the last carrier in the electron transport chain so that electrons cannot be passed to oxygen. Explain why cyanide quickly stops ATP production in the mitochondria. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest why the concentration of lactate in the blood rises in a person poisoned with cyanide. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Electrons cannot leave the chain, so carriers stay reduced and the chain stops; no protons are pumped, so no proton gradient and ATP synthase stops; reduced NAD and FAD cannot be reoxidised, so the Krebs cycle and link reaction also stop.
    B1 electron flow along ETC stops (carriers remain reduced); B1 no H⁺ pumped / no proton gradient so no ATP synthesis by ATP synthase; B1 NAD / FAD not regenerated → Krebs cycle and link reaction stop.
  2. (b) Cells can only make ATP by glycolysis; to regenerate NAD, pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, so lactate accumulates.
    B1 cells rely on anaerobic respiration / glycolysis only; B1 pyruvate converted to lactate to reoxidise NAD.
Q12Anaerobic respiration · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Compare anaerobic respiration in yeast with anaerobic respiration in mammalian muscle. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the regeneration of NAD is essential for anaerobic respiration. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Similarities: both start with glycolysis, both reoxidise reduced NAD, both produce 2 ATP per glucose. Differences: yeast produces ethanol and CO₂, muscle produces lactate and no CO₂; yeast pathway has two steps (pyruvate decarboxylated to ethanal by pyruvate decarboxylase, then ethanal reduced to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase), muscle has one step (pyruvate reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase); lactate can be converted back to pyruvate, ethanol is not converted back to pyruvate and is lost from the cells.
    1 mark each for any four, at least one similarity and one difference: both use glycolysis / net 2 ATP; both regenerate NAD; yeast ethanol + CO₂ vs muscle lactate; hydrogen acceptor ethanal vs pyruvate; enzymes named (pyruvate decarboxylase & alcohol dehydrogenase vs lactate dehydrogenase); decarboxylation only in yeast; lactate reconverted in liver vs ethanol not converted back.
  2. (b) NAD is needed for glycolysis to continue (to oxidise triose phosphate), which is the only source of ATP.
    B1 so glycolysis can continue producing ATP.
Q13Anaerobic respiration · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Rice is grown in flooded paddy fields across Punjab and Sindh.

  1. (a) Describe how rice plants are adapted to grow with their roots in flooded paddy fields with very little oxygen. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why rice roots can respire anaerobically for long periods but most crop roots cannot. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Aerenchyma (large air spaces) in stems and roots allow oxygen to diffuse from the leaves to the roots; roots tolerate high ethanol concentrations; roots have high levels of alcohol dehydrogenase so ethanol is broken down / anaerobic respiration continues; stems grow rapidly to keep leaves above water; shallow roots / leaves have water-repellent surfaces trapping air.
    1 mark each for any four of: aerenchyma / air spaces; oxygen diffuses to roots from aerial parts; root cells tolerant of ethanol; high alcohol dehydrogenase activity; respire anaerobically producing ethanol; fast-growing stems keep leaves above water; air film on leaf surfaces.
  2. (b) In most crops ethanol builds up to toxic levels, killing the root cells.
    B1 ethanol toxicity in other species / rice breaks down or tolerates ethanol.
Q14Anaerobic respiration · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A sprinter's blood lactate concentration was measured before, during and after a 400 m race.

  1. (a) Assuming aerobic respiration yields 32 ATP per glucose and anaerobic respiration 2 ATP, calculate how many times faster glucose must be broken down anaerobically to give the same rate of ATP production. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why blood lactate concentration rises during a 400 m sprint and continues to rise for a few minutes after the race. [3]
  3. (c) Describe what happens to lactate after exercise. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 16 times
    B1 32/2 = 16.
  2. (b) During the sprint oxygen supply to muscles cannot meet demand, so muscle cells respire anaerobically and pyruvate is reduced to lactate; lactate diffuses into the blood; after the race lactate still diffuses out of muscle cells into the blood before the liver removes it.
    B1 insufficient oxygen supply to muscle / demand for ATP exceeds aerobic supply; B1 lactate produced from pyruvate (to regenerate NAD); B1 lactate continues to diffuse from muscle into blood after exercise (removal by liver slower).
  3. (c) Lactate is carried in the blood to the liver, oxidised back to pyruvate (using oxygen – the oxygen debt), which is either respired aerobically or converted to glucose / glycogen.
    B1 transported to liver; B1 converted to pyruvate, then respired or converted to glucose / glycogen (requires extra oxygen).

  2. (b) The equation for the complete oxidation of the lipid tripalmitin is2C₅₁H₉₈O₆ + 145O₂ → 102CO₂ + 98H₂OCalculate the RQ of tripalmitin. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. [2]

Q16Respiratory substrates, RQ & respirometers · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Two respirometers contained equal masses of germinating sunflower seeds at 25 °C. Tube A contained potassium hydroxide solution; tube B contained water instead. In 20 minutes the liquid in the capillary of tube A moved 36 mm towards the seeds and in tube B it moved 9 mm towards the seeds. Both capillaries had the same diameter.

  1. (a) Explain why the liquid moves towards the seeds in tube A. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the RQ of the seeds. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest which respiratory substrate the seeds were mainly using. Explain your answer. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The seeds take in oxygen for aerobic respiration; the CO₂ they release is absorbed by potassium hydroxide, so the volume of gas falls and the pressure decreases.
    B1 oxygen used in respiration; B1 CO₂ absorbed by KOH so gas volume / pressure in the tube decreases.
  2. (b) 0.75
    M1 O₂ uptake = 36 mm; CO₂ output = 36 − 9 = 27 mm; A1 RQ = 27/36 = 0.75.
  3. (c) A mixture of lipid and carbohydrate (or mainly lipid), because 0.75 is between 0.7 (lipid) and 1.0 (carbohydrate) and close to 0.7.
    B1 lipid (with some carbohydrate), RQ between 0.7 and 1.0 / close to 0.7.
Q17Respiratory substrates, RQ & respirometers · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A respirometer containing 5.0 g of germinating mung bean seeds and soda lime was kept at 30 °C. The coloured liquid in the capillary tube (internal radius 0.5 mm) moved 24 mm in 10 minutes.

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in mm³ per gram per hour. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a thermobarometer (an identical tube with glass beads instead of seeds) should be set up alongside the respirometer. [2]
  3. (c) During the first two days of germination of castor bean seeds, which store mainly lipid, the RQ was 0.71; by day 10, when the seedlings had green leaves, it was 0.98. Suggest an explanation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 22.6 mm³ g⁻¹ h⁻¹
    M1 volume = π r² l = π × 0.5² × 24 = 18.85 mm³; M1 ÷ 5.0 g ÷ (10/60) h; A1 22.6.
  2. (b) Changes in temperature or atmospheric pressure would make the liquid move even without respiration; the thermobarometer measures this movement so it can be subtracted (or used to correct the readings).
    B1 temperature / atmospheric pressure changes alter gas volume; B1 allows correction of readings for these changes.
  3. (c) At first the seeds respire stored lipid (RQ about 0.7); later the lipid stores are used up and the seedlings photosynthesise, so they respire carbohydrate (RQ about 1.0).
    B1 early: lipid stores respired; B1 later: carbohydrate (from photosynthesis / converted from lipid) respired.
Q18ATP & energy · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the structure of an ATP molecule. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why ATP is described as the universal energy currency of cells. [3]
  3. (c) Name the two ways ATP is synthesised in respiration. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Adenine (a base) joined to ribose (a pentose sugar) and three phosphate groups.
    B1 adenine + ribose; B1 three phosphate groups.
  2. (b) It is used for energy transfer in all organisms / cells; hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and Pi releases energy in small, manageable amounts; it is water soluble and easily transported within cells; it is quickly re-made from ADP and Pi; one molecule type can be used by many different reactions.
    1 mark each for any three of: found in all living cells; hydrolysis releases a small, suitable amount of energy (about 30 kJ mol⁻¹); single step reaction, quick; soluble / moves easily in cell; easily regenerated; links energy-releasing and energy-requiring reactions.
  3. (c) Substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation (chemiosmosis).
    B1 both named.
Q19ATP & energy · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Mean energy values of respiratory substrates (kJ g⁻¹): carbohydrate 15.8; lipid 39.4; protein 17.0.

  1. (a) Calculate how many times more energy is released per gram from lipid than from carbohydrate. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why lipids release more energy per gram than carbohydrates when respired. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why migratory birds such as Siberian cranes that fly to Pakistan each winter store lipid rather than carbohydrate before migration. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.5 times
    B1 39.4/15.8 = 2.494 ≈ 2.5.
  2. (b) Lipids contain more hydrogen atoms per unit mass (fatty acids are long hydrocarbon chains); most energy comes from oxidising hydrogen in the ETC: more hydrogen means more reduced NAD/FAD, more protons pumped and more ATP; carbohydrates contain more oxygen and are already partly oxidised.
    1 mark each for any three of: more H atoms per gram in lipid; hydrogen carried by NAD / FAD to ETC; more protons pumped / greater proton gradient; more ATP produced by oxidative phosphorylation; carbohydrates contain proportionally more oxygen.
  3. (c) Lipid gives more energy per unit mass, so the bird carries less weight for the same energy store (and produces metabolic water).
    B1 more energy per gram so lighter load (accept metabolic water).
Q20ATP & energy · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

An adult body contains about 250 g of ATP at any one time, yet hydrolyses about 70 kg of ATP per day.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean number of times each ATP molecule is resynthesised from ADP and Pi per day. [1]
  2. (b) The relative molecular mass of ATP is 507. Hydrolysis of one mole releases 30.5 kJ. Calculate the energy released per day by hydrolysis of ATP, in kJ, to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why cells do not store large amounts of ATP. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 280 times
    B1 70 × 1000 / 250 = 280.
  2. (b) 4210 kJ
    M1 moles = 70 000/507 = 138.1 mol; A1 × 30.5 = 4211 ≈ 4210 kJ.
  3. (c) ATP is unstable / readily hydrolysed so cannot be stored for long; it is quickly regenerated from ADP and Pi when needed, so a small pool recycled rapidly is enough; energy is stored instead in glycogen or lipid.
    1 mark each for any two of: ATP unstable / hydrolyses easily; rapid turnover from ADP + Pi makes storage unnecessary; would affect water potential / takes up space; energy stored as glycogen / starch / lipid instead.
Q21Skill: planning a method · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

A student wants to find out whether yeast respires glucose, sucrose, maltose and lactose at different rates.

  1. (a) Describe how you would make 50 cm³ of a 2 % sugar solution of each sugar, from solid sugar and distilled water. [2]
  2. (b) Describe how the student could compare the rate of respiration of yeast with each sugar by measuring carbon dioxide production. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Weigh 1.0 g of sugar, dissolve in some distilled water and make up to 50 cm³ in a volumetric flask (or measuring cylinder).
    M1 2 g per 100 cm³ → 1.0 g per 50 cm³; A1 dissolve and make up to total volume 50 cm³.
  2. (b) Mix the same volume (e.g. 20 cm³) of each sugar solution with the same mass of yeast (e.g. 1 g) in a flask connected to a gas syringe; keep at 30 °C in a water bath; allow 5 min to equilibrate; measure volume of gas collected every minute for 10 min (or total volume in a fixed time); rate = volume ÷ time; control with water and no sugar; repeat 3 times.
    1 mark each for any four of: same yeast mass / suspension volume; same volume and concentration of each sugar; collect CO₂ in gas syringe / inverted measuring cylinder over water; constant temperature water bath; equilibration time; measure volume over a fixed time / calculate rate; control with no sugar; repeats and mean.
Q22Skill: planning a method · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Methylene blue is a redox indicator that turns colourless when it is reduced by the hydrogen released by dehydrogenase enzymes in respiring yeast. Describe a method to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of respiration of yeast using methylene blue. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Prepare yeast suspension (e.g. 10 g dried yeast in 100 cm³ of 5 % glucose solution) and leave to activate; place 10 cm³ yeast suspension in tubes in thermostatically controlled water baths at 5 temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C) for 10 min to equilibrate; add 1 cm³ of 0.05 % methylene blue, mix by inverting once, start timer; record time for blue colour to disappear (compare with a colour standard of yeast suspension without indicator); do not shake (oxygen would reoxidise the dye); control: boiled yeast; rate = 1/time; 3 repeats at each temperature and mean; standardise yeast concentration, glucose concentration and volume, dye volume and concentration.
    1 mark each for any seven of: at least 5 temperatures with suitable range; method of controlling temperature (water bath, thermometer check); equilibration of yeast and dye separately before mixing; same volume and concentration of yeast suspension; same concentration of glucose; same volume of methylene blue; measure time taken to decolourise; end point standardised (colour standard); avoid shaking / cover with liquid paraffin to keep oxygen out; control with boiled yeast; rate = 1/t; 3 repeats and mean; safety (e.g. methylene blue stains, hot water).
Q23Skill: calculations & data processing · Core · Paper 5 style · 4 marks

The time taken for methylene blue to decolourise in tubes of yeast suspension at different temperatures:20 °C: 480 s30 °C: 300 s40 °C: 190 s50 °C: 120 s60 °C: 260 s

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of respiration at each temperature as 1000/t, where t is time in seconds. Give your answers to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in rate between 20 °C and 50 °C, using unrounded rates. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20 °C: 2.1; 30 °C: 3.3; 40 °C: 5.3; 50 °C: 8.3; 60 °C: 3.8 (arbitrary units)
    M1 1000/t for each; A1 all five correct to 1 d.p.
  2. (b) 300 %
    M1 (1000/120 − 1000/480) ÷ (1000/480) × 100 = (8.333 − 2.083)/2.083 × 100; A1 300 % (allow 295 % from rounded rates 8.3 and 2.1).
Q24Skill: calculations & data processing · Standard · Paper 5 style · 4 marks

A respirometer containing 2.0 g of woodlice and soda lime was set up at 20 °C. The distance moved by the liquid in the capillary (radius 0.4 mm) from the start was recorded.1 min: 8 mm2 min: 17 mm3 min: 25 mm4 min: 33 mm5 min: 42 mm

  1. (a) Calculate the volume of oxygen absorbed between the readings at 1 minute and 5 minutes. Use V = πr²l and give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in mm³ per gram per hour. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 17.1 mm³
    M1 l = 42 − 8 = 34 mm; A1 π × 0.4² × 34 = 17.1 mm³.
  2. (b) 128 mm³ g⁻¹ h⁻¹
    M1 17.09/4 min × 60 = per hour; ÷ 2.0 g; A1 128.
Q25Skill: errors, reliability & improvements · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

A student measured the oxygen uptake of germinating seeds using a simple respirometer on the laboratory bench next to a sunny window. She sealed the tube with a rubber bung, started timing immediately and took one reading of the position of the liquid after 15 minutes.

  1. (a) Identify three sources of error in this investigation and suggest an improvement for each. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. room temperature changed during the experiment → place respirometer in a thermostatically controlled water bath; no thermobarometer, so pressure changes not allowed for → set up a control tube with glass beads of the same volume; leaks at joints → seal with petroleum jelly and test for leaks; difficult to read meniscus position → use a scale with mm divisions and read at eye level / mark with fine pen; seeds not given time to equilibrate → leave for 10 min with tap open before starting; only one reading → repeat three times.
    For each of three: B1 error relevant to this method; B1 matching improvement.
Q26Skill: variables & controls · Core · Paper 5 style · 5 marks

A student used respirometers to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of respiration of fly maggots.

  1. (a) Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable and two standardised variables. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest a control for this experiment and explain why it is needed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) IV: temperature. DV: rate of oxygen uptake (distance moved by liquid per minute). Standardised (any two): mass of maggots; number / age / species of maggots; volume and mass of soda lime; diameter of capillary; time allowed to equilibrate; time of measurement.
    B1 IV temperature; B1 DV rate of oxygen uptake / distance per unit time; B1 any two standardised variables.
  2. (b) A respirometer with an equal volume of glass beads (or dead maggots) instead of living maggots, to show that movement of the liquid is due to respiration and to correct for temperature and pressure changes.
    B1 tube with glass beads / no living organisms; B1 shows changes are due to respiration / allows correction.
Q27Skill: explaining results with biology · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

Yeast was added to a sealed flask containing 5 % glucose solution at 30 °C. The total volume of CO₂ released (cm³) was recorded every 10 hours.0 h: 010 h: 420 h: 1130 h: 1940 h: 2450 h: 2660 h: 26

  1. (a) Describe the changes in the total volume of CO₂ produced over the 60 hours. [2]
  2. (b) Explain these results using your knowledge of respiration in yeast. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) CO₂ production rose slowly at first (0–10 h), then rapidly between 10 and 30 h, then slowed and stopped at 26 cm³ after 50 h.
    B1 initial slow increase then rapid increase; B1 levels off / no further increase at 26 cm³ from 50 h.
  2. (b) At first the yeast population is small / adjusting, so little CO₂; yeast then multiplies and respires glucose rapidly, aerobically while oxygen lasts then anaerobically, releasing CO₂ in the link reaction and Krebs cycle and by decarboxylation of pyruvate to ethanal; CO₂ production stops because glucose is used up and/or ethanol accumulates to a toxic concentration, killing yeast / inhibiting enzymes.
    1 mark each for any four of: lag – few yeast cells / enzymes being synthesised; population increases so more respiration; oxygen used up so anaerobic respiration (fermentation); CO₂ from decarboxylation (pyruvate → ethanal, or Krebs cycle while aerobic); glucose substrate runs out; ethanol accumulates and is toxic / denatures enzymes; pH falls as CO₂ dissolves.
Q28Skill: graphs & charts · Standard · Paper 5 style · 5 marks

The volume of oxygen absorbed by 20 g of germinating pea seeds was measured.0 min: 0.0 cm³2 min: 1.8 cm³4 min: 3.6 cm³6 min: 5.5 cm³8 min: 7.3 cm³10 min: 9.0 cm³

  1. (a) Describe how you would plot these results on a graph. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake between 2 and 8 minutes, in cm³ per minute, to 2 significant figures. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Time (min) on the x-axis and volume of oxygen absorbed (cm³) on the y-axis; both axes labelled with units; scale using more than half the grid with even intervals; points plotted as small crosses; line of best fit drawn (points lie close to a straight line).
    1 mark each for any three of: correct axes orientation; labels with units; suitable linear scale using > half the grid; accurate plotting; ruled line of best fit (or point to point if justified).
  2. (b) 0.92 cm³ min⁻¹
    M1 (7.3 − 1.8)/(8 − 2); A1 = 0.917 ≈ 0.92.
Q29Skill: hypotheses & predictions · Core · Paper 5 style · 4 marks

A student plans to measure the rate of CO₂ production by yeast in glucose solutions of 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 %.

  1. (a) State a suitable hypothesis for this investigation. [1]
  2. (b) Predict the shape of the graph of rate of CO₂ production against glucose concentration and explain your prediction. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The rate of respiration of yeast increases as glucose concentration increases (until a maximum is reached).
    B1 testable hypothesis linking glucose concentration and rate, with direction.
  2. (b) Rate increases steeply at low glucose concentrations, then levels off at high concentrations; at low concentration glucose is the limiting factor; at high concentration enzymes / glucose transport proteins are saturated (or yeast concentration limits), so rate cannot rise further; very high concentrations may decrease the rate by causing water loss by osmosis.
    B1 rises then levels off (plateau); B1 glucose limiting at low concentration / more collisions with enzymes; B1 enzymes / carrier proteins saturated or another factor limiting at high concentration (allow fall due to osmotic water loss).
Q30Skill: conclusions & interpreting data · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

Rate of oxygen uptake by germinating wheat grains (mm³ g⁻¹ h⁻¹) at different temperatures:10 °C: 4.220 °C: 8.130 °C: 15.640 °C: 18.050 °C: 6.3

  1. (a) Describe the effect of temperature on the rate of oxygen uptake. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the results between 40 °C and 50 °C. [2]
  3. (c) A student concluded that the optimum temperature for respiration in these seeds is 40 °C. Evaluate this conclusion. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rate increases from 4.2 at 10 °C to a maximum of 18.0 at 40 °C, then falls sharply to 6.3 at 50 °C.
    B1 increase up to 40 °C, then decrease; B1 data quoted with units, e.g. maximum 18.0 mm³ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 40 °C.
  2. (b) Respiratory enzymes (dehydrogenases, ATP synthase) are denatured: hydrogen bonds break, the active site changes shape so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
    B1 enzymes denatured / bonds in tertiary structure broken; B1 active site shape changed, substrate no longer fits.
  3. (c) The highest rate recorded was at 40 °C, but the true optimum could be anywhere between 30 and 50 °C because readings were taken only every 10 °C; more readings between 35 and 45 °C would be needed; no repeats shown.
    B1 highest rate at 40 °C so supports; B1 large intervals — optimum could be between 30 and 50 °C / need smaller intervals / repeats.
