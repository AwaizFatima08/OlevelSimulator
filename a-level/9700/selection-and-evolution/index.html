Q1Natural & artificial selection · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Since 2016, many cases of typhoid in Sindh have been caused by strains of Salmonella Typhi that are resistant to several antibiotics (extensively drug-resistant typhoid).

  1. (a) Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest two ways in which the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria can be reduced. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A random mutation produces an allele giving resistance (e.g. coding for an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic); when the antibiotic is used it acts as a selection pressure; bacteria without the allele are killed, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce (binary fission), passing on the allele; the allele frequency increases over generations; (resistance alleles on plasmids can also pass between bacteria by horizontal gene transfer/conjugation).
    1 mark each for any four of: mutation (random, already present); antibiotic is the selection pressure; resistant survive and reproduce; allele passed to offspring; allele frequency increases; plasmid transfer.
  2. (b) Any two of: use antibiotics only when needed/prescribed; complete the full course; avoid using antibiotics in animal feed; improve sanitation and clean drinking water; vaccination against typhoid; infection control/hand washing.
    1 mark each.
Q2Natural & artificial selection · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe what is meant by stabilising, directional and disruptive selection. [3]
  2. (b) Human birth mass is an example of stabilising selection. Explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Stabilising: individuals with intermediate phenotypes are favoured, extremes selected against; the mean stays the same and variation decreases. Directional: individuals at one extreme are favoured; the mean shifts in that direction. Disruptive: both extremes are favoured over the intermediate; can produce two distinct groups.
    B1 each.
  2. (b) Babies of very low birth mass have a higher death rate (less able to keep warm/more infections) and very high birth mass increases complications in birth; intermediate birth masses have the highest survival, so the mean is maintained and variation is reduced.
    B1 extremes have higher mortality; B1 intermediate favoured/mean maintained.
Q3Natural & artificial selection · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a mutation that is harmful in one environment may be beneficial in another. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The mutation arises at random and is not caused by the environment. When the environment changes, the altered phenotype gives higher survival and reproduction, so the allele is passed on more often and its frequency increases.
    1 mark each for any three: mutation is random and not caused by the environment; the environment changes (or the organism moves); the phenotype now gives an advantage / higher fitness; these individuals survive and reproduce more; the allele frequency increases.
Q4Natural & artificial selection · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It is a slow-growing organism that is treated with antibiotics.

  1. (a) State what is meant by the term 'selection pressure'. [1]
  2. (b) Tuberculosis (TB) is treated with a combination of several antibiotics taken together for at least six months, rather than with one antibiotic alone. Suggest why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any environmental factor (for example a drug, predator or climate) that causes differences in survival and reproduction between individuals with different phenotypes.
    1 mark: a factor that affects survival/reproduction, so that some phenotypes/alleles are favoured over others.
  2. (b) Resistance to any one drug arises from a rare mutation. A bacterium that is resistant to all the drugs at once would need several rare mutations together, which is very unlikely, so the combination is far less likely to select a resistant population.
    1 mark each for any two: resistance to one drug needs a rare mutation; resistance to several drugs needs several mutations together, which is very unlikely; a drug still kills any bacteria with resistance to another drug; the combination makes resistant survivors rare.
Q5Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Sahiwal cattle are a dairy breed from Punjab, valued for tolerating heat and resisting ticks. Breeders want to increase their milk yield.

  1. (a) Describe how breeders could use artificial selection to increase the milk yield of Sahiwal cattle over many generations. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why breeders should also keep selecting for heat tolerance while selecting for milk yield. [1]
  3. (c) State one difference between artificial selection and natural selection. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Measure milk yield of cows and select those with the highest yield; bulls cannot be measured directly, so select bulls whose mothers/daughters (progeny testing) have high yields; breed selected cows with selected bulls (e.g. by artificial insemination); select the highest-yielding offspring and breed them; repeat over many generations so the frequency of alleles for high yield increases.
    1 mark each for any four of the points.
  2. (b) Selecting only for yield may reduce/lose alleles for heat tolerance or tick resistance, which are needed in Pakistan's climate.
    B1.
  3. (c) In artificial selection humans choose which individuals breed, for characteristics useful to humans; in natural selection the environment/selection pressures decide which survive and reproduce.
    B1.
Q6Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Maize farmers in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa buy new hybrid maize seed every year. The seed is produced by crossing two inbred (homozygous) lines.

  1. (a) Explain why the inbred lines are often small and produce low yields. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the F1 hybrid plants are vigorous and uniform. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why farmers do not save seed from their hybrid crop to sow the next year. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Repeated inbreeding increases homozygosity; harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be expressed in the homozygous state (inbreeding depression).
    B1 homozygous; B1 harmful recessive alleles expressed.
  2. (b) Hybrids are heterozygous at many loci, so harmful recessive alleles are masked (hybrid vigour); both parents are homozygous, so all F1 plants have the same genotype and are uniform.
    B1 heterozygous/hybrid vigour; B1 identical genotypes, uniform.
  3. (c) Seed from F1 × F1 is the F2 generation; alleles segregate, producing many different genotypes, so plants are not uniform and many have lower yield.
    B1 segregation/variety of genotypes in F2; B1 not uniform/lower yield.
Q7Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Stem rust is a fungal disease that can destroy wheat crops. Wheat breeders in Pakistan cross local high-yielding varieties with varieties that carry a resistance allele.

  1. (a) Outline how breeders could produce a high-yielding variety that is resistant to stem rust. [3]
  2. (b) New races of the stem rust fungus sometimes appear that can infect previously resistant wheat. Explain how this happens. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cross a high-yielding variety with a resistant variety; grow offspring and expose them to the fungus, selecting plants that are resistant and have high yield; breed these together/backcross to the high-yielding parent for several generations, selecting each time, until the variety is true-breeding for both features.
    B1 cross the two varieties; B1 select resistant, high-yield offspring (test by infecting); B1 repeat for many generations/backcrossing.
  2. (b) Mutation in the fungus produces a new allele allowing it to infect resistant wheat; widely planted resistant wheat is a selection pressure; fungi with the new allele reproduce, so the new race spreads/allele frequency increases.
    B1 mutation in fungus; B1 resistant wheat selects for it; B1 the new race survives, reproduces and spreads.
Q8Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Rock pocket mice live in deserts in North America. Most mice living on pale sandy rock have pale fur; most mice living on dark lava flows have dark fur. Dark fur is caused by a dominant allele of the Mc1r gene. Owls, which hunt by sight, are their main predators.

  1. (a) Explain how natural selection has led to most mice on lava flows having dark fur. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why a new dominant mutation for dark fur could spread through a population faster than a new recessive mutation would. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) There is variation in fur colour in the population, caused by different alleles; owls see and catch pale mice more easily on dark rock (predation is the selection pressure); dark mice are camouflaged, so more survive and reproduce; they pass on the dark allele, so its frequency increases over generations.
    1 mark each for: variation/alleles; predation as selection pressure; dark mice survive and reproduce; allele passed on/frequency increases.
  2. (b) A dominant allele is expressed in heterozygotes, so it shows in the phenotype immediately and is selected for at once; a recessive allele is hidden in heterozygotes and only selected when homozygous, which is rare at first.
    B1 dominant expressed in heterozygote/visible to selection; B1 recessive hidden until homozygous.
Q9Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Cotton bollworms are pests of cotton in Punjab. Many farms grow Bt cotton, which produces a toxin from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis that kills bollworm larvae that feed on it. Farmers are advised to plant a 'refuge': a block of non-Bt cotton near each Bt field.

  1. (a) Explain how the refuge slows the spread of resistance to the Bt toxin in the bollworm population. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Non-Bt refuge plants let susceptible bollworms survive without exposure to the toxin. Resistance is rare and recessive, so resistant moths that mate with susceptible moths produce mostly heterozygous or susceptible offspring, which are killed on the Bt cotton. The resistance allele therefore stays at a low frequency.
    1 mark each for any four: susceptible bollworms survive on refuge plants (no selection there); the resistance allele is rare and recessive; resistant adults mate at random with susceptible adults from the refuge; their offspring are heterozygous or susceptible and are killed by the Bt toxin; so the resistance allele frequency stays low and the toxin stays effective.
Q10Natural & artificial selection · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A population of 1,200 green lizards and 800 brown lizards lived in a mixed habitat. A bird predator fed on the lizards for one season. At the end of the season, 360 green lizards and 560 brown lizards had survived.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of each colour of lizard that survived. Give each answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) State which colour was favoured by natural selection, and name the type of selection shown by these results. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage of the surviving lizards that were brown. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Green: 30.0 %; brown: 70.0 %.
    1 mark each: green 360 ÷ 1200 × 100 = 30.0 %; brown 560 ÷ 800 × 100 = 70.0 %.
  2. (b) Brown was favoured (70 % survived compared with 30 % of green). This is directional selection, because one extreme phenotype is favoured and the population shifts towards brown.
    1 mark: brown (higher survival); 1 mark: directional selection (one phenotype favoured, the mean shifts).
  3. (c) 60.9 %
    1 mark for 560 ÷ (360 + 560) = 0.6087; 1 mark for 60.9 %. Compared with 40 % of the population at the start, brown is more common among survivors.
Q11Natural & artificial selection · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Mosquitoes of the genus Anopheles transmit malaria. In one district, the same insecticide was sprayed in houses every year. The percentage of mosquitoes surviving a standard dose was: year 1 – 2 %; year 3 – 9 %; year 5 – 31 %; year 7 – 68 %. Spraying then stopped. In year 10 the survival was 40 %.

  1. (a) Explain the change in survival between years 1 and 7. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why survival fell between year 7 and year 10. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Some mosquitoes already carried a resistance allele (from mutation) at a low frequency (2 % survival); the insecticide killed non-resistant mosquitoes – a selection pressure; resistant mosquitoes survived and reproduced, passing on the allele; the frequency of the resistance allele increased each generation (directional selection), so survival rose to 68 %.
    1 mark each for any four of: pre-existing allele/mutation; selection pressure; survivors reproduce; allele passed on; allele frequency increases; directional selection; use of data.
  2. (b) Without insecticide there is no advantage to resistance; the resistance allele may carry a cost (e.g. lower fertility or slower development), so non-resistant mosquitoes are selected for/out-reproduce resistant ones and the allele frequency falls.
    B1 no selection for resistance; B1 resistant individuals at a disadvantage without insecticide.
Q12Natural & artificial selection · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Sickle cell anaemia is caused by the allele Hbˢ. People with genotype HbˢHbˢ have sickle cell anaemia; heterozygotes (HbᴬHbˢ) are mostly healthy and are less likely to die from severe malaria than HbᴬHbᴬ people. The Hbˢ allele is much more common in parts of Africa where malaria is common than in places without malaria.

  1. (a) Explain why the Hbˢ allele remains at a high frequency in areas where malaria is common. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest why the frequency of Hbˢ is falling slowly among descendants of people who moved from these areas to countries without malaria. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) HbˢHbˢ people often die young/have fewer children, so Hbˢ alleles are lost; HbᴬHbᴬ people are more likely to die of malaria, so Hbᴬ alleles are lost; heterozygotes have a selective advantage – they survive malaria and reproduce, passing on both alleles; so both alleles are kept in the population (balanced polymorphism/heterozygote advantage).
    1 mark each for: selection against HbˢHbˢ; selection against HbᴬHbᴬ by malaria; heterozygote advantage; both alleles maintained.
  2. (b) Malaria is no longer a selection pressure, so heterozygotes have no advantage; HbˢHbˢ individuals are still selected against, so the allele frequency falls slowly (slowly because most Hbˢ alleles are carried in heterozygotes, where they are not selected against).
    B1 no heterozygote advantage without malaria; B1 selection against homozygotes only, so slow decline.
Q13Natural & artificial selection · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

On an island, a severe drought killed most plants that produce small, soft seeds, leaving mainly large, hard seeds. The mean beak depth of a population of seed-eating finches was 9.2 mm before the drought and 9.7 mm in the generation hatched after it.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in mean beak depth. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Name the type of selection shown and explain how the change happened. [4]
  3. (c) Explain why the change in mean beak depth would not have occurred if beak depth were controlled only by the environment. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.43 %
    M1 (9.7 − 9.2) ÷ 9.2 × 100; A1 5.43 %.
  2. (b) Directional selection; beak depth varies and is partly genetic (polygenic); during the drought only birds with deeper beaks could crack the large, hard seeds; so they survived and bred, while birds with shallow beaks starved; alleles for deep beaks were passed on, so the mean in the next generation increased.
    B1 directional; B1 variation with genetic basis; B1 deep-beaked birds survived/reproduced (food as selection pressure); B1 alleles passed on/mean shifted.
  3. (c) Characteristics caused only by the environment are not inherited, so offspring would not show the change.
    B1.
Q14Natural & artificial selection · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

In a population of finches, a gene has two alleles, S and s. At birth, 500 individuals were counted: 80 were SS, 240 were Ss and 180 were ss. The probability of surviving to breed was 0.60 for SS, 0.80 for Ss and 0.90 for ss.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of each genotype that survived to breed. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the frequency of allele S among the survivors. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the change in the frequency of allele S from its value at birth (0.40) is evidence of natural selection. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) SS: 48; Ss: 192; ss: 162; total surviving 402.
    1 mark each: 0.60 × 80 = 48; 0.80 × 240 = 192; 0.90 × 180 = 162; total 402 (1 mark for total or consistent working).
  2. (b) 0.358 (288 ÷ 804 = 0.358)
    1 mark for counting S alleles: (2 × 48) + 192 = 288 out of 2 × 402 = 804 alleles; 1 mark for 0.358.
  3. (c) The change follows genotype: SS survive worst (0.60) and ss best (0.90), so the survival differences between genotypes favour alleles of the ss type over S. This is systematic selection based on phenotype, not the random sampling changes expected from genetic drift.
    1 mark: survival differs between genotypes (fitness differs); 1 mark: the change is predictable and systematic (directional), unlike random genetic drift.
Q15Natural & artificial selection · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Dachshunds were bred for a long body and short legs. Many now have spinal problems. Breeders in some lines select dogs for extreme, showy body shapes and mate closely related animals.

  1. (a) Explain why selecting for an extreme body shape can reduce the fitness of a breed. [3]
  2. (b) Explain how close inbreeding increases the chance that harmful recessive alleles are expressed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The alleles that produce the extreme shape often have harmful side-effects (pleiotropy), or are linked to alleles that damage the spine. Breeders select the extreme trait, which also selects these harmful alleles, so they increase in frequency and reduce survival or reproduction.
    1 mark each for any three: the trait has harmful side-effects / pleiotropy; harmful alleles are linked to the trait; selection for the trait also selects the harmful alleles; the harmful alleles increase in frequency; reduced survival or reproduction lowers fitness.
  2. (b) Close relatives share alleles inherited from a common ancestor, so their offspring are more likely to receive the same recessive allele from both parents and be homozygous for it. The harmful phenotype is then expressed.
    1 mark: relatives share alleles; 1 mark: offspring more likely to be homozygous recessive, so the harmful allele is expressed.
Q16Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define the term 'species' using the biological species concept. [2]
  2. (b) State the difference between allopatric speciation and sympatric speciation. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, and are reproductively isolated from other such groups.
    B1 interbreed to produce fertile offspring; B1 reproductively isolated from other species.
  2. (b) Allopatric: populations are separated by a geographical barrier. Sympatric: a new species forms in the same area without geographical separation (e.g. by polyploidy or behavioural isolation).
    B1 each.
Q17Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State two differences between genetic drift and natural selection. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why both processes change the gene pool of a population. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Drift is random/due to chance, selection is non-random (depends on phenotype and environment); drift has most effect in small populations, selection acts in any size population; selection leads to adaptation, drift does not.
    1 mark each for any two.
  2. (b) Both change the frequency of alleles in the population from one generation to the next; alleles can be lost or become fixed.
    B1 change allele frequencies; B1 alleles lost/fixed.
Q18Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Core · Paper 4 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by 'reproductive isolation'. [1]
  2. (b) Give one prezygotic barrier and one postzygotic barrier to reproduction, each with an example. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so gene flow between them stops.
    1 mark: no interbreeding (or no fertile offspring), so no gene flow.
  2. (b) Prezygotic: for example different breeding seasons or habitats, or different courtship/song (no mating or no fertilisation). Postzygotic: for example hybrid offspring that are sterile (such as a mule) or fail to develop.
    1 mark for a prezygotic barrier with a correct example; 1 mark for a postzygotic barrier with a correct example.
Q19Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Core · Paper 4 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State two ways in which the allele frequencies of a population can change without natural selection. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: genetic drift (random change, especially in a small population); the founder effect; a bottleneck; migration (gene flow) in or out; mutation.
    1 mark each for any two of the listed causes.
Q20Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A species of freshwater fish lived in one large lake. Over thousands of years the water level fell and the lake split into two smaller lakes, one with clear, rocky water and one with muddy water and dense weed.

  1. (a) Explain how two separate species of fish could evolve from the original population. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) The two populations are geographically isolated, so no gene flow/interbreeding between them; different mutations arise in each population; different selection pressures in each lake (e.g. food, predators, water clarity) favour different alleles; genetic drift may also change allele frequencies (especially in small populations); over many generations the gene pools become so different that the two populations could no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring even if they met (reproductive isolation) – allopatric speciation.
    1 mark each for any five of: geographical isolation; no gene flow; different mutations; different selection pressures (with example); drift; allele frequencies change; reproductive isolation/cannot produce fertile offspring.
Q21Speciation, drift & molecular evidence · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) is hexaploid with 2n = 42. It is thought to have arisen when tetraploid emmer wheat (2n = 28) hybridised with a wild grass, Aegilops tauschii (2n = 14).

  1. (a) Calculate the number of chromosomes in the hybrid formed from one gamete of each parent. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why this hybrid was sterile. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how a fertile hexaploid species could have formed from the hybrid in the same place as its parents, and why this is an example of sympatric speciation. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 21
    B1 14 + 7 = 21.
  2. (b) The chromosomes from the two parents are not homologous, so they cannot pair up in prophase I of meiosis; viable gametes cannot be formed.

    1 mark each for any six: IV and DV defined with units; a zero control with no added nitrate; same starting number of fronds, volume and stock; controlled light and temperature; at least three replicates per concentration with random allocation; counts at start and end over a fixed time; mean, range and graph.

Q128Skill: variables & controls · Standard · Paper 5 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) A student changes both pH and temperature at the same time in an experiment on enzyme activity. Explain why this makes the results difficult to interpret, and describe how the design should be changed. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Changes in activity cannot be assigned to pH or to temperature, because the two effects may interact or cancel each other. The design should change one independent variable at a time, keeping the other constant (for example, a pH series at one fixed temperature, then a temperature series at the best pH).
    1 mark: cannot tell which variable caused the change (confounded); 1 mark: the effects may interact; 1 mark: change one variable at a time while holding the other constant (or use separate experiments).

Q130Skill: variables & controls · Standard · Paper 5 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Compare a positive control and a negative control in a test for protein using biuret reagent, and explain why each is used. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A positive control is a solution known to contain protein (for example albumin) that gives a purple (violet) colour, showing that the reagent works. A negative control uses water or a solution with no protein, which stays blue, showing that a colour change is not caused by the reagent itself or by the water.
    1 mark: positive control contains known protein and turns purple; 1 mark: negative control has no protein and stays blue; 1 mark: the controls show the reagent works and that a positive result is genuinely due to protein.

Q132Skill: variables & controls · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a single-variable design is needed, and describe a study in which two variables are confounded. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A single-variable design means that only the independent variable changes, so any change in the dependent variable can be attributed to it. Example of confounding: a sunny site is given one fertiliser and a shaded site a different fertiliser, so the differences in growth could be due to light, to the fertiliser, or to both.
    1 mark: only one variable changes, so the effect can be attributed to it; 1 mark: a valid confounded design described; 1 mark: the effects cannot be separated, so the conclusion is invalid.
Q133Skill: variables & controls · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a variable that is controlled by design and a variable that is randomised. Choose a suitable method for a field study of leaf damage across tree heights, and justify it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A controlled variable is kept the same for all samples (for example the species of tree or the time of sampling). A randomised variable is one that cannot be kept constant, so it is allocated or sampled at random so that its effects are spread across the groups. For leaf damage at different heights, sample leaves at each height using a random method (for example random numbers on a branch) and record variables that cannot be controlled (aspect, light, age of leaf) so that they can be compared or randomised.
    1 mark: controlled variable held constant; 1 mark: randomised variable spread at random so effects average out; 1 mark: a suitable random sampling method for leaves at each height; 1 mark: recording or randomising uncontrollable factors such as light or aspect.
Q134Skill: variables & controls · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A survey finds that children who eat more fried food have higher body mass index (BMI). List two possible confounding variables and describe how each could be controlled or allowed for. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Confounding variables: physical activity (less active children may eat more fried food and have higher BMI); family income or sugar intake (may affect both diet and BMI). Control: measure physical activity and record it, then compare children with similar activity; match groups for age and income; use statistical analysis to adjust for these variables. Even so, the survey cannot prove that fried food causes higher BMI.
    1 mark each for two valid confounding variables; 1 mark each for two suitable controls or allowances (measuring and matching, or statistical adjustment).
Q135Skill: variables & controls · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A trial tests a herbal remedy for headaches. Explain why a placebo and a double-blind design are used. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A placebo (an inactive tablet that looks the same) controls for the effect of expectation, because people who expect a benefit may report one even without the active ingredient. A double-blind design means that neither the participants nor the researchers who assess the headaches know who received the remedy or the placebo, which prevents observer bias and expectation from affecting the results. Random allocation spreads other differences between the groups evenly.
    1 mark: a placebo controls for expectation/placebo effect; 1 mark: double-blind stops participants knowing the treatment (expectation); 1 mark: double-blind stops researchers biasing the assessment; 1 mark: random allocation spreads other variables evenly.
