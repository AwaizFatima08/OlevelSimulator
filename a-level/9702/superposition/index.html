Q2Superposition principle & stationary waves · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State the principle of superposition. [2]
  2. (b) State two differences between a stationary wave and a progressive wave. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how a stationary wave is formed on a stretched string fixed at both ends. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements.
    B1: waves meet/overlap at a point; B1: resultant displacement = (vector) sum of individual displacements.
  2. (b) Stationary: no net energy transfer; amplitude varies with position (nodes and antinodes); points between adjacent nodes are in phase.
    B1 each for any two: stationary wave transfers no energy (progressive does); amplitude varies from zero at nodes to maximum at antinodes (progressive: same amplitude everywhere); all points between two adjacent nodes vibrate in phase (progressive: phase changes continuously with distance); wave profile does not move along.
  3. (c) Waves travel along the string and reflect at the fixed ends; the incident and reflected waves, of the same frequency and speed, travelling in opposite directions, superpose.
    B1: wave reflected at the end; B1: two waves of same frequency/wavelength (and similar amplitude) travelling in opposite directions superpose/interfere.
Q3Superposition principle & stationary waves · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A plastic pipe of length 0.68 m is open at both ends. Air in the pipe is made to vibrate. Speed of sound = 340 m s⁻¹. Ignore end corrections.

  1. (a) State where the nodes and antinodes are when the pipe sounds its lowest note. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the frequency of the lowest note. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the frequency of the next higher note the pipe can produce. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Antinodes at both open ends; one node at the centre.
    B1: antinode at each open end; B1: single node in the middle.
  2. (b) 250 Hz
    Length = λ/2 → λ = 1.36 m (M1); f = 340/1.36 = 250 Hz (A1).
  3. (c) 500 Hz
    Two nodes, λ = 0.68 m → f = 500 Hz (A1).
Q4Superposition principle & stationary waves · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Two progressive waves of the same frequency and amplitude 4.0 cm meet at a point in phase. What is the amplitude of the resultant wave at that point? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C 8.0 cm
    Waves in phase reinforce: the displacements add, giving 4.0 + 4.0 = 8.0 cm. Zero is the result for antiphase waves; 4.0 cm ignores one wave; 16 cm squares the amplitude, which is not how amplitudes add.
Q5Superposition principle & stationary waves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A string of length 1.20 m is fixed at both ends and made to vibrate by a vibration generator. When the frequency is 150 Hz a stationary wave with three loops (three antinodes) is formed.

  1. (a) Calculate the wavelength of the waves on the string. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the speed of the waves on the string. [1]
  3. (c) Determine the lowest frequency at which a stationary wave can be formed on this string, and the frequency that gives four loops. [2]
  4. (d) State the number of nodes, including the ends, when there are four loops. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.80 m
    Three loops = 3 × λ/2 = 1.20 m → λ = 0.80 m (A1).
  2. (b) 120 m s⁻¹
    v = fλ = 150 × 0.80 = 120 m s⁻¹ (A1).
  3. (c) 50 Hz and 200 Hz
    Fundamental: λ = 2.40 m, f = 120/2.40 = 50 Hz (A1); four loops: λ = 0.60 m, f = 200 Hz (A1).
  4. (d) 5
    B1: four loops have nodes at both ends plus three between = 5.
Q6Superposition principle & stationary waves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A vertical tube, open at the top, has its lower end in water so that the length of the air column can be changed. A tuning fork of frequency 512 Hz is held over the top. Loud sounds are heard when the air column has lengths 0.165 m and 0.505 m (the first two resonances).

  1. (a) Explain why a loud sound is heard at certain lengths of the air column. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the speed of sound in the tube. [2]
  3. (c) The antinode at the open end is slightly outside the tube. Use the data to find how far outside the tube it is. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A stationary wave forms in the air column (resonance) with a node at the water surface and an antinode near the open end.
    B1: sound reflected at water surface forms stationary wave; B1: resonance when the column length allows a node at the closed end and antinode at the open end (natural frequency = fork frequency).
  2. (b) 348 m s⁻¹
    Difference between successive resonance lengths = λ/2 (M1): λ = 2 × 0.340 = 0.680 m; v = 512 × 0.680 = 348 m s⁻¹ (A1).
  3. (c) 0.005 m (about 5 mm)
    First resonance: l₁ + e = λ/4 (M1); e = 0.170 − 0.165 = 0.005 m (A1).

Q8Superposition principle & stationary waves · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A stationary wave of wavelength 0.60 m is set up on a long rope. One fixed end is at x = 0, which is a node. Points P, Q and R on the rope are at x = 0.15 m, x = 0.20 m and x = 0.45 m.

  1. (a) State the positions of all the nodes between x = 0 and x = 0.90 m inclusive. [1]
  2. (b) State the phase difference between the oscillations of (i) P and Q, (ii) P and R. [2]
  3. (c) At one instant P is at its maximum upward displacement. Describe the displacements of Q and R at this instant. [2]
  4. (d) State the displacement of every point on the rope a quarter of a period later. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) x = 0, 0.30 m, 0.60 m, 0.90 m
    B1: nodes every λ/2 = 0.30 m, starting at the fixed end.
  2. (b) (i) 0; (ii) 180° (π rad)
    B1: P (x = 0.15 m) and Q (x = 0.20 m) are in the same loop → in phase; B1: R (x = 0.45 m) is in the adjacent loop → antiphase.
  3. (c) Q is displaced upwards by a smaller amount (it is at its own maximum); R is at its maximum downward displacement.
    B1: Q upward, smaller amplitude than P (P is at an antinode); B1: R maximum displacement downward (R is also at an antinode, opposite phase).
  4. (d) Zero (the rope is momentarily straight).
    B1: all points pass through equilibrium at the same time.

  3. (c) The best-fit line through the origin has gradient 24.0 m s⁻¹. Determine the speed of the waves on the string. [1]

  2. (b) 1/L / m⁻¹: 2.50, 2.00, 1.67, 1.43, 1.25, 1.11

Q10Superposition principle & stationary waves · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Two coherent waves, each of amplitude 3.0 cm, meet at a point P. The path difference from the two sources to P is 2.5λ. What is the resultant amplitude at P? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 0 cm
    A path difference of 2.5λ is a phase difference of 5π, i.e. the waves are in antiphase (equivalent to π). Equal amplitudes cancel: resultant 3.0 − 3.0 = 0. 6.0 cm is the in-phase case; 3.0 cm and 1.5 cm do not follow from any path difference here.

Q12Superposition principle & stationary waves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A microwave transmitter faces a flat metal plate. A detector is moved along the line between them. The distance between the first and the tenth minimum readings (9 intervals) is 13.5 cm. Speed of electromagnetic waves = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Explain why the detector reading rises and falls as it is moved. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the wavelength and the frequency of the microwaves. [3]
  3. (c) The readings at the minima are not zero. Suggest why, and explain why the minima are smaller close to the plate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Incident and reflected microwaves superpose to form a stationary wave; maxima at antinodes and minima at nodes.
    B1: reflected wave superposes with incident wave (same frequency, opposite directions); B1: stationary wave with nodes (minima) and antinodes (maxima).
  2. (b) λ = 0.030 m; f = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz
    9 node spacings = 13.5 cm → node spacing 1.50 cm = λ/2 (M1); λ = 0.030 m (A1); f = c/λ = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz (A1).
  3. (c) The reflected wave has a smaller amplitude than the incident wave (some energy absorbed/spread out), so cancellation is incomplete; near the plate the two amplitudes are more nearly equal.
    B1: amplitudes of incident and reflected waves not equal → no complete cancellation; B1: reflected wave spreads out with distance from the plate, so the amplitudes are closest near the plate.
Q13Superposition principle & stationary waves · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A guitar string of vibrating length 0.65 m sounds its fundamental frequency of 196 Hz.

  1. (a) Calculate the speed of the waves on the string. [2]
  2. (b) The player presses the string against a fret so that the vibrating length is shorter and the fundamental frequency becomes 247 Hz. The tension does not change. Calculate the new vibrating length. [2]
  3. (c) The string makes a sound wave in air of frequency 247 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. Calculate the wavelength of this sound and explain why it differs from the wavelength of the wave on the string. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 255 m s⁻¹
    Fundamental: λ = 2L = 1.30 m (M1); v = 196 × 1.30 = 255 m s⁻¹ (A1).
  2. (b) 0.516 m
    Same wave speed (M1); L = v/(2f) = 255/(2 × 247) = 0.516 m (A1).
  3. (c) 1.38 m; the frequency is the same but the wave speed in air is different.
    λ = 340/247 = 1.38 m (A1); B1: frequency is fixed by the source; speed in air differs from speed on string, so λ = v/f differs.

  3. (c) For d = 2.0 μm, the 4th-order red maximum would need sinθ = 4 × 700/2000 = 1.4, which is greater than 1, so that order cannot exist.
    1 mark for sinθ > 1 for the 4th order; 1 mark for conclusion that no overlap occurs.

  3. (c) Calculate the angle of the highest observable order for the violet light. [2]

  3. (c) The frequency of the loudspeaker is doubled. Calculate the new angle of the first minimum. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) a sinθ = λ, where a is the width of the opening and θ is the angle from the normal to the first minimum.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) 13.6°
    λ = v/f = 340/1700 = 0.20 m; sinθ = 0.20/0.85 = 0.235, so θ = 13.6°.
  3. (c) 6.8°
    λ halves to 0.10 m; sinθ = 0.10/0.85 = 0.118, so θ = 6.8°.
Q49Diffraction · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A single narrow slit is illuminated with monochromatic light. The width of the slit is reduced from 0.50 mm to 0.10 mm. Describe the change to the diffraction pattern. [2]
  2. (b) Explain this change in terms of the first-minimum condition. [2]
  3. (c) State by what factor the angular position of the first minimum changes when the slit is narrowed as in (a), assuming small angles. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The central maximum becomes wider and the whole pattern spreads out; the bright and dark bands are further apart.
    1 mark for wider central maximum; 1 mark for pattern spreading out.
  2. (b) The first minimum satisfies a sinθ = λ; a smaller slit width a gives a larger sinθ and hence a larger angle, so the pattern spreads more.
    1 mark for a sinθ = λ; 1 mark for smaller a gives larger θ.
  3. (c) The angle increases by a factor of five (because a decreases by a factor of five).
    1 mark.
Q50Diffraction · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why it is easier to hear a person speaking around a wall than to see them, using typical wavelength values. The wavelength of sound in air is between about 0.3 m and 3 m; the wavelength of visible light is about 500 nm. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Diffraction is significant when the wavelength is comparable to or greater than the size of the obstacle or opening. Sound wavelengths (about 0.3–3 m) are comparable to doorways and walls, so sound diffracts strongly; light (about 500 nm) is much smaller than objects, so it diffracts negligibly and casts sharp shadows.
    1 mark for diffraction depends on λ compared with the aperture or obstacle; 1 mark for sound wavelength comparable to door and wall size; 1 mark for light wavelength far smaller, so little diffraction.

  3. (c) The slit is narrowed to 0.050 mm. State the new width of the central maximum. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The central maximum lies between the first minima on either side, at angles ±λ/a; the other maxima lie between successive minima, which are closer together, so they are narrower.
    1 mark for central maximum between two first minima; 1 mark for other maxima being between closer minima.
  2. (b) 25.3 mm

  2. (b) Determine whether a second minimum exists and, if so, its angle. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 14.5°
    sinθ = λ/a = 3.0/12 = 0.25, so θ = 14.5°.
  2. (b) Yes, at 30°
    The second minimum satisfies a sinθ = 2λ; sinθ = 2 × 3.0/12 = 0.50, so θ = 30°. It exists because sinθ < 1.
Q55Diffraction · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

  2. (b) State which wavelength spreads more and explain why. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the difference between the two angles. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 589 nm: 0.675°; 450 nm: 0.516°
    sinθ = λ/a: 589 nm gives sinθ = 0.01178, θ = 0.675°; 450 nm gives sinθ = 0.00900, θ = 0.516°.
  2. (b) 589 nm spreads more: its λ/a is larger, so the first minimum is further from the central maximum.

    Difference = 0.675 − 0.516 = 0.16°.
Q56Diffraction · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A laser beam of wavelength 650 nm passes through an aperture of width 2.0 mm. Approximating the spread of the beam by the first-minimum formula sinθ = λ/a, calculate the angle θ. [2]
  2. (b) The beam travels 50 m. Estimate the diameter of the spot formed on a wall 50 m from the aperture. [2]
  3. (c) State one reason why the spot is larger than the aperture. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.25 × 10⁻⁴ rad (≈ 0.019°)
    sinθ ≈ θ = 650 × 10⁻⁹/2.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.25 × 10⁻⁴ rad.
  2. (b) 3.3 cm
    Diameter ≈ 2 × D × θ = 2 × 50 × 3.25 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.3 cm (≈ 3 cm).
  3. (c) The beam diffracts (spreads) as it passes through the aperture, because the aperture width is not much larger than the wavelength.
    1 mark.

  1. (a) Calculate the wavelength of each radio signal. [2]
  2. (b) A village lies in a valley behind a range of hills about 1 km wide. Explain why the long-wave signal can be received in the village but the FM signal cannot. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1520 m and 3.0 m
    λ = c/f: 3.00 × 10⁸/198 × 10³ = 1520 m (A1); 3.00 × 10⁸/100 × 10⁶ = 3.0 m (A1).
  2. (b) The long-wave wavelength is comparable to the size of the hills, so it diffracts significantly around/over them; the FM wavelength is much smaller, so it hardly diffracts and the village is in its 'shadow'.

  2. (b) A student uses a slit of width 0.50 μm with the same light. Explain why no first minimum is observed. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.00 μm
    a = λ/sinθ = 500 × 10⁻⁹/0.500 = 1.00 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  2. (b) For this slit sinθ = λ/a = 500/500 = 1.0, which would require θ = 90°. This angle cannot be observed, so the first minimum does not appear within the pattern.
    1 mark for sinθ = 1 (θ = 90°); 1 mark for conclusion that no minimum is seen.
Q59Diffraction · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) An ultrasound transmitter emits waves of frequency 50 kHz in water. The speed of sound in water is 1480 m s⁻¹. Calculate the wavelength in water. [2]
  2. (b) The waves pass through a gap of width 40 mm. Calculate the angle of the first minimum. [2]
  3. (c) The gap is reduced to 2.0 mm. Explain why there is no first minimum in this case. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 29.6 mm
    λ = v/f = 1480/50 000 = 0.0296 m = 29.6 mm.
  2. (b) 47.7°
    sinθ = λ/a = 29.6/40 = 0.740, so θ = 47.7°.
  3. (c) λ/a = 29.6/2.0 = 14.8 is greater than 1, so sinθ cannot equal λ/a; the wave spreads over all directions and no first minimum exists.
    1 mark for λ/a > 1 so no angle exists; 1 mark for waves spreading through all directions.
Q60Diffraction · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Light of wavelength 500 nm is incident on a double slit with slit separation 0.30 mm and slit width 0.050 mm. The screen is 2.0 m away. Calculate the fringe separation of the double-slit pattern. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the angle to the first minimum of the single-slit envelope. [2]
  3. (c) Hence determine the number of bright fringes in the central envelope (the central maximum of the single-slit pattern), including the central fringe. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.33 mm
    x = λD/s = 500 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.0/0.30 × 10⁻³ = 3.33 mm (≈ 3.3 mm).
  2. (b) 0.57°
    The envelope has minima at a sinθ = λ, so sinθ = 500 × 10⁻⁹/0.050 × 10⁻³ = 0.0100 and θ = 0.57°.
  3. (c) 11 bright fringes (n = 0 and n = ±1 to ±5)
    Double-slit maxima occur at sinθ = nλ/s; envelope minima occur at sinθ = mλ/a. The n = ±6 fringes coincide with the first envelope minima (s/a = 6), so they are absent. Maxima with |n| ≤ 5 lie inside the envelope: 2 × 5 + 1 = 11 bright fringes.
