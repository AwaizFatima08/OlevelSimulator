Q1Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Mass of proton = 1.007276 u; mass of neutron = 1.008665 u; mass of ⁴₂He nucleus = 4.001506 u. 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Define mass defect of a nucleus. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the mass defect of a helium-4 nucleus in u. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of helium-4 in MeV. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
    B1.
  2. (b) 0.0304 u
    Δm = 2 × 1.007276 + 2 × 1.008665 − 4.001506 (M1) = 0.0304 u (A1).
  3. (c) 7.07 MeV
    BE = 0.0304 × 931.5 = 28.3 MeV (M1); ÷ 4 = 7.07 MeV (A1).
Q2Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

Speed of light c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) State the equation that relates energy to mass, explaining each symbol. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the energy equivalent of 1.0 g of mass. [1]
  3. (c) The Sun radiates energy at a rate of 3.85 × 10²⁶ W. Calculate the mass the Sun loses each second because of this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) E = mc², where E is energy, m is mass and c is the speed of light in a vacuum.
    B1.
  2. (b) 9.0 × 10¹³ J
    E = 1.0 × 10⁻³ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 9.0 × 10¹³ J (B1).
  3. (c) 4.28 × 10⁹ kg
    m = E/c² (M1) = 3.85 × 10²⁶ ÷ (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.28 × 10⁹ kg (A1).
Q3Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the general shape of a graph of binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number A. [3]
  2. (b) Use the shape of the graph to explain why both fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei release energy. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rises steeply from very low values for the lightest nuclei (with ⁴He a local peak); reaches a broad maximum of about 8.8 MeV near A ≈ 56–62 (iron/nickel); then decreases slowly to about 7.6 MeV for the heaviest nuclei.
    B1 steep rise for small A; B1 maximum ≈ 8.8 MeV around A ≈ 56 (Fe); B1 gradual decrease for large A.
  2. (b) In both cases the products have a higher binding energy per nucleon than the original nuclei (they move towards the peak), so the total binding energy increases and the mass decreases; the mass difference is released as energy.
    B1 products have greater BE per nucleon (closer to peak); B1 increase in total BE / decrease in mass released as energy.
Q4Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A deuteron (²₁H nucleus) has a mass of 2.013553 u. Mass of proton = 1.007276 u; mass of neutron = 1.008665 u.

  1. (a) Show that 1 u is equivalent to about 934 MeV using u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the binding energy of the deuteron in joules, using u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. [2]
  3. (c) Express this binding energy in MeV. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 934 MeV
    E = mc² = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ × 9.00 × 10¹⁶ = 1.494 × 10⁻¹⁰ J (M1); ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J MeV⁻¹ = 934 MeV (A1).
  2. (b) 3.57 × 10⁻¹³ J
    Δm = 1.007276 + 1.008665 − 2.013553 = 0.00239 u (C1); E = Δm × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ × c² = 3.57 × 10⁻¹³ J (A1).
  3. (c) 2.23 MeV
    ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ (B1).
Q5Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A possible fission reaction is ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + x ¹₀n. The total mass of the products is 0.186 u less than the total mass of the reactants. 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Determine the value of x. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the energy released in one fission, in MeV. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how the neutrons produced can lead to a chain reaction, and why a moderator is needed in a reactor. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3
    Nucleon numbers: 235 + 1 = 141 + 92 + x, so x = 3 (B1).
  2. (b) 173 MeV
    E = 0.186 × 931.5 (M1) = 173 MeV (A1).
  3. (c) Each fission releases more than one neutron, and each can cause a further fission; a moderator slows the fast neutrons down so they are more likely to be absorbed by U-235 and cause fission.
    B1 neutrons released go on to cause further fissions; B1 moderator slows neutrons (thermal neutrons more likely to cause fission).
Q6Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

The nucleus ⁵⁶₂₆Fe has a mass of 55.920662 u. Mass of proton = 1.007276 u; mass of neutron = 1.008665 u. 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Calculate the binding energy of an iron-56 nucleus in MeV. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the binding energy per nucleon. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why iron-56 cannot release energy by either fission or fusion. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 492 MeV
    Mass of nucleons = 26 × 1.007276 + 30 × 1.008665 = 56.44913 u (M1); Δm = 0.5285 u (M1); BE = 492 MeV (A1).
  2. (b) 8.79 MeV
    ÷ 56 = 8.79 MeV (B1).
  3. (c) It is at (or very near) the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve; any products of fission or fusion would have lower binding energy per nucleon, so energy would have to be supplied.
    B1 near maximum BE per nucleon; B1 products would have lower BE/nucleon, so energy absorbed not released.
Q7Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

In a fusion reactor, deuterium and tritium fuse: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n. Masses: ²₁H 2.014102 u; ³₁H 3.016049 u; ⁴₂He 4.002603 u; ¹₀n 1.008665 u. (These are atomic masses; the electron masses cancel.) 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy released in one reaction, in MeV. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why fusion requires a very high temperature. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 17.6 MeV
    Δm = (2.014102 + 3.016049) − (4.002603 + 1.008665) (M1) = 0.0189 u (A1); E = 17.6 MeV (A1).
  2. (b) The nuclei are both positively charged and repel; they need very high kinetic energies (high temperature) to overcome the electrostatic repulsion and get close enough for the strong force to act.
    B1 electrostatic repulsion between positive nuclei; B1 high KE to approach closely (within range of strong force).
Q8Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases about 200 MeV. Molar mass of U-235 = 0.235 kg mol⁻¹; Avogadro constant = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; 1 day = 86 400 s.

  1. (a) Show that the energy released by the complete fission of 1.0 kg of uranium-235 is about 8 × 10¹³ J. [3]
  2. (b) A power station produces 600 MW of electrical power with an overall efficiency of 33%. Calculate the mass of uranium-235 used each day. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8.2 × 10¹³ J
    Number of nuclei = (1.0 ÷ 0.235) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.56 × 10²⁴ (M1); energy per fission = 200 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J (M1); total = 8.20 × 10¹³ J (A1).
  2. (b) 1.92 kg
    Input power = 600 ÷ 0.33 = 1820 MW (C1); energy per day = 1.57 × 10¹⁴ J (M1); mass = 1.92 kg (A1).
Q9Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Radium-226 decays by α-emission to radon-222. Atomic masses: Ra-226 226.025410 u; Rn-222 222.017578 u; He-4 4.002603 u. 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Write the nuclear equation for this decay. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the energy released in the decay, in MeV. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the alpha particle carries away most of this energy as kinetic energy. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
    B1.
  2. (b) 4.87 MeV
    Δm = 226.025410 − 222.017578 − 4.002603 = 0.00523 u (M1); E = 4.87 MeV (A1).
  3. (c) Momentum is conserved and the radium was at rest, so the α-particle and radon nucleus have equal and opposite momenta; KE = p²/2m, so the much lighter α-particle has much more KE.

Q10Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Binding energies per nucleon: ²³⁵₉₂U 7.591 MeV; ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba 8.326 MeV; ⁹²₃₆Kr 8.513 MeV. Consider the fission ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n.

  1. (a) Explain why the binding energy of the free neutrons does not appear in the calculation. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total binding energy of each of the three nuclei. [2]
  3. (c) Hence calculate the energy released in the fission. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A free neutron is not bound in a nucleus, so its binding energy is zero.
    B1.
  2. (b) U-235: 1784 MeV; Ba-141: 1174 MeV; Kr-92: 783.2 MeV
    BE = A × (BE per nucleon) (M1); three correct values (A1).
  3. (c) 173 MeV
    Energy released = BE(products) − BE(reactants) (M1) = 1957 − 1784 = 173 MeV (A1).
Q11Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

In the fusion reaction ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n, the energy released is 17.6 MeV, shared as kinetic energy between the helium nucleus and the neutron.

  1. (a) Assuming the initial momentum is negligible, show that the neutron receives about 4/5 of the energy released. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the kinetic energy of the neutron in MeV, using the mass ratio m_α/mₙ = 4.002603/1.008665. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the speed of the neutron, treating it non-relativistically. (mass of neutron = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J) [2]
  4. (d) Suggest why the energy of fusion reactors is captured using a blanket of material surrounding the plasma. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Equal and opposite momenta p; KE = p²/2m, so KEₙ/KE_α = m_α/mₙ ≈ 4; neutron gets 4/(4 + 1) = 4/5.
    M1 equal momenta and KE = p²/2m; A1 ratio 4:1 hence 4/5.
  2. (b) 14.1 MeV
    KEₙ = 17.6 × m_α/(m_α + mₙ) = 17.6 × 4.002603 ÷ 5.011268 (M1) = 14.1 MeV (A1) (accept 14.0 MeV from 17.6 × 4/5).
  3. (c) 5.19 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
    v = √(2E/m) (M1) = √(2 × 14.06 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 5.19 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ (A1) (5.18 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ from 14.0 MeV).
  4. (d) The neutrons are uncharged so magnetic fields cannot contain them; they escape the plasma and deposit their kinetic energy (as heat) in the blanket.
    B1.
Q12Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

In the core of the Sun the net effect of a chain of fusion reactions is 4 ¹₁p → ⁴₂He + 2 ⁰₊₁e + 2ν. Mass of proton = 1.007276 u; mass of ⁴₂He nucleus = 4.001506 u; mass of positron = 0.000549 u. 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy released in this reaction, in MeV, ignoring the neutrinos' mass. [2]
  2. (b) When the annihilation of the two positrons is included, the total energy released per helium nucleus formed is 26.7 MeV. The Sun's power output is 3.85 × 10²⁶ W. Calculate the number of helium nuclei formed in the Sun each second. [2]
  3. (c) Hence calculate the mass of hydrogen (protons) converted each second. (mass of proton = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 24.7 MeV
    Δm = 4 × 1.007276 − 4.001506 − 2 × 0.000549 (M1) = 0.0265 u; E = 24.7 MeV (A1).
  2. (b) 9.01 × 10³⁷ s⁻¹
    26.7 MeV = 4.27 × 10⁻¹² J (C1); N = 3.85 × 10²⁶ ÷ 4.27 × 10⁻¹² = 9.01 × 10³⁷ s⁻¹ (A1).
  3. (c) 6.02 × 10¹¹ kg

Q13Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A D–T fusion reaction (²₁H + ³₁H) releases 17.6 MeV. A fission of U-235 (²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n) releases 200 MeV.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy released per nucleon of reactants for each reaction. [2]
  2. (b) Hence calculate the ratio (energy released per kilogram of fusion fuel) ÷ (energy released per kilogram of fission fuel). [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the fission reaction still releases much more energy per reaction than the fusion reaction. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) fusion 3.52 MeV; fission 0.847 MeV
    Fusion: 17.6 ÷ 5 = 3.52 MeV (B1); fission: 200 ÷ 236 = 0.847 MeV (B1).
  2. (b) 4.2
    Mass ∝ nucleon number, so ratio = 3.52 ÷ 0.847 = 4.2 (B1).
  3. (c) Energy released = change in total binding energy; uranium has 236 nucleons, each gaining about 0.85 MeV of binding energy, so the total is large even though the gain per nucleon is small.
    B1 many more nucleons involved; B1 total = number of nucleons × increase in BE per nucleon.
Q14Mass defect & binding energy (fission/fusion) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Binding energies per nucleon: ⁶₃Li 5.33 MeV; ⁴₂He 7.07 MeV; ³₁H 2.83 MeV.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy released in the reaction ⁶₃Li + ¹₀n → ⁴₂He + ³₁H. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the free neutron contributes nothing to the reactants' binding energy. [1]
  3. (c) This reaction is used to make tritium for fusion reactors. Explain why it releases energy even though neither reaction type is 'fission of heavy' or 'fusion of light' nuclei in the usual sense. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.79 MeV
    Total BE: He-4 = 4 × 7.07 = 28.28 MeV; H-3 = 3 × 2.83 = 8.49 MeV; Li-6 = 6 × 5.33 = 31.98 MeV (M1); energy released = BE(products) − BE(reactants) (M1) = 28.28 + 8.49 − 31.98 = 4.79 MeV (A1).
  2. (b) It is not part of a nucleus, so it has no binding energy.
    B1.
  3. (c) Energy is released whenever the products have a greater total binding energy than the reactants; ⁴He has an unusually high binding energy per nucleon, so the total binding energy increases.
    B1 total BE increases; B1 helium-4 especially tightly bound.

Q17Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.27 years. 1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s.

  1. (a) The number of nuclei decaying per unit time.

Q18Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

  1. (a) 50.0 MBq

    B1.
Q19Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by radioactive decay being (i) spontaneous and (ii) random. [2]

  3. (c) The activity per gram of living wood was the same when the tree died as it is today (atmospheric C-14 level constant) / the sample has not been contaminated.
    B1.

Q22Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Starting from dN/dt = −λN, state the expression for the number N of undecayed nuclei after time t, and explain why the activity follows the same law. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the time for the activity of a sodium-24 sample (half-life 15.0 hours) to fall to 10% of its initial value. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the time to fall from 100% to 50% is the same as the time to fall from 20% to 10%. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) N = N₀e^(−λt); A = λN, and λ is constant, so A = A₀e^(−λt).
    B1 N = N₀e^(−λt); B1 A ∝ N.
  2. (b) 49.8 hours
    0.10 = e^(−λt) so t = ln 10/λ (M1) = 15.0 × ln 10/ln 2 = 49.8 hours (A1).
  3. (c) Decay is exponential: the half-life is constant, independent of the amount present.
    B1.

  1. (a) Calculate the activity that must be prepared. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the activity remaining in the patient 12 hours after injection, ignoring any loss from the body. [2]
Show answer

Q24Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Standard · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

A student records the count rate C (corrected for background) from a short-lived source.t / min: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25C / min⁻¹: 1200, 778, 505, 327, 212, 138ln (C / min⁻¹): 7.09, 6.66, 6.22, 5.79, 5.36, 4.93

  1. (a) Explain why a graph of ln C against t should be a straight line, and state what its gradient represents. [2]
  2. (b) Use the first and last values of ln C to find the gradient. [1]
  3. (c) Hence determine the half-life of the source. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest why it is better to plot ln C against t than to read the half-life directly from a graph of C against t. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C = C₀e^(−λt) gives ln C = ln C₀ − λt (form y = c + mx); gradient = −λ.
    B1 log form; B1 gradient = −λ.
  2. (b) −0.0864 min⁻¹
    (4.93 − 7.09) ÷ 25 = −0.0864 min⁻¹ (B1).
  3. (c) 8.0 min
    λ = 0.0864 min⁻¹ (M1); t½ = 0.693/λ = 8.0 min (A1).
  4. (d) A straight-line graph tests the exponential relationship and uses all the data (best-fit line), reducing the effect of random fluctuations.
    B1.

Q26Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Uranium-238 decays through a chain of short-lived nuclides to stable lead-206; the overall half-life is 4.47 × 10⁹ years. A rock sample contains 0.30 lead-206 nuclei for every uranium-238 nucleus.

  1. (a) Show that the number of uranium-238 nuclei remaining is 1/1.30 of the original number. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the age of the rock. [2]
  3. (c) State two assumptions made. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) N/N₀ = 1/1.30
    Each decayed U nucleus gives one Pb nucleus, so N₀ = N_U + N_Pb = N_U(1 + 0.30) (M1, A1).
  2. (b) 1.69 × 10⁹ years
    t = (t½/ln 2) × ln 1.30 (M1) = 1.69 × 10⁹ years (A1).
  3. (c) No lead-206 was present when the rock formed; no uranium or lead has entered or left the rock (and the intermediate nuclides in the chain are negligible).
    B1 each.

  2. (b) Calculate the mass of cobalt-60 needed. [1]

Polonium-210 (half-life 138 days) emits α-particles of energy 5.30 MeV and has been used as a compact heat source in spacecraft. u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; 1 day = 86 400 s.

  1. (a) Calculate the initial activity of 1.0 g of polonium-210. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the initial thermal power produced, assuming all the α-particle energy is absorbed in the capsule. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the time, in days, for the power to fall to 25 W. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.67 × 10¹⁴ Bq
    N = 1.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ (210 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) = 2.87 × 10²¹ (C1); λ = 5.81 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹ (C1); A = λN = 1.67 × 10¹⁴ Bq (A1).

Q30Radioactive decay (activity, decay constant, half-life) · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A detector placed near a small caesium-137 source (half-life 30.2 years) records a count rate of 480 per minute after correction for background. The detector records only 2.0% of the emissions from the source. u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. 1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s.

  1. (a) Calculate the activity of the source. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the number of caesium-137 nuclei in the source. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the mass of caesium-137 in the source. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest one reason why the detector records only a small fraction of the emissions. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 400 Bq
    Count rate = 480 ÷ 60 = 8.0 s⁻¹ (C1); A = 8.0 ÷ 0.020 = 400 Bq (A1).
  2. (b) 5.51 × 10¹¹
    λ = 7.26 × 10⁻¹⁰ s⁻¹ (C1); N = A/λ = 5.51 × 10¹¹ (A1).
  3. (c) 1.25 × 10⁻¹³ kg
    m = N × 137 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.25 × 10⁻¹³ kg (B1).
  4. (d) Emissions go in all directions and only a small fraction enter the detector window (and not all that enter are detected).
    B1.
