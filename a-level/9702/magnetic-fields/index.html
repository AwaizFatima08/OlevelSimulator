Q1Electromagnetic induction (Faraday, Lenz) · Core · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. [2]
  2. (b) State Lenz's law. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how Lenz's law is a consequence of the principle of conservation of energy. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The magnitude of the induced e.m.f. is proportional to (equal to) the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage.
    M1: induced e.m.f. ∝ / = rate of change of A1: magnetic flux linkage (not just 'flux').
  2. (b) The direction of the induced e.m.f. is such that it tends to produce effects (a current) that oppose the change producing it.
    M1: direction of induced e.m.f./current. A1: opposes the change (in flux linkage) that causes it.
  3. (c) Because the induced current opposes the change, work must be done (e.g. pushing a magnet against a repulsive force) to produce it; this work is the source of the electrical energy. If it aided the change, energy would be created from nothing.
    B1: work has to be done against the opposing force. B1: this work becomes the electrical energy; otherwise energy would be created.

  1. (a) Calculate the magnetic flux linkage of the coil. [2]
  2. (b) The coil is pulled out of the field in 0.050 s. Calculate the average e.m.f. induced. [2]

Q3Electromagnetic induction (Faraday, Lenz) · Core · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

A bar magnet is dropped vertically, north pole first, through the centre of a fixed coil connected to a data logger that records the induced e.m.f.

  1. (a) Describe how the e.m.f. varies with time as the magnet falls through the coil. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the second pulse has a larger peak value. [2]
  3. (c) The coil is connected to a resistor. Explain why the magnet then has a slightly smaller acceleration than g while it is near the coil. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A pulse of e.m.f. as the magnet enters, falling to zero when it is at the centre, then a pulse of opposite sign as it leaves; the second pulse has a larger peak and a shorter duration.
    B1: e.m.f. as the magnet enters, (near) zero in the middle. B1: opposite polarity as it leaves. B1: second peak larger and narrower (magnet moving faster).
  2. (b) The magnet is accelerating, so it moves faster as it leaves; the flux linkage changes at a greater rate, so by Faraday's law the e.m.f. is larger.
    B1: greater speed when leaving. B1: greater rate of change of flux linkage → larger e.m.f.
  3. (c) An induced current flows; by Lenz's law it creates a magnetic field that opposes the motion of the magnet (repels it on entry, attracts it on exit), so there is an upward force on the magnet.
    B1: induced current produces magnetic field. B1: force on magnet opposes its motion (upward force).

  1. (a) Calculate the e.m.f. induced between the wing tips. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why this e.m.f. cannot be used to light a lamp on board by connecting wires between the wing tips. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.378 V
    C1: E = BLv (wings cut the vertical component of the field). A1: E = 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ × 35 × 240 = 0.378 V.

    B1: the wires also cut the field / have an equal e.m.f. induced. B1: e.m.f.s cancel around the circuit, so no net e.m.f./current.

A small coil of 500 turns and area 2.0 cm² is placed inside an electromagnet with its plane perpendicular to the field. Q9Electromagnetic induction (Faraday, Lenz) · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A flat aluminium plate hangs from a pivot as a pendulum. It swings through the gap between the poles of a strong permanent magnet.

  1. (a) Explain why the oscillations of the aluminium plate die away quickly when it swings between the poles of the magnet. [3]
  2. (b) Several narrow slots are cut into the plate. Explain why the plate now swings for much longer. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest one practical use of this effect. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) As the plate moves through the field, the flux through parts of it changes, inducing e.m.f.s and hence eddy currents in the plate; by Lenz's law these currents produce forces that oppose the motion; the plate's kinetic energy is converted to thermal energy in the plate.
    B1: changing flux / plate cuts field lines → e.m.f. induced, eddy currents flow. B1: forces on currents oppose the motion (Lenz). B1: kinetic energy → thermal energy (I²R heating).
  2. (b) The slots break up the paths for the eddy currents, so the currents (and the opposing forces) are much smaller, and less energy is dissipated each swing.
    B1: slots increase resistance / reduce eddy current loops. B1: smaller opposing force / less energy dissipated.
  3. (c) Magnetic braking (e.g. in trains or roller-coasters) or damping the needle of a meter/balance.
    B1: any valid application, e.g. eddy-current brakes, damping a balance.

[15]
Show answer

Show answer
  1. (a) Between the wires the fields of the two currents (in the same direction) are in opposite directions; at some point their magnitudes are equal, so they cancel.

    B1: attract.

  1. (a) BIL = Δm g, so Δm = (BL/g) I

Q60Magnetic fields & force on a current-carrying conductor · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 6 marks

Describe how the student can determine the flux density of the field. State the apparatus, the variables, what is controlled, and how the results are analysed. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Vary the current I through the wire, keeping its length L in the field fixed. Record the change in balance reading (force F) for each current. Plot F against I; the gradient is BL, so B = gradient/L. Keep the wire horizontal and perpendicular to the field, and the same length in the field.
