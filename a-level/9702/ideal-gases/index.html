Q1Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) State four assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases. [4]
  2. (b) Explain, in terms of molecules, how a gas exerts a pressure on the walls of its container. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any four of: molecules are in continuous random motion; collisions (with each other and the walls) are perfectly elastic; the volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the gas; there are no intermolecular forces (except during collisions); the time of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions; there is a very large number of molecules.
    B1 each for any four of the listed assumptions
  2. (b) Molecules collide with the wall and rebound, so their momentum changes; the wall exerts a force on each molecule and so (Newton's third law) the molecules exert a force on the wall; many collisions per second give a steady average force, and pressure = force/area.
    B1 change of momentum on collision with wall; B1 force = rate of change of momentum (and Newton's third law); B1 pressure = total force/area of wall
Q2Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Air in a room is at 27 °C (300 K). Nitrogen molecules have a mass of 28 u. Take the Boltzmann constant k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean translational kinetic energy of a nitrogen molecule. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the root-mean-square speed of the nitrogen molecules. [2]
  3. (c) State how the mean kinetic energy of an oxygen molecule in the same air compares with that of a nitrogen molecule. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J
    ⟨Eₖ⟩ = (3/2)kT (C1) = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J (A1)
  2. (b) 517 m s⁻¹
    m = 28 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg; ½m⟨c²⟩ = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ (C1); c_rms = √(2 × 6.21 × 10⁻²¹/4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 517 m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) It is the same, because the mean kinetic energy depends only on the temperature.
    B1
Q3Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A sealed flask contains an ideal gas. Take the Boltzmann constant k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean translational kinetic energy of the molecules at 100 °C. [2]
  2. (b) A student says that heating the gas from 0 °C to 100 °C makes the mean kinetic energy of the molecules 'infinitely larger'. Calculate the actual ratio (mean KE at 100 °C)/(mean KE at 0 °C) and explain the student's error. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 7.72 × 10⁻²¹ J
    T = 373 K (C1); ⟨Eₖ⟩ = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 373 = 7.72 × 10⁻²¹ J (A1)
  2. (b) ratio = 373/273 = 1.37; the mean KE is proportional to the thermodynamic (kelvin) temperature, not to the Celsius temperature.
    B1 1.37; B1 must use kelvin: ⟨Eₖ⟩ ∝ T in K, and 0 °C is not zero energy
Q4Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Show that the equation pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ can be written as p = ⅓ρ⟨c²⟩, where ρ is the density of the gas. [1]
  2. (b) Air at sea level in Gwadar has a pressure of 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and a density of 1.20 kg m⁻³. Calculate the root-mean-square speed of the air molecules. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why individual air molecules travel much less than 500 m in one second along a straight line. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Nm is the total mass of the gas and Nm/V = ρ, so p = ⅓(Nm/V)⟨c²⟩ = ⅓ρ⟨c²⟩.
    B1
  2. (b) 502 m s⁻¹
    ⟨c²⟩ = 3p/ρ = 3 × 1.01 × 10⁵/1.20 = 2.53 × 10⁵ m² s⁻² (C1); c_rms = 502 m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) They collide very frequently with other molecules, changing direction (random path).
    B1
Q5Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain, in terms of the motion of molecules, why the pressure of a fixed mass of gas increases when it is heated at constant volume. [3]
  2. (b) A sealed can of air at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and 20 °C is left on a car dashboard in Hyderabad in summer, where it reaches 60 °C. Calculate the new pressure. Assume the volume of the can is constant. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Higher temperature → greater mean kinetic energy, so molecules move faster; each collision with the walls gives a larger change in momentum and collisions happen more often; so a larger force per unit area (pressure).
    B1 mean KE/speed of molecules increases; B1 larger momentum change per collision; B1 more frequent collisions → greater rate of change of momentum → greater pressure
  2. (b) 1.14 × 10⁵ Pa
    p/T constant, T in kelvin: 293 K → 333 K (C1); p = 1.0 × 10⁵ × 333/293 = 1.14 × 10⁵ Pa (A1)
Q6Kinetic theory of gases · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

A container of volume 0.040 m³ holds 2.4 × 10²⁴ molecules of nitrogen, each of mass 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. The pressure of the gas is 2.5 × 10⁵ Pa.

  1. (a) Explain what is meant by the root-mean-square speed of the molecules of a gas. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean-square speed ⟨c²⟩ of the molecules. [2]
  3. (c) Hence calculate the r.m.s. speed. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The square root of the mean of the squares of the speeds of all the molecules.
    B1
  2. (b) 2.69 × 10⁵ m² s⁻²
    ⟨c²⟩ = 3pV/(Nm) (C1) = 3 × 2.5 × 10⁵ × 0.040/(2.4 × 10²⁴ × 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 2.69 × 10⁵ m² s⁻² (A1)
  3. (c) 518 m s⁻¹
    √2.69 × 10⁵ = 518 m s⁻¹ (B1)
Q7Kinetic theory of gases · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Take the Boltzmann constant k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Use the equations pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ and pV = NkT to show that the mean translational kinetic energy of a molecule is (3/2)kT. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the temperature at which the mean translational kinetic energy of a helium atom is 1.0 × 10⁻²⁰ J. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ = NkT, so m⟨c²⟩ = 3kT and ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT.
    M1 equate the two expressions; M1 m⟨c²⟩ = 3kT; A1 multiply by ½ to obtain ⟨Eₖ⟩ = ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT
  2. (b) 483 K
    T = 2⟨Eₖ⟩/(3k) (C1) = 2 × 1.0 × 10⁻²⁰/(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³) = 483 K (A1)
Q8Kinetic theory of gases · Standard · Paper 4 style · 4 marks

In a computer model of a gas, five molecules have speeds of 300 m s⁻¹, 400 m s⁻¹, 500 m s⁻¹, 600 m s⁻¹ and 700 m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean speed of the five molecules. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the root-mean-square speed of the five molecules. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the r.m.s. speed is always greater than the mean speed unless all the speeds are equal. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 500 m s⁻¹
    B1 2500/5 = 500 m s⁻¹
  2. (b) 520 m s⁻¹
    ⟨c²⟩ = (9 + 16 + 25 + 36 + 49) × 10⁴/5 = 2.70 × 10⁵ m² s⁻² (C1); c_rms = √(2.70 × 10⁵) = 520 m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) Squaring gives extra weight to the larger speeds, so the square root of the mean of the squares exceeds the mean.
    B1
Q9Kinetic theory of gases · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

Take the Boltzmann constant k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Describe the shape of a graph of c_rms (y-axis) against thermodynamic temperature T (x-axis) for an ideal gas. [2]
  2. (b) The r.m.s. speed of the molecules of a gas at 300 K is v. State the temperature at which the r.m.s. speed is 2v. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the temperature at which the r.m.s. speed of carbon dioxide molecules (mass 44 u) is 400 m s⁻¹. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A curve through the origin with decreasing gradient: c_rms ∝ √T.
    B1 passes through the origin; B1 curve with gradient decreasing (c_rms ∝ √T)
  2. (b) 1200 K
    B1 c_rms ∝ √T so T increases by a factor of 4
  3. (c) 282 K
    T = m⟨c²⟩/(3k) (C1) = 44 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ × 400²/(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³) = 282 K (A1)
Q10Kinetic theory of gases · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Argon is a monatomic gas. Take R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ and k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹.

  1. (a) Explain why, for an ideal monatomic gas, the internal energy is equal to the total translational kinetic energy of its atoms. [2]
  2. (b) Show that the internal energy U of n moles of an ideal monatomic gas at thermodynamic temperature T is (3/2)nRT. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the increase in internal energy when 2.0 mol of argon is heated from 300 K to 400 K. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) There are no intermolecular forces in an ideal gas, so the potential energy of the atoms is zero; a monatomic gas has only translational kinetic energy.
    B1 no intermolecular forces so no potential energy; B1 internal energy = sum of KE (and PE) of the particles
  2. (b) U = N × (3/2)kT, and Nk = nN_A × (R/N_A) = nR, so U = (3/2)nRT.
    M1 U = N(3/2)kT; A1 Nk = nR
  3. (c) 2490 J
    ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT (C1) = 1.5 × 2.0 × 8.31 × 100 = 2490 J (A1)
Q11Kinetic theory of gases · Standard · Paper 5 style · 7 marks

A computer simulation gives the mean-square speed ⟨c²⟩ of the atoms of a monatomic gas at different thermodynamic temperatures T.T/K: 250, 300, 350, 400, 450⟨c²⟩/10⁵ m² s⁻²: 1.56, 1.87, 2.18, 2.49, 2.81It is suggested that ⟨c²⟩ = 3kT/m, where m is the mass of one atom. k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Explain why a graph of ⟨c²⟩ against T should be a straight line through the origin, and state an expression for its gradient. [2]
  2. (b) Determine the gradient of the graph. [2]
  3. (c) Use your gradient to determine the mass of one gas atom, in kg and in u. [2]
  4. (d) Predict the value of c_rms at 600 K. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) ⟨c²⟩ = (3k/m)T, which has the form y = mx with no intercept; gradient = 3k/m.
    B1 ⟨c²⟩ ∝ T (y = mx); B1 gradient = 3k/m
  2. (b) 625 m² s⁻² K⁻¹
    M1 large triangle, e.g. (450, 2.81 × 10⁵) and (250, 1.56 × 10⁵); A1 gradient = 1.25 × 10⁵/200 = 625 m² s⁻² K⁻¹ (allow 620–630)
  3. (c) 6.62 × 10⁻²⁶ kg = 40 u (argon)
    C1 m = 3k/gradient = 3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³/625; A1 6.62 × 10⁻²⁶ kg, which is 40 u
  4. (d) 612 m s⁻¹
    B1 ⟨c²⟩ = 625 × 600 = 3.75 × 10⁵ m² s⁻², c_rms = 612 m s⁻¹

Q13Kinetic theory of gases · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) A molecule of mass m moves with velocity component cₓ perpendicular to one face of a cube of side L. It collides elastically with this face. State the change in momentum of the molecule. [1]
  2. (b) Show that the average force exerted on this face by the molecule is mcₓ²/L. [2]
  3. (c) The cube contains N identical molecules moving at random. Use your answer to (b) to derive the equation pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩. [3]
  4. (d) State one point in the derivation where an assumption of the kinetic theory is used, and name the assumption. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2mcₓ (directed away from the wall)
    B1 2mcₓ
  2. (b) Time between collisions with the same face = 2L/cₓ; F = Δp/Δt = 2mcₓ/(2L/cₓ) = mcₓ²/L.
    M1 time between collisions 2L/cₓ; A1 force = rate of change of momentum = mcₓ²/L
  3. (c) Total force = Nm⟨cₓ²⟩/L, so p = F/L² = Nm⟨cₓ²⟩/L³ = Nm⟨cₓ²⟩/V. For random motion ⟨c²⟩ = ⟨cₓ²⟩ + ⟨c_y²⟩ + ⟨c_z²⟩ = 3⟨cₓ²⟩, so pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩.
    M1 total force = Nm⟨cₓ²⟩/L and p = F/L²; M1 V = L³; A1 ⟨cₓ²⟩ = ⅓⟨c²⟩ because motion is random (no preferred direction), giving pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩
  4. (d) e.g. the momentum change 2mcₓ uses perfectly elastic collisions; or ignoring collisions between molecules on the way across uses negligible molecular volume / no intermolecular forces.
    B1 any correctly linked assumption
Q14Kinetic theory of gases · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

Take the Boltzmann constant k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

  1. (a) Hydrogen molecules have a mass of 2.0 u and oxygen molecules a mass of 32 u. Show that, at the same temperature, the r.m.s. speed of hydrogen molecules is four times that of oxygen molecules. [2]
  2. (b) In the upper atmosphere the temperature can reach 1000 K. Calculate the r.m.s. speed of hydrogen molecules at this temperature. [2]
  3. (c) The escape speed from the Earth is 11.2 km s⁻¹. Your answer to (b) is only about one third of this. Suggest why hydrogen has nevertheless escaped from the Earth's atmosphere over its history, but oxygen has not. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT is the same for both, so c_rms ∝ 1/√m; ratio = √(32/2.0) = 4.
    M1 same mean KE at same T so c_rms ∝ m^(−1/2); A1 √16 = 4
  2. (b) 3530 m s⁻¹
    c_rms = √(3kT/m) (C1) = √(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 1000/(2.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷)) = 3530 m s⁻¹ (A1)
  3. (c) The r.m.s. is an average: some molecules move much faster than c_rms, and over millions of years enough reach escape speed and leave; oxygen molecules are four times slower, so the fraction reaching 11.2 km s⁻¹ is negligible.
    B1 molecules have a range (distribution) of speeds, some ≫ c_rms; B1 oxygen's c_rms is 4 times smaller so (almost) none reach escape speed
Q15Kinetic theory of gases · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

An oxygen molecule of mass 5.3 × 10⁻²⁶ kg moves backwards and forwards at 450 m s⁻¹ between two opposite walls of a cubical box of side 0.20 m, hitting each wall at right angles. Collisions are elastic and the molecule hits nothing else.

  1. (a) Calculate the change in momentum of the molecule in one collision with the wall. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the time between successive collisions of the molecule with this wall. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the average force exerted on the wall by this molecule, and the resulting pressure on the wall. [2]
  4. (d) The box actually contains 2.0 × 10²³ such molecules at the same r.m.s. speed of 450 m s⁻¹, moving randomly. Determine the pressure in the box. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4.77 × 10⁻²³ N s
    Δp = 2mc = 2 × 5.3 × 10⁻²⁶ × 450 = 4.77 × 10⁻²³ N s (B1)
  2. (b) 8.89 × 10⁻⁴ s
    t = 2L/c = 0.40/450 = 8.89 × 10⁻⁴ s (B1)
  3. (c) F = 5.37 × 10⁻²⁰ N; p = 1.34 × 10⁻¹⁸ Pa
    F = Δp/t = 5.37 × 10⁻²⁰ N (B1); p = F/L² = F/0.040 = 1.34 × 10⁻¹⁸ Pa (B1)
  4. (d) 89400 Pa
    M1 only one-third of the motion is perpendicular to each wall on average: p = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩/V; A1 ⅓ × 2.0 × 10²³ × 5.3 × 10⁻²⁶ × 450²/0.0080 = 89400 Pa

  1. (a) Calculate the r.m.s. speed of (i) the helium atoms (4.0 u), (ii) the argon atoms (40 u). [3]

  2. (b) They are equal, because the gases are in thermal equilibrium at the same temperature and ⟨Eₖ⟩ = (3/2)kT depends only on T.

  3. (c) 2900 K

Q17Kinetic theory of gases · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Nitrogen gas is stored in a cylinder. k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of molecules per cubic metre of the gas at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 273 K. [2]
  2. (b) Treating each molecule as a sphere of diameter 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, estimate the fraction of the volume of the gas occupied by the molecules themselves. [2]
  3. (c) The gas is compressed to 300 times this pressure at the same temperature. Assuming it still behaves ideally, estimate the new fraction, and explain why the gas would no longer behave as an ideal gas. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.68 × 10²⁵ m⁻³
    N/V = p/kT (C1) = 1.01 × 10⁵/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 273) = 2.68 × 10²⁵ m⁻³ (A1)
  2. (b) 7.1 × 10⁻⁴
    volume of one molecule = 4/3 π (1.85 × 10⁻¹⁰)³ = 2.65 × 10⁻²⁹ m³ (C1); fraction = 2.68 × 10²⁵ × 2.65 × 10⁻²⁹ = 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ (A1)

    B1 0.21 (300 × answer to (b)); B1 assumption of negligible molecular volume fails; B1 intermolecular forces no longer negligible
Q18Kinetic theory of gases · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Use the kinetic theory equations to show that equal volumes of any two ideal gases at the same pressure and temperature contain the same number of molecules. [3]
  2. (b) Two flasks, each of volume 0.0250 m³, contain hydrogen and carbon dioxide respectively at 1.2 × 10⁵ Pa and 310 K. Calculate the number of molecules in each flask. (k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹) [2]
  3. (c) State which flask contains the greater mass of gas and explain why the pressures are equal although the molecules have very different masses. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ = ⅔N × ½m⟨c²⟩ = ⅔N(3/2 kT) = NkT; so N = pV/kT, which depends only on p, V and T, not on the type of molecule.
    M1 write pV = ⅔N⟨Eₖ⟩; M1 ⟨Eₖ⟩ = (3/2)kT is the same for any gas at the same T; A1 N = pV/kT, same for both
  2. (b) 7.01 × 10²³ in each
    N = pV/kT (C1) = 1.2 × 10⁵ × 0.0250/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 310) = 7.01 × 10²³ (A1)
  3. (c) Carbon dioxide (heavier molecules). Its molecules move more slowly (lower c_rms), so each has the same mean KE; the product m⟨c²⟩ is the same, so the pressure is the same.
    B1 carbon dioxide flask; B1 lighter molecules move faster, m⟨c²⟩ (mean KE) equal so p equal

Show answer

  3. (c) Use your gradient to determine the value of absolute zero in °C. [2]
