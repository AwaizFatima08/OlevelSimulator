Q1SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State which mechanism, SN1 or SN2, is mainly followed in the hydrolysis of (i) 1-bromobutane (ii) 2-bromo-2-methylpropane. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why tertiary halogenoalkanes react mainly by the SN1 mechanism. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) SN2 (ii) SN1
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Tertiary carbocation is the most stable because three alkyl groups donate electrons (positive inductive effect) and stabilise the positive charge; the three bulky alkyl groups hinder (steric hindrance) attack by a nucleophile from the side opposite the halogen, so SN2 is difficult.
    B1 tertiary carbocation stable; B1 alkyl groups electron-donating/inductive effect; B1 steric hindrance prevents SN2 attack.
Q2SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Mean bond enthalpies: C–F 484 kJ mol⁻¹, C–Cl 346 kJ mol⁻¹, C–Br 290 kJ mol⁻¹, C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹. Describe how the C–X bond enthalpy changes from C–F to C–I, and state how this change affects the rate of hydrolysis of CH₃F, CH₃Cl, CH₃Br and CH₃I by aqueous NaOH. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why CH₃F does not undergo hydrolysis by aqueous NaOH under normal laboratory conditions. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bond enthalpy decreases from C–F to C–I. The weaker the C–X bond, the more easily it is broken, so the rate of hydrolysis increases in the order CH₃F (slowest) < CH₃Cl < CH₃Br < CH₃I (fastest).
    B1 bond enthalpy decreases C–F → C–Cl → C–Br → C–I (C–F strongest, C–I weakest); B1 weaker C–X bond is broken more easily, so rate increases C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I.
  2. (b) The C–F bond is very strong, so it is very hard to break.
    B1 strong C–F bond.
Q3SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCl, is hydrolysed by aqueous sodium hydroxide. Rate experiments at 25 °C gave these results: [(CH₃)₃CCl] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³ gives a rate of 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Doubling [(CH₃)₃CCl] doubles the rate. Doubling [OH⁻] has no effect on the rate. Deduce the order with respect to each reactant and write the rate equation. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate constant k, with its units, using the first experiment. [2]
  3. (c) State which mechanism these results support, and identify the slow step. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) First order with respect to (CH₃)₃CCl; zero order with respect to OH⁻; rate = k[(CH₃)₃CCl]
    B1 first order in (CH₃)₃CCl and zero order in OH⁻; B1 rate = k[(CH₃)₃CCl].
  2. (b) k = 2.4 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹
    M1 k = rate / [(CH₃)₃CCl] = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.10; A1 2.4 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ (units s⁻¹ because the rate equation has only one concentration term).
  3. (c) SN1; the slow step is the heterolysis of the C–Cl bond to form (CH₃)₃C⁺ and Cl⁻.
    B1 SN1; B1 slow step is C–Cl breaking (carbocation formed), not involving OH⁻.
Q4SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain why bromoethane reacts faster than chloroethane when each is heated with aqueous NaOH. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The C–Br bond is weaker (lower bond enthalpy) than the C–Cl bond, so it breaks more easily; Br⁻ is a better leaving group than Cl⁻ because it is a weaker base.
    B1 C–Br weaker / lower bond enthalpy than C–Cl; B1 C–Br is broken more easily, lower activation energy; B1 Br⁻ is a better leaving group (weaker base).

1-bromobutane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

  1. (a) Describe the SN2 mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromobutane with hydroxide ions. Describe in words where each curly arrow starts and ends, and describe the transition state. [4]
  2. (b) State the rate equation for this reaction. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ carbon bonded to Br; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br; transition state [HO···C···Br]⁻ with partial bonds and a negative charge, OH and Br on opposite sides of carbon; products butan-1-ol and Br⁻.
    B1 C–Br dipole (Cδ+, Brδ−); B1 arrow from OH⁻ lone pair to C; B1 arrow from C–Br bond to Br; B1 transition state with partial bonds and overall negative charge.
  2. (b) rate = k[CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br][OH⁻]
    B1.
Q6SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

An isomer of C₄H₉Br reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions. Initial rates at 25 °C:Expt 1: [C₄H₉Br] = 0.050, [OH⁻] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³, rate = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹Expt 2: [C₄H₉Br] = 0.100, [OH⁻] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³, rate = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹Expt 3: [C₄H₉Br] = 0.050, [OH⁻] = 0.20 mol dm⁻³, rate = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

  1. (a) Deduce the orders with respect to C₄H₉Br and to OH⁻, and write the rate equation. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate constant, with units. [1]
  3. (c) Deduce the mechanism and hence the structural formula of this isomer of C₄H₉Br. Explain. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) First order in C₄H₉Br, zero order in OH⁻; rate = k[C₄H₉Br]
    B1 orders from data; B1 rate equation.
  2. (b) 0.0072 s⁻¹
    B1 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.050.
  3. (c) SN1, so the isomer is tertiary: (CH₃)₃CBr, 2-bromo-2-methylpropane
    M1 rate independent of [OH⁻] → SN1; A1 tertiary isomer (CH₃)₃CBr.

Q8SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Compare the hydrolysis of primary (1-bromopropane), secondary (2-bromopropane) and tertiary (2-bromo-2-methylpropane) halogenoalkanes by the SN1 mechanism. State the order of SN1 rate, from fastest to slowest. [2]
  2. (b) The SN2 mechanism shows the opposite trend for these three compounds. Explain why, in terms of the attacking nucleophile. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) tertiary > secondary > primary
    B1 tertiary fastest, primary slowest for SN1; B1 explanation via carbocation stability (more alkyl groups stabilise the positive charge by electron donation).
  2. (b) In SN2 the OH⁻ must approach the carbon from the side opposite the halogen; alkyl groups crowd this site, so primary halogenoalkanes react fastest and tertiary ones react least (steric hindrance).
    B1 OH⁻ approaches from the opposite side to C–X (backside attack); B1 more alkyl groups cause steric hindrance, so primary is fastest.

    SN1 has two steps; the first, slow step is heterolytic fission of the C–Br bond to a carbocation. Option A describes SN2 (single step), C describes elimination, D is not a step.
Q10SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Optically active 2-bromobutane is hydrolysed by aqueous NaOH. Explain why the product obtained by an SN1 pathway is optically inactive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The planar carbocation intermediate can be attacked by OH⁻ from either face, giving equal amounts of the two mirror-image alcohols, a racemic mixture.
    B1 planar carbocation (sp² carbon); B1 attack from both faces equally gives 50:50 mixture of enantiomers (racemic, optically inactive).
Q11SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃, can undergo nucleophilic substitution with hydroxide ions.

  1. (a) Explain why 2-bromobutane is chiral. [1]
  2. (b) A single enantiomer of 2-bromobutane reacts with OH⁻ by the SN2 mechanism. State and explain whether the butan-2-ol formed rotates plane-polarised light. [2]
  3. (c) If the same reaction occurred by SN1, the product would not rotate plane-polarised light. Explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Carbon 2 is bonded to four different groups (H, Br, CH₃, C₂H₅).
    B1.
  2. (b) Yes – OH⁻ attacks from the side opposite Br, so the configuration is inverted and a single enantiomer of butan-2-ol forms, which is optically active.
    B1 attack opposite to leaving group/inversion; B1 single enantiomer so optically active.
  3. (c) The carbocation intermediate is planar, so the nucleophile can attack equally from either side; a racemic mixture (50 : 50 enantiomers) forms and the rotations cancel.
    B1 planar carbocation, attack from both faces; B1 racemic mixture, no net rotation.
Q12SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Three test-tubes each contain 1 cm³ of ethanol, 1 cm³ of aqueous silver nitrate and three drops of a halogenoalkane, warmed in a water bath at 50 °C: 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane.Bond energies / kJ mol⁻¹: C–Cl 340; C–Br 280; C–I 240

  1. (a) Predict the order in which precipitates appear, fastest first, and state the colour of each precipitate. [2]
  2. (b) Use the bond energies to explain the order of reactivity. [2]
  3. (c) A student predicts that 1-chlorobutane should react fastest because the C–Cl bond is the most polar. Explain why this prediction is wrong. [1]
  4. (d) Explain the purpose of the ethanol. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1-iodobutane (yellow AgI) first, then 1-bromobutane (cream AgBr), then 1-chlorobutane (white AgCl) last.
    B1 order I > Br > Cl; B1 all colours.
  2. (b) C–I is the weakest bond (240 kJ mol⁻¹), so it breaks most easily; C–Cl is the strongest (340 kJ mol⁻¹), so the chloroalkane is hydrolysed most slowly.
    B1 bond strength decreases C–Cl > C–Br > C–I; B1 weaker bond broken more easily so faster rate.
  3. (c) Bond strength, not bond polarity, is the dominant factor controlling the rate of hydrolysis.
    B1.
  4. (d) It is a solvent in which both the halogenoalkane and aqueous silver nitrate dissolve (they mix).
    B1.
Q13SN1/SN2 & reactivity · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Neopentyl bromide, 1-bromo-2,2-dimethylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCH₂Br, is a primary halogenoalkane but reacts extremely slowly by both SN1 and SN2 mechanisms. Explain why it reacts so slowly by SN2 and why it also reacts slowly by SN1. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) SN2: the bulky (CH₃)₃C– group next to CH₂Br blocks the backside approach of OH⁻ (severe steric hindrance). SN1: a primary carbocation would be very unstable, so the first step is very difficult.
    B1 bulky tert-butyl group adjacent to C–Br; B1 blocks backside attack by nucleophile (steric hindrance) so SN2 slow; B1 SN1 needs primary carbocation which is very unstable; B1 so the slow ionisation step is also very slow.

  1. (a) A reaction profile for the SN1 hydrolysis of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is described as follows.
