Q1Accounting principles · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The owner of a pharmacy paid her personal mobile phone bill from the business bank account. The amount was recorded as drawings, not as an expense of the business. Which principle has been applied? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A business entity
    Business entity treats the business as separate from its owner, so the owner's private expense is drawings. Going concern (B) is about the business continuing in the future and is not relevant here.
Q2Accounting principles · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Which principle does this apply? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B prudence
    Prudence means profits and assets are not overstated: if goods are expected to sell for less than cost, the loss is recognised now. Matching (A) concerns charging expenses to the period in which the related revenue is earned.

Q4Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain the money measurement principle. [1]
  2. (b) Give one example of something that is valuable to a business but is not recorded in its books because of this principle. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Only transactions and items that can be expressed in money terms are recorded in the accounting records.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) e.g. the skill or loyalty of the staff; good relations with customers; the owner's experience; a good location.
    1 mark.
Q5Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the duality principle. [1]
  2. (b) Mustafa bought a computer for his business and paid $1,350 by cheque. State the two accounts affected and whether each is debited or credited. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Every transaction has two aspects, which are recorded as a debit entry in one account and an equal credit entry in another account.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Debit computer (office equipment) account $1,350; credit bank account $1,350.
    1 mark each.
Q6Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain the consistency principle. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the consistency principle is important to users of financial statements. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Once a business has chosen an accounting method (e.g. a depreciation method), it should use the same method from one year to the next.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) It makes the results of different years comparable, so changes in profit reflect performance rather than a change of method.
    1 mark.
Q7Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the realisation principle. [1]
  2. (b) Goods were delivered to a customer on 29 December and the customer paid on 12 January. The business's year ends on 31 December. State in which year the sale should be recorded and give a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Revenue is recognised (and profit is earned) when the goods or services are passed to the customer and the customer accepts liability to pay, not when cash is received or an order is made.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) In the year ending 31 December (the year of delivery), because the goods were delivered and the customer became liable to pay before the year end.
    1 mark year, 1 mark reason.
Q8Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the materiality principle. [1]
  2. (b) A large supermarket chain bought a waste-paper bin for $9. Explain how it should be treated in the accounts and why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Items of small value need not be treated strictly according to the rules if this would not affect users' understanding; for example, a cheap item that will last several years can be treated as an expense.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) Charge it as an expense in the income statement (not as a non-current asset), because the amount is too small to matter to users and depreciating it would waste time.
    1 mark treatment, 1 mark reason.
Q9Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the business entity principle. [1]
  2. (b) Sadia took $250 of goods from her shop for her family. Explain how the business entity principle affects the recording of this transaction. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The business is treated as a separate entity from its owner, so only business transactions are recorded in the business's books.
    1 mark.
  2. (b) The goods are no longer a business asset used to earn revenue; they are recorded as drawings (debit drawings, credit purchases), reducing Sadia's capital rather than being treated as a business expense.
    1 mark: drawings/not a business expense. 1 mark: correct entries or effect on capital.
Q10Accounting principles · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark

  1. (a) A prudence
    Prudence requires losses to be recognised as soon as they are expected, not when they finally occur. Realisation concerns when revenue is recognised; duality concerns the two-sided effect of each transaction; money measurement concerns recording only items that can be measured in money.
Q11Accounting principles · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

Name the accounting principle that is applied in each case.

  1. (a) Historic cost is used to value the business's premises at the price originally paid for them, $120,000, even though their market value has risen. [1]
  2. (b) The business uses the same depreciation method for its vehicles every year. [1]
  3. (c) An office stapler costing $4 is expensed in full in the year of purchase, although it will last several years. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Historical cost (cost concept)
    Assets are recorded at the price paid, not at market value.
  2. (b) Consistency
    Accounting methods are applied the same way from one period to the next.
  3. (c) Materiality
    Items too small to affect a user's decision need not be treated in strict detail.
Q12Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C matching
    Matching (accruals) requires expenses to be charged to the period to which they relate, whatever the date of payment, so one month is carried forward as a prepayment. Prudence (A) is about not overstating profit or assets.
Q13Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which principle requires non-current assets to be recorded in the books at the price paid for them? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A historic cost
    Under historic cost, assets are recorded at their original cost, which can be checked against invoices. Money measurement (D) says only items that can be expressed in money are recorded, but does not say which money value is used.
Q14Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Femi's financial year ends on 31 December. On 1 April he paid insurance of $4,500 covering the 15 months from 1 April to 30 June of the following year. Calculate the insurance expense for the year ended 31 December. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the amount that will be shown as a prepayment in the statement of financial position on 31 December. [1]
  3. (c) Name the accounting principle applied. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) $2,700
    4,500 ÷ 15 = 300 per month (M1). 9 months (April–December) × 300 = 2700 (A1).
  2. (b) $1,800
    4,500 − 2700 = 1800 (6 months × 300).
  3. (c) Matching (accruals)
    1 mark.

Q16Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Rashid Electronics has a financial year ending on 31 December. The following transactions took place in December:1 Goods invoiced at $3,400 were delivered to a customer on 28 December; payment was received on 10 January.2 A customer paid a deposit of $1,000 on 15 December for a television to be delivered on 5 January.3 An order for goods worth $2,600 was received on 30 December; delivery is planned for February.4 Cash sales for December were $5,800.

  1. (a) Calculate the revenue from these transactions that should be included for the year ended 31 December. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why the deposit in transaction 2 is not revenue for the year ended 31 December. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $9,200
    Items 1 and 4 only: 3,400 + 5,800 = 9200. 1 mark each for including 1 and 4, 1 for excluding 2 and 3.
  2. (b) The goods have not yet been delivered, so the sale has not been realised. The deposit is money owed back to the customer (a liability) until the television is delivered.
    1 mark: not realised/goods not delivered. 1 mark: treated as a liability/deferred income.
Q17Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Tariq bought a machine for $40,000. He could depreciate it by the straight-line method over 10 years with no residual value, or by the reducing balance method at 25% per annum. Calculate the depreciation charge for the first year under each method. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the effect on the profit for the first year of using the reducing balance method instead of the straight-line method. [1]
  3. (c) Tariq suggests changing his depreciation method each year to give the highest profit. Name the principle this would break and explain why he should not do this. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Straight-line $4,000; reducing balance $10,000
    40,000 ÷ 10 = 4,000 (1); 40,000 × 25% = 10,000 (1).
  2. (b) Profit is $6,000 lower.
    10000 − 4000 = 6000.
  3. (c) Consistency. Profits of different years could not be compared fairly, because changes in profit would be caused by the change of method, not by performance.
    1 mark: consistency. 1 mark: comparability/misleading explanation.
Q18Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Ming's shop has inventory at a cost of $12,000 and shop fittings with a carrying amount of $18,000. Ming has decided to close the shop next month. In a closing-down sale the inventory is expected to raise only $7,500 and the fittings $9,000.

  1. (a) Name the accounting principle that no longer applies to Ming's shop. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the total value at which these assets should now be shown, and the reduction compared with their book values. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the fittings were previously shown at their carrying amount rather than at their sale value. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Going concern
    1 mark.
  2. (b) $16,500; a reduction of $13,500
    7,500 + 9,000 = 16500 (1). 30000 − 16500 = 13500 (1).
  3. (c) While the business was expected to continue (going concern), the fittings were to be used in the business, not sold, so they were shown at cost less depreciation; sale values were not relevant.
    1 mark: business expected to continue/assets held for use. 1 mark: shown at cost less depreciation (historic cost), not sale value.
Q19Accounting principles · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A small taxi business with total assets of $30,000 and a large airline both buy a set of spanners for $400. Explain why the taxi business might treat the spanners as a non-current asset while the airline treats them as an expense. [3]
  2. (b) State one other factor, apart from the amount, that can make an item material. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Materiality depends on the size of the business. $400 is a significant amount for the small taxi business, so it may be capitalised and depreciated. For the airline $400 is immaterial, so it is simply charged as an expense because the treatment would not affect users' decisions.
    1 mark: materiality named/explained. 1 mark: significant for small business. 1 mark: insignificant for large business, so expensed.
  2. (b) Its nature, e.g. an item that is unusual, illegal or of special interest to users (such as payments to directors).
    1 mark.

Q22Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which statements about the going concern principle are correct?1 It assumes the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future.2 If a business is about to close, its assets should be valued at the amounts they are expected to raise when sold.3 It requires a business to make a profit every year. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 1 and 2 only
    Statements 1 and 2 are correct: a continuing business values non-current assets at cost less depreciation, but a business that is closing uses expected sale values. Statement 3 is wrong: a business can make a loss and still be a going concern.
Q23Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Bushra's draft profit for the year was $36,250. Her accountant found the following:1 Bushra's private holiday costing $2,800 had been paid from the business bank account and debited to general expenses.2 Electricity of $430 owing at the year end had not been recorded.3 Closing inventory included goods valued at their cost of $1,600; they can now be sold for only $950.4 An order of $3,000 for goods to be delivered next year had been recorded as a credit sale. The goods are included in closing inventory at cost.

  1. (a) Calculate the corrected profit for the year. [4]
  2. (b) Name the accounting principle that applies to each of items 1 to 4. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) $34,970
    36250 + 2,800 − 430 − 650 − 3,000 = 34970. 1 mark for each correct adjustment (max 3), 1 for the final figure.
  2. (b) 1 business entity 2 matching (accruals) 3 prudence 4 realisation
    1 mark each.
Q24Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

At the year end Gareth's trade receivables were $24,600. He decided to maintain a provision for doubtful debts of 3% of trade receivables. The provision brought forward from last year was $600.

  1. (a) Calculate the provision for doubtful debts at the year end. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the amount charged in the income statement for the year. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the net trade receivables shown in the statement of financial position. [1]
  4. (d) Name the principle applied when making this provision and explain why it is made even though no customer has yet failed to pay. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $738
    24,600 × 3% = 738.
  2. (b) $138
    Increase only: 738 − 600 = 138. (Charging the whole provision loses the A mark.)
  3. (c) $23,862
    24,600 − 738 = 23862.
  4. (d) Prudence. Some customers are likely not to pay, so the expected loss is recognised now and trade receivables are not overstated.
    1 mark: prudence. 1 mark: avoids overstating assets/profit by recognising likely losses.
Q25Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Zubair owns a factory building that cost $120,000 and is shown in his statement of financial position at that amount. He wants to show the building at its estimated market value of $300,000 because he is applying for a bank loan. Name the principle that requires the building to be shown at $120,000. [1]
  2. (b) Advise Zubair whether he should show the building at its market value. Justify your answer by giving two points for and two points against. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Historic cost
    1 mark.
  2. (b) For: the statement of financial position would show a more up-to-date value; the bank would see a larger asset available as security; his net assets and capital would look stronger. Against: market value is an estimate and is subjective, so less reliable; it breaks the historic cost (and prudence) principle; an unrealised gain would be included; the bank will probably arrange its own valuation anyway; comparison with earlier years is distorted. Recommendation, e.g. he should keep the building at cost and give the bank a separate professional valuation.
    Up to 2 marks for points for, up to 2 for points against, 1 for a justified recommendation (only if both sides are given).
Q26Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Kamal's draft profit for the year was $22,900. His capital on 1 January was $50,000 and drawings recorded were $9,000. It was then found that the business bank account had been used to pay Kamal's home electricity bill of $540 and his son's school fees of $1,800. Both amounts had been debited to business expense accounts.

  1. (a) Calculate the corrected profit for the year. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the corrected drawings. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate Kamal's closing capital before and after the corrections, and explain the result. [3]
  4. (d) Name the principle that has been broken. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) $25,240
    22900 + 540 + 1,800 = 25240.
  2. (b) $11,340
    9,000 + 540 + 1,800 = 11,340.
  3. (c) Both $63,900. Profit and drawings both increase by $2,340, so capital is unchanged.
    Before: 50,000 + 22900 − 9,000 = 63900 (1). After: 50,000 + 25240 − 11,340 = 63900 (1). Explanation (1).
  4. (d) Business entity
    1 mark.

Q28Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

For each treatment, name the accounting principle that has NOT been followed and state the correct treatment.

  1. (a) A business changes its depreciation method every year so that profit stays about the same. [2]
  2. (b) Unsold inventory is valued at its selling price. [2]
  3. (c) The owner's private car insurance is charged as a business expense. [2]

Q29Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the going concern principle, and why it allows non-current assets to be depreciated over several years rather than written off at once. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) The business is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future, so its assets are expected to be used over their useful lives. Their cost is therefore spread over several years by depreciation, rather than valued at the price a forced sale would raise.
    1 mark: the business is assumed to continue for the foreseeable future; 1 mark: assets are used over their useful lives; 1 mark: cost is spread over those years by depreciation.
Q30Accounting principles · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Nida pays $5,400 on 1 October 2025 for 18 months' rent on a shop, from 1 October 2025 to 31 March 2027. Her financial year ends on 31 December.

  1. (a) Calculate the rent expense for the year ended 31 December 2025. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the prepayment at 31 December 2025. [2]
  3. (c) Name the accounting principle that requires the rent to be spread over the periods it covers. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) $900
    Rent of 5,400 for 18 months = 300 a month. Three months (October to December 2025) = 3 × 300 = 900.
  2. (b) $4,500
    5,400 − 900 = 4,500, which covers the 15 months from 1 January 2026 to 31 March 2027.
  3. (c) The matching principle.
    Expenses are matched to the periods that benefit from them.
Q31Accounting policies · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) The owner of a business wants to compare this year's financial statements with last year's and with those of a similar business. Which quality of financial information is most important for this? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A comparability
    Comparability means information is prepared on the same basis over time and between businesses. Understandability (B) is about information being clear to users, not about comparisons. Materiality and realisation are principles, not qualities of information.
Q32Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by the relevance of financial information. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what is meant by the reliability of financial information. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Information is relevant if it can influence users' decisions, for example by helping them assess past performance or predict future results, and it is available in time to be useful.
    1 mark: affects/helps users' decisions. 1 mark: development (timely, helps predict or confirm).
  2. (b) Information is reliable if it is free from significant error and bias, can be checked (verified) and faithfully represents what it claims to show.
    1 mark: free from error/bias. 1 mark: verifiable/faithful representation.
Q33Accounting policies · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B comparability
    Consistent use of the same policies over time, and across businesses, makes financial information comparable. Relevance means the information can influence decisions; understandability means users can follow it; materiality decides whether an item is significant enough to matter and is not one of the four characteristics in this subtopic (relevance, reliability, comparability, understandability).
Q34Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

Name the qualitative characteristic of financial information shown in each statement.

  1. (a) The financial statements of this year and last year use the same accounting policies. [1]
  2. (b) The information is free from bias and can be depended on by users. [1]
  3. (c) The information is presented clearly so that users with a reasonable knowledge of business can understand it. [1]
  4. (d) The information helps users to make decisions about the business. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Comparability
    Consistent policies allow the figures to be compared over time.
  2. (b) Reliability
    Reliable information is free from material error and bias.
  3. (c) Understandability
    This describes clear and concise presentation for users.
  4. (d) Relevance
    Relevant information can influence decisions, for example by confirming or correcting past expectations.

Show answer
  1. (a) Any two: the method of depreciation for non-current assets; the method of valuing inventory; the basis of recognising revenue; the policy for doubtful debts.
    1 mark for each valid item.
Q36Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Name the body that issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). [1]
  2. (b) State the number of the international standard that deals with inventories. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB).
    The IASB is the standard-setting body for IFRS.
  2. (b) IAS 2 (Inventories).
    IAS 2 sets out how inventories are measured, including the lower of cost and net realisable value.

  1. (a) The specific principles, bases, conventions, rules and practices that a business applies in preparing and presenting its financial statements, for example how it values inventory or depreciates non-current assets.

Q38Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks

  1. (a) Any two: financial statements can be compared over time and between businesses; users can rely on the figures; there is less scope for manipulation of the figures; users save time in understanding the statements.
    1 mark for each valid benefit.
Q39Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by understandability of financial information. [2]

  1. (a) Information is presented clearly and concisely, so that users with a reasonable knowledge of business and accounting can understand it.
    1 mark: clear and concise presentation; 1 mark: users with reasonable knowledge can follow it.
Q40Accounting policies · Core · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) State the number of the international standard that deals with property, plant and equipment, and name one requirement of it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) IAS 16 (Property, Plant and Equipment). It requires items to be measured at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment, or at revalued amount, and depreciation of assets with a limited useful life.

Q41Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is the main purpose of international accounting standards? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B to reduce differences in accounting methods so that financial statements can be compared across countries
    International accounting standards set out how items should be recognised, measured and presented, so that financial statements prepared in different countries are consistent and comparable. They do not guarantee a profit (C) or set prices.
Q42Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why financial statements should be understandable. Give an example of how this can be achieved. [2]
  2. (b) Apart from understandability, name two other objectives a business should consider when selecting its accounting policies. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Users with reasonable knowledge of business must be able to make sense of the information; e.g. items are grouped under clear headings (non-current assets, current liabilities) and similar items are combined rather than listed in detail.
    1 mark: users must be able to understand it to use it. 1 mark: example (clear headings, standard layout, notes).
  2. (b) Any two of: comparability, relevance, reliability.
    1 mark each.
Q43Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which of the following is an accounting policy, rather than an accounting estimate? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C the business uses the reducing balance method to depreciate its vehicles
    Choosing a depreciation method is an accounting policy. Useful life, residual value and the expected percentage of doubtful debts are estimates, which can be revised as new information becomes available.
Q44Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a business should disclose its accounting policies in the notes to its financial statements. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) So that users can understand how the figures were measured, and compare them with other periods and businesses. Disclosure supports the reliability and understandability of the statements.
    1 mark for each valid reason, up to 2.
Q45Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why financial information must be provided on time (timeliness) if it is to be relevant. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Old information may no longer help users make decisions. Timely information can influence decisions before the opportunity to act has passed.
    1 mark: old information loses its usefulness; 1 mark: timely information can still influence decisions.
Q46Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why reliability requires financial statements to be free from material error and bias. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Users can depend on figures that represent what they claim to represent. Freedom from bias means figures are not deliberately manipulated to favour one group of users.
    1 mark: users can depend on the figures; 1 mark: no deliberate bias or manipulation.
Q47Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a multinational group might want all its subsidiaries in different countries to use the same accounting policies. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) So the group accounts are consistent and comparable, and head office can compare the results of each subsidiary. It also makes combining the results into group accounts more reliable.
    1 mark: comparability between subsidiaries; 1 mark: reliable consolidation of the group results.
Q48Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a change in the valuation policy for a company's properties must be disclosed in the notes to the accounts. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The change affects the values of the assets and the profit. Users need to know about the change to interpret the figures and to compare them with earlier years.
    1 mark: the change affects asset values and profit; 1 mark: users need it to interpret and compare the figures.
Q49Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain why the notes on accounting policies should state the basis used for valuing non-current assets (cost or revaluation). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Users need the basis to interpret the asset values and to compare them with other businesses. It shows how far the figures are based on historical cost and how far on market-based estimates.
    1 mark: users interpret asset values; 1 mark: shows the extent of estimates and the basis of measurement.
Q50Accounting policies · Standard · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a business using IFRS can show a bank that its inventory valuation is reliable. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) By stating the valuation method and the basis (lower of cost and net realisable value) in the notes, so that the bank can check how the figure was reached.
    1 mark: states the method and basis; 1 mark: the basis can be checked, supporting reliability.
Q51Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) An investor is comparing the financial statements of a company in Pakistan with those of a similar company in Malaysia. Explain how international accounting standards help the investor. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why there may be a conflict between making information relevant and making it reliable. Give an example. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both companies follow the same rules for recognising and measuring items (e.g. inventory, depreciation), so their profits and assets are prepared on a similar basis and can be compared fairly when deciding where to invest.
    1 mark: common rules/methods. 1 mark: makes the comparison meaningful for a decision.
  2. (b) Relevant information must be up to date and available quickly, but checking it fully to make it reliable takes time. For example, a current market value of a building is more relevant than its cost but is an estimate, so it is less reliable; historic cost is reliable but may be out of date.
    1 mark: relevance needs timeliness/current values. 1 mark: reliability needs checking/verifiable figures, which may be older. 1 mark: suitable example.
Q52Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks

A business changes its valuation of inventory from FIFO to AVCO.

  1. (a) Explain the effect of this change on comparability with previous years, and state one action the business should take to help users. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Comparability is reduced, because the figures are no longer measured on the same basis as in previous years. The business should disclose the change in policy, and the effect of the change on the figures, in the notes.
    1 mark: comparability reduced; 1 mark: figures measured on a different basis; 1 mark: disclose the change and its effect in the notes.
Q53Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A business bought a machine on 1 January 2024 for $60,000 with nil residual value. In 2024 it depreciated the machine at 25% a year on the reducing balance method (charge $15,000). From 1 January 2025 it changed to straight-line depreciation over a further 3 years, with nil residual value.

  1. (a) Calculate the depreciation charge for 2025 under the new policy. [2]
  2. (b) State the effect on 2025 profit compared with keeping the reducing balance method. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the change must be disclosed in the notes. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $15,000
    Net book value at 1 January 2025 = 60,000 − 15,000 = 45,000. Straight-line over the remaining 3 years: 45,000 ÷ 3 = 15,000.
  2. (b) Profit is $3,750 lower under the straight-line method, because the charge is 15,000 instead of 11,250 (25% × 45,000) under reducing balance.
    15,000 − 11,250 = 3,750 difference in the charge, so profit is lower by 3,750 under the new method.
  3. (c) The change alters the reported profit and asset values, so users need to know it to interpret the figures and compare them with other years.
    1 mark: the change affects profit and asset values; 1 mark: users need the information to compare the years.
Q54Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain the difference between a change in accounting policy and a change in an accounting estimate. Give one example of each. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) A change in accounting policy changes the method or basis used, for example FIFO to AVCO. A change in estimate revises a judgement as new information becomes available, for example changing the useful life of a machine from 5 years to 4 years.
    1 mark: change of policy defined; 1 mark: change of estimate defined; 1 mark: correct examples of each.
Q55Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A piece of equipment cost $90,000 with an estimated residual value of $6,000 and an original useful life of 6 years (straight-line). At the start of year 3 the business revises the total useful life to 8 years. The residual value is unchanged.

  1. (a) Calculate the depreciation charge for year 3 under the revised estimate, to the nearest dollar. [3]
  2. (b) State whether this is a change in policy or a change in estimate, and explain why. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $9,333
    Depreciation to the end of year 2 = 2 × (90,000 − 6,000) ÷ 6 = 28,000. Carrying amount at 1 January year 3 = 90,000 − 28,000 = 62,000. Remaining depreciable amount = 62,000 − 6,000 = 56,000 over the remaining 6 years (8 − 2): 56,000 ÷ 6 = 9,333.33, to the nearest dollar 9,333.
  2. (b) A change in estimate. The method (straight-line) is unchanged; only the expected useful life has been revised as new information became available.
    1 mark for the classification; 1 mark for the reason.
Q56Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain the conflict between relevance and reliability when a business values a building at market value (estimated at $260,000) rather than historical cost ($180,000). [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Market value is more up to date and more useful for decisions, so it is more relevant. Historical cost is objective and verifiable, so it is more reliable. Market value is an estimate that may be biased, so the business must balance the two qualities, and disclose the basis used.
    1 mark: market value is relevant; 1 mark: historical cost is more reliable (verifiable); 1 mark: market value is an estimate that may be biased; 1 mark: conclusion that the trade-off must be disclosed.
Q57Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Explain how consistent accounting policies improve comparability over time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The same policies allow profit and financial position to be compared from year to year, because differences in the figures reflect the business rather than changes in method. A business shows consistency by applying the same policy each year and disclosing any change.
    1 mark: same policy allows year-to-year comparison; 1 mark: changes must be disclosed.
Q58Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how prudence and neutrality can conflict, using inventory valuation as an example. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Prudence requires that assets are not overstated and that expected losses are recognised early, for example by valuing inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Neutrality requires information free from bias in either direction. If inventory is written down too cautiously, the figures become biased downwards, so the two principles must be balanced.
    1 mark: prudence explained; 1 mark: neutrality explained; 1 mark: inventory example; 1 mark: balance and conclusion.
Q59Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 2 marks
  1. (a) Describe how differences between national accounting rules can make the profits of two companies difficult to compare. Give one example. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Different countries may recognise or measure the same item differently, so the same transaction produces different profits. For example, one country may allow property to be revalued upwards while another requires historical cost.
    1 mark: differences in rules change the measured figures; 1 mark: a valid example.
Q60Accounting policies · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Explain how prudence affects the treatment of a fall in the market value of investments held at market value, and how matching is relevant to the timing of the loss. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Prudence means the fall in value is recognised as a loss in profit at once, even though it has not been realised by sale. Matching means the loss is recorded in the period in which the fall in value occurred, not when the investment is later sold.
    1 mark: prudence recognises the fall immediately; 1 mark: the loss is recognised in profit now; 1 mark: matching records it in the period of the fall.
