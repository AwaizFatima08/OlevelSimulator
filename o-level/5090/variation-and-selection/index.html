Practice questions

Original practice questions written by HomiLabs in the exam style. They are not Cambridge past paper questions. Try each one before opening the answer.

Q1Natural selection & resistance · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) What is the first step in the development of an antibiotic-resistant population of bacteria?A A random mutation produces a bacterium with an allele for resistance.B The bacteria choose to become resistant when the antibiotic is used.C The antibiotic causes all the bacteria to adapt to it.D The resistant bacteria are killed by the antibiotic. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A A random mutation produces a bacterium with an allele for resistance.
    Resistance begins with a chance mutation that is present before the antibiotic is used; the antibiotic then selects for it. C is wrong: individual bacteria do not adapt in response to the drug.
Q2Natural selection & resistance · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the process of natural selection. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) There is variation within a population; organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support; there is competition (a struggle for survival) for resources; individuals with features better adapted to the environment are more likely to survive; they reproduce and pass on their alleles to offspring; over many generations these alleles become more common.
    1 mark each for any five: variation; overproduction; competition/struggle for survival; better adapted survive; reproduce; pass on alleles; over many generations allele frequency increases
Q3Natural selection & resistance · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by an antibiotic-resistant bacterium. [1]
  2. (b) Name one type of bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why antibiotic resistance is a concern for hospitals. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A bacterium that is not killed (or whose growth is not stopped) by an antibiotic.
    B1
  2. (b) MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus)
    B1
  3. (c) Infections become difficult or impossible to treat; so patients may become seriously ill or die, and stay longer in hospital.
    B1 harder to treat; B1 longer illness/death/spread to other patients
Q4Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a population of bacteria resistant to an antibiotic can develop in a hospital. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) A random mutation gives some bacteria an allele for resistance; when the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed; resistant bacteria survive (selection pressure); they reproduce rapidly by binary fission/asexually; passing on the resistance allele; the proportion of resistant bacteria increases over generations.
    1 mark each for any five: mutation; variation in population; antibiotic kills non-resistant; resistant survive; reproduce (rapidly); allele passed on; frequency increases. Do not credit 'bacteria adapt/become immune'.
Q5Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In one district of Sindh the same insecticide was sprayed to control malaria mosquitoes every year. Samples of mosquitoes were tested with a standard dose.Year: 2015, 2017, 2019, 2021Mosquitoes surviving / %: 4, 15, 41, 70

  1. (a) Calculate the increase in the percentage of mosquitoes surviving between 2015 and 2021. [1]
  2. (b) Explain the change in the percentage of mosquitoes surviving the spraying. [4]
  3. (c) Suggest one way to slow the spread of resistance. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 66 percentage points (from 4% to 70%)
    B1 70 − 4 = 66
  2. (b) Some mosquitoes had a mutation giving resistance; spraying killed the non-resistant mosquitoes; resistant mosquitoes survived and reproduced; they passed the resistance allele to their offspring so the proportion of resistant mosquitoes increased each generation.
    1 mark each for any four: variation/mutation; non-resistant killed; resistant survive and breed; allele passed on; frequency increases over generations
  3. (c) Use different insecticides in rotation / spray less often / use other methods such as nets or removing standing water.
    B1
Q6Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Why are patients told to complete the whole course of antibiotics even if they feel better?A so that all the bacteria, including the less sensitive ones, are killed and cannot multiplyB so that the bacteria have time to become resistantC so that the antibiotic can kill the viruses as wellD so that the patient's white blood cells stop making antibodies [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A so that all the bacteria, including the less sensitive ones, are killed and cannot multiply
    Stopping early leaves the bacteria that are hardest to kill, which can multiply and pass on resistance. C is wrong because antibiotics do not kill viruses.
Q7Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

On a small island, the mean beak depth of a population of seed-eating finches was 9.2 mm. After a long drought killed most plants with small, soft seeds, the mean beak depth of the next generation was 9.7 mm.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in mean beak depth. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest how the drought caused this change in the population. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.4%
    M1 (9.7 − 9.2) ÷ 9.2 × 100; A1 5.4%
  2. (b) During the drought, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large hard seeds remained; birds with deeper beaks could crack them, so they were more likely to survive; they bred and passed on alleles for deep beaks to their offspring.
    B1 food changed / large hard seeds left; B1 deeper-beaked birds survived; B1 reproduced and passed on alleles
Q8Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A hospital recorded the percentage of samples (isolates) of the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus that were resistant to one antibiotic.Year: 1990, 2000, 2010, 2020Resistant isolates / %: 5, 18, 39, 52

  1. (a) Calculate the mean increase in the percentage of resistant isolates per year between 1990 and 2020. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) State the decade in which resistance increased most quickly. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why using antibiotics to treat viral infections such as colds increases the problem of resistance. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest two other ways of reducing the development of resistant bacteria. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1.57 percentage points per year
    M1 (52 − 5) ÷ 30; A1 1.57
  2. (b) 2000–2010 (21 percentage points)
    B1
  3. (c) Antibiotics do not affect viruses; but they kill non-resistant bacteria in the body, selecting for resistant bacteria which survive and multiply.
    B1 antibiotics have no effect on viruses; B1 unnecessary use increases selection of resistant bacteria
  4. (d) Any two of: complete the full course of antibiotics; only use antibiotics when necessary/prescribed; limit antibiotic use in farm animals; use different antibiotics/narrow-spectrum; good hygiene in hospitals to stop spread.
    1 mark each
Q9Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Students counted the snails of one species with brown shells and with yellow shells in two habitats.Woodland with dark leaf litter: brown 180, yellow 60Grassland with pale dry grass: brown 45, yellow 105

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of brown snails in each habitat. [2]
  2. (b) Thrushes feed on these snails and find them by sight. Use natural selection to explain the difference between the two habitats. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Woodland: 75%; grassland: 30%
    M1 180 ÷ 240 × 100 and 45 ÷ 150 × 100; A1 both
  2. (b) Shell colour varies and is inherited; in woodland brown shells are camouflaged against the dark leaf litter, so yellow snails are seen and eaten more; in grassland yellow snails are camouflaged and brown ones eaten more; survivors reproduce and pass on the alleles for their shell colour, so each habitat has more of the camouflaged colour.
    1 mark each for any four: variation/inherited colour; predation as selection pressure; camouflage on background (one habitat explained); survivors reproduce; alleles passed on/allele frequency changes
Q10Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Compare natural selection with artificial (selective) breeding. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the statement 'the bacteria became resistant because they needed to survive' is incorrect. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Similarities: both act on variation in a population; both cause alleles to become more common over many generations. Differences: in natural selection the environment determines which individuals survive and reproduce, in selective breeding humans choose; natural selection produces features that help survival (adaptation), selective breeding produces features useful to humans; selective breeding is usually faster.
    1 mark each for any four valid comparative points; at least one similarity and one difference for full marks
  2. (b) Resistance arises by random mutation, not in response to need; the antibiotic only selects bacteria that already had the resistance allele.
    B1 mutation is random/not caused by need; B1 antibiotic selects existing resistant bacteria
Q11Adaptive features · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which feature helps a desert plant in the Thar Desert to reduce water loss?A a thick waxy cuticleB large, thin leavesC many stomata on the upper leaf surfaceD large air spaces in the stem for floating [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a thick waxy cuticle
    A thick waxy cuticle reduces evaporation from the leaf surface. Large, thin leaves (B) would increase water loss.
Q12Adaptive features · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Define the term adaptive feature. [2]
  2. (b) For each feature of a camel living in the Cholistan Desert, suggest how it helps the camel survive: (i) wide, padded feet; (ii) long eyelashes and nostrils that can close. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) An inherited feature; that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment.
    B1 inherited; B1 helps survival and reproduction
  2. (b) (i) spread the weight so the camel does not sink into soft sand; (ii) keep sand out of the eyes and nose during sandstorms.
    1 mark each
Q13Adaptive features · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain how each feature helps the lotus survive in water: (i) large air spaces in the leaf stalks and leaves; (ii) stomata on the upper surface of the floating leaves; (iii) very little xylem tissue. [3]
  2. (b) State the name given to plants adapted to live in water. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) provide buoyancy so leaves float in light (and store/move oxygen to submerged parts); (ii) upper surface is in contact with air so gas exchange can take place; (iii) water supports the plant and there is plenty of water, so little transport or support tissue is needed.
    1 mark each
  2. (b) Hydrophytes
    B1
Q14Adaptive features · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how each of these features of xerophytes reduces water loss: (i) leaves reduced to spines; (ii) stomata sunken in pits; (iii) leaves that roll up with hairs on the inside. [3]
  2. (b) Describe two features of the roots of xerophytes and explain how they help. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) much smaller surface area for transpiration; (ii) and (iii) trap moist air near the stomata, reducing the water vapour concentration gradient so less water diffuses out.
    B1 smaller surface area; B1 humid air trapped (sunken stomata); B1 humid air trapped/reduced gradient (rolled leaves/hairs)
  2. (b) Very deep roots reach water deep underground; or wide shallow spreading roots absorb rain quickly before it evaporates.
    1 mark each for feature + explanation
Q15Adaptive features · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Students counted stomata per mm² on three plants, P, Q and R, from different habitats.P: upper surface 380, lower surface 0Q: upper surface 0, lower surface 450R: upper surface 10, lower surface 35

  1. (a) Suggest which plant is a hydrophyte with floating leaves and which is a xerophyte. Give a reason for each. [4]
  2. (b) Calculate how many times more stomata per mm² plant Q has in total than plant R. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why plant Q has no stomata on its upper surface. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) P is the hydrophyte – all its stomata are on the upper surface, which is in contact with the air (the lower surface touches water). R is the xerophyte – it has very few stomata, reducing water loss by transpiration.
    B1 P; B1 reason; B1 R; B1 reason
  2. (b) 10.0 times
    M1 450 ÷ 45; A1 10.0
  3. (c) The upper surface is in direct sunlight and is hotter, so having no stomata there reduces water loss.
    B1
Q16Adaptive features · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

The fennec fox lives in hot deserts of North Africa and has very large ears. The Arctic fox lives in very cold regions and has small, rounded ears.

  1. (a) Explain how ear size is an adaptive feature in each fox. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest one other adaptive feature of the Arctic fox and explain how it helps. [1]
  3. (c) Define the term fitness in the context of natural selection. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Large ears of the fennec fox give a large surface area; so more heat is lost to the surroundings, helping it keep cool in the hot desert; small ears of the Arctic fox reduce surface area; so less heat is lost in the cold.
    B1 large ears large surface area; B1 more heat loss/cooling in desert; B1 small ears less surface area; B1 less heat loss in cold
  2. (b) Thick fur traps air for insulation / white winter coat for camouflage in snow / fat layer for insulation.
    B1
  3. (c) The probability of an organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found.
    B1
Q17Continuous & discontinuous variation · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which human characteristic shows discontinuous variation?A heightB body massC ABO blood groupD hand span [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C ABO blood group
    ABO blood group falls into a few distinct categories (A, B, AB, O) with no intermediates. Height (A) has a continuous range of values.
Q18Continuous & discontinuous variation · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State two differences between continuous and discontinuous variation. [2]
  2. (b) Give one example of each type of variation in plants. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Continuous variation has a range of values with intermediates, discontinuous has a few distinct categories with no intermediates; continuous is caused by both genes and the environment, discontinuous is usually caused by genes only.
    1 mark each
  2. (b) Continuous: leaf length / plant height / seed mass. Discontinuous: flower colour in some species (e.g. purple or white pea flowers) / round or wrinkled pea seeds.
    1 mark each
Q19Continuous & discontinuous variation · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A student measured the lengths of 20 bean seeds from the same pod batch, in mm:14, 16, 15, 17, 18, 15, 16, 16, 19, 13, 17, 16, 15, 18, 16, 17, 14, 16, 15, 17

  1. (a) Calculate the mean length and the range of the seed lengths. [2]
  2. (b) Complete a frequency table for the classes 13–14, 15–16, 17–18 and 19–20 mm. [2]
  3. (c) State the type of graph that should be drawn to show these results, and the type of variation shown. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mean 16.0 mm; range 6 mm
    B1 mean (320 ÷ 20); B1 range (19 − 13)
  2. (b) 13–14: 3; 15–16: 10; 17–18: 6; 19–20: 1
    2 marks all correct; 1 mark for three correct
  3. (c) Histogram (bars touching); continuous variation
    1 mark each
Q20Continuous & discontinuous variation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A study measured the mean difference in adult height between pairs of twins.Identical twins raised together: 1.7 cmIdentical twins raised apart: 4.4 cmNon-identical twins raised together: 4.5 cm

  1. (a) Identical twins have the same genes. Explain why identical twins raised apart show a larger difference in height than identical twins raised together. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what the data for non-identical twins raised together show about the effect of genes on height. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Height is affected by the environment as well as genes; twins raised apart have different diets/living conditions, so their heights differ more.
    B1 environment affects height; B1 example such as different diet/health
  2. (b) Non-identical twins share the same environment but have different genes, yet differ more than identical twins raised together; so genes also affect height.
    B1 same environment but different genes; B1 larger difference shows genes have an effect
Q21Continuous & discontinuous variation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A class survey in a school in Lahore recorded the ABO blood groups of 120 students.A: 33; B: 41; AB: 9; O: 37

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of students with each blood group. Give your answers to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) State the type of graph that should be used to display these results and describe one feature of it. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why blood group is not affected by the environment. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 27.5%; B 34.2%; AB 7.5%; O 30.8%
    M1 number ÷ 120 × 100; A2 all four correct (A1 for two or three)
  2. (b) Bar chart; with gaps between the bars (categories on the x-axis).
    B1 bar chart; B1 bars separated / not touching
  3. (c) It is controlled only by genes (alleles inherited from parents); it does not change with diet or lifestyle.
    B1
Q22Selective breeding · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which statement describes selective breeding?A Humans choose individuals with useful features and breed them together over many generations.B Individuals best adapted to the environment survive and reproduce.C A gene from another species is inserted into an organism.D Offspring are produced by mitosis and are genetically identical. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A Humans choose individuals with useful features and breed them together over many generations.
    In selective breeding, humans choose the parents. B describes natural selection, where the environment does the selecting.
Q23Selective breeding · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Describe how a farmer could use selective breeding to increase the milk yield of buffaloes. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Choose the cows with the highest milk yield; mate them with bulls whose mothers/daughters had high milk yield; select the offspring with the highest milk yield and breed them; repeat over many generations.
    B1 select high-yield females; B1 select bull from high-yield line; B1 select best offspring to breed; B1 repeat over many generations
Q24Selective breeding · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how selective breeding could produce a wheat variety that has both a high yield and resistance to a fungal disease. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why selective breeding takes many years. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cross a high-yield variety with a disease-resistant variety; grow the offspring and select plants that show both high yield and resistance; breed these together; repeat over many generations until all offspring show both features.
    B1 cross the two varieties; B1 select offspring with both features; B1 breed these; B1 repeat for many generations
  2. (b) Each generation must grow to maturity before it can be bred; desirable alleles are mixed with undesirable ones, so many generations of selection are needed.
    B1 time for each generation to mature; B1 many generations needed to fix the features
Q25Selective breeding · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Give two differences between selective breeding and genetic modification. [2]
  2. (b) Name one crop and one animal that have been improved by selective breeding, and state the feature selected in each. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Genetic modification transfers individual genes, selective breeding mixes whole sets of genes; GM can move genes between different species, selective breeding only within one species (or very close relatives); GM is much faster.
    1 mark each for any two
  2. (b) e.g. wheat – higher grain yield / disease resistance; cattle – higher milk yield (or chickens – more eggs; sheep – more wool).
    1 mark each for organism + feature
Q26Selective breeding · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A cattle farm kept records of the mean milk yield per lactation of cows over four generations of selective breeding.Generation: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4Mean milk yield / dm³: 1800, 1950, 2100, 2280, 2400

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in mean milk yield from generation 0 to generation 4. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean increase in yield per generation. [1]
  3. (c) Discuss the possible disadvantages of breeding cattle selected only for high milk yield. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 33.3%
    M1 (2400 − 1800) ÷ 1800 × 100; A1 33.3%
  2. (b) 150 dm³ per generation
    B1 600 ÷ 4
  3. (c) Reduced genetic variation in the herd; so the herd is more vulnerable to new diseases or environmental change; harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be expressed (inbreeding), causing health problems such as leg or udder problems.
    1 mark each for any three: less variation; more susceptible to disease; inbreeding/harmful recessive conditions; animal welfare problems; other useful features lost
Q27Mutation · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) What is a gene mutation?A a random change in the base sequence of DNAB the loss of a whole chromosome during mitosisC the swapping of genes between plants by selective breedingD the change in a population over many generations [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a random change in the base sequence of DNA
    A gene mutation is a random change in the base sequence of DNA. D describes evolution, which can result from mutation plus selection but is not itself a mutation.
Q28Mutation · Core · Paper 2 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State two factors that increase the rate of mutation. [2]
  2. (b) State why mutation is important for evolution. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ionising radiation (e.g. X-rays, gamma rays); certain chemicals (e.g. tar in tobacco smoke).
    1 mark each
  2. (b) It is the source of new alleles / new variation on which natural selection acts.
    B1
Q29Mutation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a gene mutation can result in a protein that does not work. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why a mutation in a cell of the testis may affect future generations, but a mutation in a skin cell will not. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The base sequence of the gene is changed; so the sequence of amino acids in the protein is changed; the protein folds into a different shape (e.g. an enzyme's active site changes) and cannot carry out its function.
    B1 base sequence altered; B1 different amino acid sequence; B1 different shape → loss of function
  2. (b) Testis cells give rise to gametes (sperm), which pass the mutated allele to the offspring; skin cells are body cells and are not passed on.
    B1 gametes carry mutation to offspring; B1 body cell mutations not inherited
Q30Mutation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Cultures of cells were exposed to different doses of radiation and the mutation frequency was recorded.Dose / units: 0, 1, 2, 4Mutations per 100 000 cells: 3, 8, 13, 23

  1. (a) Describe the relationship between radiation dose and mutation frequency. [2]
  2. (b) Use the pattern in the data to predict the mutation frequency at a dose of 3 units. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why some mutations occur even when the dose is zero. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest why hospital radiographers who take X-ray images stand behind a lead screen. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mutation frequency increases as the dose increases; it increases by 5 mutations per 100 000 cells for each extra unit of dose (directly/linearly).
    B1 positive relationship; B1 constant increase of 5 per unit / linear, with data
  2. (b) 18 per 100 000 cells
    B1 3 + 5 × 3
  3. (c) Mutations happen randomly (spontaneously) during DNA copying, and there is always some natural background radiation.
    B1
  4. (d) X-rays are ionising radiation that increases the rate of mutation; lead absorbs the radiation, reducing the risk of mutations that could lead to cancer.
    B1 X-rays cause mutation; B1 lead absorbs radiation / reduces cancer risk
