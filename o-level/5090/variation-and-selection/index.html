Q1Natural selection & resistance · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) What is the first step in the development of an antibiotic-resistant population of bacteria? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A A random mutation produces a bacterium with an allele for resistance.
    Resistance begins with a chance mutation that is present before the antibiotic is used; the antibiotic then selects for it. C is wrong: individual bacteria do not adapt in response to the drug.
Q2Natural selection & resistance · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Describe the process of natural selection. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) There is variation within a population; organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support; there is competition (a struggle for survival) for resources; individuals with features better adapted to the environment are more likely to survive; they reproduce and pass on their alleles to offspring; over many generations these alleles become more common.
    1 mark each for any five: variation; overproduction; competition/struggle for survival; better adapted survive; reproduce; pass on alleles; over many generations allele frequency increases

  1. (a) A bacterium that is not killed (or whose growth is not stopped) by an antibiotic.
    B1

  2. (b) State two causes of variation in a population that natural selection can act on. [2]
  3. (c) Living organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support. State what happens as a result. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The process, resulting from natural selection, by which populations become more suited to their environment over many generations.

  2. (b) Mutation; sexual reproduction (meiosis and random fertilisation).

Q5Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a population of bacteria resistant to an antibiotic can develop in a hospital. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) A random mutation gives some bacteria an allele for resistance; when the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed; resistant bacteria survive (selection pressure); they reproduce rapidly by binary fission/asexually; passing on the resistance allele; the proportion of resistant bacteria increases over generations.
    1 mark each for any five: mutation; variation in population; antibiotic kills non-resistant; resistant survive; reproduce (rapidly); allele passed on; frequency increases. Do not credit 'bacteria adapt/become immune'.

  3. (c) Use different insecticides in rotation / spray less often / use other methods such as nets or removing standing water.

Q8Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

On a small island, the mean beak depth of a population of seed-eating finches was 9.2 mm. After a long drought killed most plants with small, soft seeds, the mean beak depth of the next generation was 9.7 mm.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in mean beak depth. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest how the drought caused this change in the population. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.4%
    M1 (9.7 − 9.2) ÷ 9.2 × 100; A1 5.4%
  2. (b) During the drought, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large hard seeds remained; birds with deeper beaks could crack them, so they were more likely to survive; they bred and passed on alleles for deep beaks to their offspring.
    B1 food changed / large hard seeds left; B1 deeper-beaked birds survived; B1 reproduced and passed on alleles
Q9Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) In an industrial area, the proportion of dark-coloured moths increased over many years after tree bark was blackened by soot. What is the best explanation? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A dark moths were better camouflaged, so fewer were eaten by birds and more survived to reproduce and pass on the allele for dark colour

Q10Natural selection & resistance · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A grain store used warfarin poison to kill rats. The percentage of rats trapped that were resistant to warfarin was recorded.year: 2010, 2013, 2016, 2019percentage resistant: 5, 18, 46, 71After 2019 the store stopped using warfarin, and over the next few years the percentage of resistant rats fell.

  1. (a) Calculate the mean yearly increase in the percentage of resistant rats between 2010 and 2019. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how the population of rats became resistant to warfarin. [4]
  3. (c) Resistant rats need more vitamin K in their diet than non-resistant rats. Suggest why the percentage of resistant rats fell after warfarin was no longer used. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 7.3 percentage points per year
    M1 (71 − 5) ÷ 9; A1 7.3.
  2. (b) A few rats had a mutation giving resistance; warfarin killed the non-resistant rats; resistant rats survived and bred, passing on the resistance allele; over many generations the proportion of resistant rats increased.
    1 mark each: variation / mutation present; non-resistant killed; resistant survive and reproduce; allele passed on / frequency increases.
  3. (c) Without warfarin, resistance gives no advantage; resistant rats are less likely to survive or reproduce if vitamin K is short, so non-resistant rats increase.
    1 mark each: no selective advantage without poison; resistant rats at a disadvantage (vitamin K) so selected against.

Q12Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Students counted the snails of one species with brown shells and with yellow shells in two habitats.Woodland with dark leaf litter: brown 180, yellow 60Grassland with pale dry grass: brown 45, yellow 105

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of brown snails in each habitat. [2]
  2. (b) Thrushes feed on these snails and find them by sight. Use natural selection to explain the difference between the two habitats. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Woodland: 75%; grassland: 30%
    M1 180 ÷ 240 × 100 and 45 ÷ 150 × 100; A1 both
  2. Q13Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Compare natural selection with artificial (selective) breeding. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the statement 'the bacteria became resistant because they needed to survive' is incorrect. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Similarities: both act on variation in a population; both cause alleles to become more common over many generations. Differences: in natural selection the environment determines which individuals survive and reproduce, in selective breeding humans choose; natural selection produces features that help survival (adaptation), selective breeding produces features useful to humans; selective breeding is usually faster.
    1 mark each for any four valid comparative points; at least one similarity and one difference for full marks
  2. (b) Resistance arises by random mutation, not in response to need; the antibiotic only selects bacteria that already had the resistance allele.
    B1 mutation is random/not caused by need; B1 antibiotic selects existing resistant bacteria
Q14Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) These statements describe natural selection.1 individuals with the most useful features survive and reproduce2 there is variation within a population3 there is competition for resources such as food4 the alleles for useful features become more common in the next generationWhich is the correct order? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 2 → 3 → 1 → 4
    Variation exists first; competition (struggle for survival) follows from over-production; the best adapted survive and reproduce; they pass on their alleles. C puts survival before competition, which is the main distractor.
Q15Natural selection & resistance · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Patients with tuberculosis are treated with three or four different antibiotics at the same time. Explain why this greatly reduces the chance that antibiotic-resistant bacteria will develop. [4]
  2. (b) Some antibiotics are kept for use only against very serious infections that other antibiotics cannot treat. Suggest why hospitals limit the use of these antibiotics. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Resistance to each antibiotic arises by a separate rare mutation; a bacterium resistant to one antibiotic is still killed by the others; the chance of one bacterium having resistance to all of them at once is very small; so no resistant bacteria survive to reproduce and be selected.
    1 mark each: each resistance needs a mutation / mutations rare; bacteria resistant to one are killed by another; very unlikely to be resistant to all; so resistant population cannot be selected.
  2. (b) The less an antibiotic is used, the less bacteria are exposed to it, so there is less selection for resistant bacteria; the antibiotic therefore stays effective for when it is really needed.
    1 mark each: less use means less selection for resistance; antibiotic stays effective for serious cases.

Q22Adaptive features · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain how each of these features of xerophytes reduces water loss: (i) leaves reduced to spines; (ii) stomata sunken in pits; (iii) leaves that roll up with hairs on the inside. [3]
  2. (b) Describe two features of the roots of xerophytes and explain how they help. [2]

Q23Adaptive features · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Mangrove trees grow in salty, waterlogged mud along the coast of the Indus delta. Some of their roots grow upwards out of the mud. Suggest how these roots help the tree to survive. [2]
  2. (b) The leaves of some mangroves have glands that excrete salt. Explain why this is an advantage. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why few other kinds of tree grow in this habitat. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The mud has little oxygen; the upward roots are exposed to air at low tide, so oxygen can diffuse in for the root cells' aerobic respiration.
    1 mark each: waterlogged mud lacks oxygen; aerial roots take in oxygen from the air for respiration.
  2. (b) Removing salt keeps the water potential of the leaf cells suitable so they do not lose water by osmosis / salt does not reach toxic concentrations.
    1 mark each: removes excess salt; avoids water loss by osmosis / salt damage to cells.
  3. (c) They lack these adaptations: their roots cannot get oxygen in the waterlogged mud, or they lose water by osmosis / are damaged by the salt.
    B1 lack of adaptation linked to low oxygen or salt.

Q30Adaptive features · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the difference between the surface and burrow temperatures at noon. [1]

  1. (a) The mutation is rare; the albino must inherit two recessive alleles; albino animals are easily seen by predators (and may have poor eyesight), so fewer survive and reproduce.
    1 mark each: mutations rare; need two copies / homozygous recessive; selected against (conspicuous / poor eyesight).
  2. (b) Heterozygous carriers have normal colour, so they are not selected against.
    B1.
Q72Mutation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Cultures of cells were exposed to different doses of radiation and the mutation frequency was recorded.Dose / units: 0, 1, 2, 4Mutations per 100 000 cells: 3, 8, 13, 23

  1. (a) Describe the relationship between radiation dose and mutation frequency. [2]
  2. (b) Use the pattern in the data to predict the mutation frequency at a dose of 3 units. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why some mutations occur even when the dose is zero. [1]
  4. (d) Suggest why hospital radiographers who take X-ray images stand behind a lead screen. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mutation frequency increases as the dose increases; it increases by 5 mutations per 100 000 cells for each extra unit of dose (directly/linearly).
    B1 positive relationship; B1 constant increase of 5 per unit / linear, with data
  2. (b) 18 per 100 000 cells
    B1 3 + 5 × 3
  3. (c) Mutations happen randomly (spontaneously) during DNA copying, and there is always some natural background radiation.
    B1
  4. (d) X-rays are ionising radiation that increases the rate of mutation; lead absorbs the radiation, reducing the risk of mutations that could lead to cancer.
    B1 X-rays cause mutation; B1 lead absorbs radiation / reduces cancer risk
Q73Mutation · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Plates of bacteria were exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light for different times. The number of colonies that grew, and the number of these that showed a new feature (mutants), were counted.UV exposure / s: 0, 10, 20, 30surviving colonies: 500, 320, 140, 40mutant colonies: 0, 6, 9, 5

  1. (a) Calculate the mutation frequency (mutant colonies as a percentage of surviving colonies) for each exposure time. Give each answer to 1 decimal place. [3]
  2. (b) Describe the effect of UV exposure time on survival and on mutation frequency. [2]
  3. (c) A student says the 20 s exposure is 'best for producing mutants' because it produced the most mutant colonies. Evaluate this statement. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0 s: 0.0 %; 10 s: 1.9 %; 20 s: 6.4 %; 30 s: 12.5 %
    6 ÷ 320 × 100 = 1.9; 9 ÷ 140 × 100 = 6.4; 5 ÷ 40 × 100 = 12.5. 3 marks all correct, 2 marks two, 1 mark one.
  2. (b) As exposure time increases, fewer bacteria survive, but the percentage of survivors that are mutants increases.
    1 mark each.
  3. (c) 20 s gave the largest number of mutant colonies (9), but 30 s gave the highest mutation frequency; with only one plate per time the difference may not be reliable.
    1 mark each: number vs frequency; need repeats / small numbers.
Q74Mutation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A section of a gene is 36 bases long. Each group of three bases codes for one amino acid in a protein.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of amino acids coded for by this section of DNA. [1]
  2. (b) A mutation removes three bases (one complete group) from this section. Calculate the number of amino acids the section now codes for, and suggest the effect on the protein. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why some single-base changes have no effect on the protein. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 12
    B1 36 ÷ 3 = 12.
  2. (b) 11 amino acids; the protein is one amino acid shorter, so its shape may change and it may not work properly (e.g. an enzyme's active site may change).
    M1 (36 − 3) ÷ 3; A1 11; B1 changed shape / function of protein.
  3. (c) The new triplet may still code for the same amino acid (or the change is in a part that does not affect shape).
    B1.
Q75Mutation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In one fish species a particular gene mutates in about 1 out of every 100 000 eggs. One female fish lays 2 million eggs in a season.

  1. (a) Calculate how many of the eggs would be expected to carry a new mutation in this gene. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why, despite this, most of these mutations will not appear in the adult population. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why mutation is still important for the long-term survival of fish species. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20 eggs
    M1 2 000 000 ÷ 100 000; A1 20.
  2. (b) Most eggs and young fish die before adulthood (predation, starvation); many mutations are harmful or recessive and hidden, so they are removed or not seen.
    1 mark each: high mortality of young; harmful mutations selected against / recessive.
  3. (c) It is the source of new alleles and variation; if the environment changes, some individuals may have alleles that let them survive, so the species can adapt by natural selection.
    1 mark each: new alleles / variation; allows adaptation / natural selection when conditions change.
