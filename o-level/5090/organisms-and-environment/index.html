Q11Food chains & webs · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a lake near Hyderabad, phytoplankton are eaten by zooplankton and water snails. Zooplankton are eaten by small fish, which are eaten by herons. Water snails are eaten by ducks. Water hyacinth, a floating plant, has spread over most of the lake surface.

  1. (a) Suggest and explain the effect of the water hyacinth on the phytoplankton. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how this could affect the number of herons. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why the water-hyacinth leaves do not replace the phytoplankton as food for this web. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Phytoplankton decrease; the floating hyacinth blocks light, so the phytoplankton cannot photosynthesise.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) Fewer phytoplankton means less food for zooplankton/water snails, so they decrease; so fewer small fish; herons have less food so their numbers fall (or they move away).
    1 mark each for three linked steps through the web.
  3. (c) The animals in this web (zooplankton/small fish) do not eat water hyacinth / energy in hyacinth passes mainly to decomposers.

Q14Food chains & webs · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a hill forest, leopards eat wild boar and monkeys. Wild boar feed on fallen seeds, roots and young tree seedlings. Over several years leopards became rare in the area and the numbers of wild boar increased.

  1. (a) Explain why the numbers of wild boar increased after leopards became rare. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest why the number of young trees in the forest then fell. [2]
  3. (c) Use this example to explain why removing a top predator can affect organisms at every trophic level. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Leopards are predators of wild boar; with less predation, more boar survive and reproduce.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) More wild boar eat/dig up more seeds and seedlings; so fewer young trees grow.
    1 mark each.
  3. (c) Top predator controls herbivore numbers; changes in herbivores change producer numbers, which affects all other organisms that depend on those producers (food and habitat).
    1 mark each.

Q26Energy flow & pyramids · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

  1. (a) Calculate the energy reaching the tertiary consumers, in kJ per m² per year. [2]

Q27Energy flow & pyramids · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

  2. (b) Fish farms in Thailand feed farmed fish with pellets made from wild-caught smaller fish. Use ideas about energy flow to explain why this may not be sustainable. [2]

  1. (a) energy is lost at every transfer between trophic levels (e.g. as heat from respiration); so each level always has less energy than the one below

Q29Energy flow & pyramids · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

On two farms, animals were given food containing 10 000 kJ of energy. Chickens converted 4 % of the energy into body mass. Tilapia fish in ponds converted 12 % of the energy into body mass.

  1. (a) Calculate how many times more energy ends up in the tilapia than in the chickens from the same feed. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest two reasons why chickens convert less of their food energy into body mass than fish do. [2]

Show answer
  1. (a) Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms, faeces and urine; they break down proteins and urea; releasing ammonium ions into the soil; which nitrifying bacteria then convert to nitrate.

Q88Nitrogen cycle (option) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks

  1. (a) Compare how the nitrogen cycle works in the two fields. Refer to the bacteria involved and the conditions each one needs. [5]
  2. (b) Explain why the nitrate in the rice field fell faster than the nitrate in the wheat field. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest two ways the rice farmer could reduce the loss of nitrate from the field. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Waterlogged rice soil has very little oxygen: nitrifying bacteria, which need oxygen, are inactive, so little ammonium is changed to nitrate; denitrifying bacteria work in these anaerobic conditions and convert nitrate to nitrogen gas, which is lost to the air. Well-drained, aerated wheat soil has oxygen: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, so more nitrate stays available to the crop; denitrification is slow.
    1 mark each for any five, and both fields must be covered: waterlogged soil lacks oxygen; nitrifying bacteria need oxygen and are less active; denitrifying bacteria work anaerobically and turn nitrate into nitrogen gas; nitrogen gas is lost to the air; well-drained soil is aerobic so nitrifying bacteria make nitrate; denitrification is slow in aerobic soil.
  2. (b) Denitrifying bacteria turn nitrate into nitrogen gas in the waterlogged soil, and this gas escapes to the air; the rice plants absorb nitrate for growth; nitrate is washed out of the soil in drainage water (leaching).
    1 mark each for any three.
  3. (c) Drain the field for periods so the soil has air (less denitrification); add manure or compost that releases nitrate slowly; do not spread fertiliser just before heavy rain (leaching).
    1 mark each for any two sensible suggestions.
Q89Nitrogen cycle (option) · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Compare the nitrogen cycle with the carbon cycle. Give similarities and differences. [10]
Show answer
  1. (a) Similarities: both are recycled between living things and the environment; both rely on decomposers; both are passed along food chains by feeding; both are taken in by plants first; both are affected by human activity (fertilisers / fossil fuels).
