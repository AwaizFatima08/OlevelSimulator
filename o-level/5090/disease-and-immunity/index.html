Q1Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which type of cell produces antibodies? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C lymphocyte
    Lymphocytes produce antibodies. Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens but do not make antibodies.
Q2Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by an antigen. [1]
  2. (b) Describe two ways in which antibodies help to destroy pathogens. [2]
  3. (c) Explain the difference between an antibody and an antibiotic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A molecule (e.g. a protein) on the surface of a pathogen / cell that stimulates antibody production.
    B1
  2. (b) Bind to antigens and mark pathogens so phagocytes can engulf them; directly destroy pathogens / cause them to clump together.
    1 mark each
  3. (c) An antibody is a protein made by lymphocytes in the body that binds to a specific antigen; an antibiotic is a drug (medicine) taken to kill bacteria (or stop their growth).
    B1 antibody: protein made by lymphocytes / body; B1 antibiotic: drug that kills bacteria
Q3Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why antibiotics cannot be used to treat measles or influenza. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a person develops active immunity after recovering from chickenpox. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) These diseases are caused by viruses; antibiotics only kill bacteria / do not affect viruses.
    B1 caused by virus; B1 antibiotics act on bacteria only
  2. (b) Lymphocytes produced antibodies against the virus antigens during the infection; memory cells remain and give fast antibody production on re-infection.
    B1 antibodies made by own lymphocytes; B1 memory cells

Q7Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Polio vaccination campaigns in Pakistan give drops of vaccine to young children.

  1. (a) Describe what a vaccine contains. [2]
  2. (b) Describe how vaccination protects a person against polio for a long time. [3]

Q10Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Polio vaccine contains weakened polio viruses. Explain why the vaccine must be kept cold from the factory until it is given to a child. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a child given a heat-damaged vaccine may still catch polio later. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The antigens are proteins (on the virus) that can be damaged/denatured by heat; then they no longer have the right shape to stimulate the correct lymphocytes, so the vaccine does not work.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) No (or few) antibodies and memory cells against polio were made; so when the real virus enters, the response is too slow to stop it.
    1 mark each.

Q13Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In one region, 9,120,000 out of 9,600,000 children under five were given measles vaccine.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of children vaccinated. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how vaccinating a large percentage of a population also protects people who are not vaccinated. [3]

Show answer

  2. (b) Most people are immune, so the pathogen cannot easily spread / there are few people to infect; an infected person is unlikely to meet a non-immune person; so the disease is controlled (herd immunity).

  3. (c) Too young / medical condition / weak immune system / parents refuse / live in remote areas.
    B1

Q15Antibodies, immunity & vaccination · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Rabies is a viral disease passed on in the saliva of an infected animal, usually by a bite. A person bitten by a stray dog is given an injection of ready-made antibodies against rabies and also a course of rabies vaccine.

  1. (a) Explain why the injection of ready-made antibodies is given immediately. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a course of vaccine is also given. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest why rabies vaccine works even though it is given after the virus has entered the body. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The antibodies act at once against the virus; the person's own immune response (from the vaccine) would take days/weeks to make enough antibodies.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) The vaccine stimulates the person's lymphocytes to make their own antibodies and memory cells; giving long-lasting (active) protection after the injected antibodies have been broken down.
    1 mark each.
  3. (c) The virus spreads slowly to the brain (long incubation), so there is time for the body to make antibodies first.
    B1.

Q27Pathogens & transmission · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

Q29Pathogens & transmission · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

    B1.

(a) Use sterile nutrient agar plates; a volunteer touches one plate with a fingertip after each method (and an unwashed control); same finger, same pressure and time; wash for the same time with same volume/temperature of water; seal lids with tape and label; incubate at 25 °C (not 37 °C) for 2–3 days; count colonies; repeat with several volunteers and find means; never open plates after incubation; dispose of plates by autoclaving.
    1 mark each for any six, including a control and at least one safety precaution.
  2. (b) Fewest colonies after alcohol gel or soap and water; most with unwashed/water only; soap/alcohol remove or kill bacteria; fewer bacteria on hands means fewer pathogens transferred to food or mouth.
    1 mark each.
