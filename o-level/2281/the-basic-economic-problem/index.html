Q1Factors of production · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which is an example of capital as a factor of production? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a delivery van owned by a courier firm
    Capital is a man-made aid to production, such as a van. Money (B) is not itself a factor of production in economics because it does not produce anything directly; fish in the sea (C) are land (a natural resource); risk-taking (D) is enterprise.
Q2Factors of production · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark

Show answer
  1. (a) B profit
    Organising production and bearing risk is enterprise, rewarded by profit. Interest (A) is the reward to capital, rent (C) to land and wages (D) to labour; she would receive wages only if she were employed by someone else.
Q3Factors of production · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A tea estate in the hills of Sri Lanka grows tea bushes on sloping fields that receive heavy rainfall.

  1. (a) Define land as a factor of production. [2]
  2. (b) Identify two examples of land used by the tea estate. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Land is all the natural resources used in production, such as the surface of the earth, soil, minerals, water and climate.
    1 mark: natural resources / gifts of nature. 1 mark: example or 'used to produce goods and services'.
  2. (b) Any two of: the fields/soil, the rainfall, the climate/sunlight, rivers or streams used for water.
    1 mark each for any two natural resources. Do not accept tea-processing machines (capital) or pickers (labour).
Q4Factors of production · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A family bakery in Lahore makes bread and cakes. It rents a shop, employs six bakers, uses two large ovens and is run by its owner, who takes the business decisions.

  1. (a) Identify the four factors of production used by the bakery, giving one example of each from the information. [4]
  2. (b) State the reward to labour and the reward to capital. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Land – the site the shop stands on; labour – the six bakers; capital – the ovens; enterprise – the owner who organises the business and takes decisions/risks.
    1 mark for each correct factor with a matching example.
  2. (b) Labour: wages (salaries). Capital: interest.
    1 mark: wages. 1 mark: interest.
Q5Factors of production · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A country has a total population of 50 million. Of these, 32 million are of working age, and 20 million people are in the labour force (employed or actively seeking work).

  1. (a) Calculate the labour force as a percentage of the total population. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two ways in which the quantity of labour in this country could increase. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 40%
    20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40% (B1).
  2. (b) e.g. (1) Raising the retirement age keeps older workers in the labour force for longer. (2) Providing childcare encourages more women of working age to seek work, raising the participation rate. Also: net immigration of workers; higher wages attracting people into work; a larger population of working age over time.
    For each of two ways: 1 mark for the way, 1 mark for explaining how it adds to the number of people/hours available for work.
Q6Factors of production · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark

Show answer
  1. (a) B profit
    Enterprise (the entrepreneur) takes the risk of organising production and is rewarded with profit. Interest (A) is the reward to capital, rent (C) to land and wages (D) to labour.
Q7Factors of production · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change would increase the occupational mobility of labour? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a government-funded scheme retraining coal miners as electricians
    Occupational mobility is the ease of moving between jobs; retraining gives miners the skills for a new occupation. Higher house prices (B) reduce geographical, not occupational, mobility. Stricter licences (C) and narrower specialisation (D) make it harder to change jobs, so they reduce occupational mobility.
Q8Factors of production · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Hospitals in rural Balochistan have many vacancies for doctors, while hospitals in Karachi receive many more applications than they have posts.

  1. (a) Define geographical mobility of labour. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why doctors may be geographically immobile. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The ease with which workers can move from one area or region to another to take up a job.
    1 mark: moving between places/areas. 1 mark: to find or take a job / for work.
  2. (b) Any two, developed: family and social ties keep doctors in the city; differences in housing costs or lack of suitable housing; fewer good schools and facilities for their children in rural areas; lack of information about vacancies; cost of moving; differences in language or culture.
    For each of two reasons: 1 mark for the reason, 1 mark for explaining why it discourages moving to the rural area.
Q9Factors of production · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A garment factory in Dhaka, Bangladesh, sends its machine operators on a training course and replaces old sewing machines with faster computer-controlled ones.

  1. (a) Explain how training improves the quality of labour. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse how an improvement in the quality of capital can increase the factory's output. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Training gives workers new skills and knowledge, so each worker can produce more output or better-quality output in the same time (higher productivity).
    1 mark: increases skills/knowledge. 1 mark: leads to more or better output per worker / higher productivity.
  2. (b) Faster machines sew more garments per hour; they break down less often, so less production time is lost; computer control reduces mistakes and wasted cloth; so with the same workers and hours, total output rises and average cost may fall.
    Up to 4 marks for a logical chain: better technology → each machine produces more per hour (1) → fewer breakdowns/less waste (1) → each worker using the machine is more productive (1) → total output rises with the same inputs (1).
Q10Factors of production · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain two functions of an entrepreneur. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why profit is described as the reward for risk-taking. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Organising: bringing together land, labour and capital to produce. Risk-bearing: putting money into the business without knowing whether it will make a profit. (Also: making decisions on what, how and how much to produce; innovating with new products or methods.)
    For each of two functions: 1 mark for identifying it, 1 mark for development.
  2. (b) The entrepreneur is paid only what is left after all other factors have been paid; this may be a loss, so profit is the return that compensates for taking that uncertain risk.
    1 mark: profit is the residual / not guaranteed / may be a loss. 1 mark: without the chance of profit no one would take the risk of starting a business.
Q11Factors of production · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change would increase the quality, rather than the quantity, of a country's labour force? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C free vocational training courses for school leavers
    The quality of labour is the skills, education and health of workers; vocational training raises skills and so productivity. A, B and D all increase the number of workers available (quantity), not their skills.
Q12Factors of production · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Over-fishing in the sea off the coast of Gwadar has reduced the stocks of several fish species. Local fishing crews now catch less each trip.

  1. (a) Explain why fish stocks are classified as land rather than capital. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a fall in the quantity of one factor of production, such as fish stocks, must reduce a country's total output. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Fish in the sea are a natural resource (a gift of nature) and are not made by people, so they are land; capital is man-made, such as boats and nets.
    1 mark: natural resource / not man-made. 1 mark: contrast with capital being man-made (e.g. boats).
  2. (b) Output in the fishing industry is likely to fall, but total output need not fall if resources are reallocated or other factors substitute.
    Up to 3 marks for 'must reduce': fewer fish means less catch with the same labour and boats; fishing workers may become unemployed; related industries (processing, exports) lose output; incomes and spending in the area fall. Up to 3 marks for 'need not': other factors can be substituted (fish farming/aquaculture using capital); workers can move to other industries; productivity elsewhere may rise; fish stocks can recover if fishing is controlled. Max 5 without a reasoned conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion (e.g. depends on how mobile the workers are).
Q13Factors of production · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A government is considering paying part of the removal costs of workers who move from high-unemployment regions to regions with labour shortages.

  1. (a) Identify two costs to a worker of moving to a new region for a job. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the government should subsidise removal costs to improve the geographical mobility of labour. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: removal/transport costs; higher housing costs; loss of contact with family and friends; children changing school; cost of finding a new home.
    1 mark each for any two costs (financial or non-financial).
  2. (b) Arguments both ways leading to a supported conclusion.
    Up to 3 marks for 'should': fills vacancies so output rises; reduces regional unemployment and benefit spending; workers earn higher incomes and pay more tax; firms in shortage areas can expand. Up to 3 marks for 'should not': opportunity cost of government spending; moving costs are often not the main barrier (family ties, housing shortages); depopulation of poorer regions; may only help workers who would have moved anyway; better to bring jobs to the workers. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q14Factors of production · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A coastal city-state has increased its land area from 580 km² to 735 km² by reclaiming land from the sea.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in the city-state's land area. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why land is often said to be fixed in supply, yet the quantity of land available for production can change. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 26.7%
    M1: (735 − 580) ÷ 580 × 100. A1: 26.7%.
  2. (b) The total surface of the earth is fixed, so in the economy as a whole land is in fixed supply. But the land available for production can increase through reclamation, irrigation of deserts, clearing forests or discovering new mineral deposits, and can decrease through soil erosion, flooding, desertification or depletion of resources.
    1 mark: total area of the earth/natural resources is fixed. Up to 3 marks for ways the usable quantity changes, e.g. reclamation (1), irrigation/fertilisers improving land (1), discovery of new resources (1), erosion/depletion/climate change reducing it (1).
Q15Factors of production · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 14 marks

At the start of 2026 the machinery and equipment used by a country's manufacturing firms was valued at $48.0 billion. During 2026 firms spent $6.2 billion on new machinery, while machinery worth $3.8 billion wore out or became out of date (depreciation).

  1. (a) Calculate the net increase in the value of machinery in 2026 and express it as a percentage of the value at the start of the year. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why an increase in the quantity of capital may not increase output by as much as expected. [4]
  3. (c) Discuss whether or not a country should spend more on improving the quality of its labour, rather than on increasing the quantity of capital, in order to raise output. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Net increase = $2.4 billion, which is 5.0% of the starting value.
    6.2 − 3.8 = 2.4 (M1); 2.4 ÷ 48.0 × 100 = 5.0% (A1).
  2. (b) e.g. not enough skilled workers to operate the machines; unreliable electricity or poor transport; weak demand for the extra output.
    2 marks for each of two developed reasons: shortage of skilled labour/training to use the machines; machines lie idle because of power cuts or lack of raw materials; poor infrastructure (roads, ports) limits output; little demand, so firms do not use the extra capacity; the new machines may replace old ones rather than add to capacity; poor management/enterprise.
  3. (c) Improving labour quality can raise productivity widely and lasts, but takes a long time; capital can raise output faster but needs skilled workers. Best answer: both are complementary; conclusion depends on the country's current shortages.
    Labour quality (up to 4): education and training raise productivity of every worker; skilled workers can use existing and new capital better; helps occupational mobility and adaptability; benefits spread across all industries; can attract foreign firms. Capital (up to 4): machines can raise output quickly; new technology embodies higher productivity; may be needed where infrastructure is poor; training takes many years and trained workers may emigrate (brain drain); education has an opportunity cost. Conclusion (needed for top band): e.g. the two are complements; which comes first depends on whether the shortage is of skills or of equipment. Levels: 1–3 one-sided or undeveloped; 4–6 both sides developed; 7–8 both sides plus justified conclusion.
Q16Production possibility curve · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) On a production possibility curve diagram, what does a point inside the curve show? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B Some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
    A point inside the PPC means more of both goods could be made, so some resources are idle or used inefficiently. Points on the curve show full, efficient use (A); points outside are unattainable (C); growth is shown by an outward shift of the curve (D).
Q17Production possibility curve · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The table shows the maximum combinations of rice and textiles that a country can produce when all its resources are fully employed.Point | Rice (million tonnes) | Textiles (million metres)A | 0 | 100B | 20 | 95C | 40 | 85D | 60 | 65E | 80 | 35F | 90 | 0

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing rice production from 40 million tonnes to 60 million tonnes. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the opportunity cost of one extra million tonnes of rice when moving from point D to point E. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the opportunity cost of rice rises as more rice is produced. [2]
  4. (d) State whether producing 40 million tonnes of rice and 70 million metres of textiles would be an efficient use of resources. Explain your answer. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 20 million metres of textiles
    85 − 65 = 20 million metres of textiles (B1). Must be expressed in textiles.
  2. (b) 1.5 million metres of textiles
    M1: (65 − 35) ÷ (80 − 60) = 30 ÷ 20. A1: 1.5 million metres of textiles per million tonnes of rice.
  3. (c) Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. As more rice is grown, the country must use workers and land that are better suited to textiles, so more textiles must be given up for each extra tonne of rice.
    1 mark: factors of production are not equally suitable / not perfectly mobile between uses. 1 mark: less suitable resources are moved, so more textiles are lost per extra unit of rice.
  4. (d) No. Point C shows 40 million tonnes of rice can be produced with 85 million metres of textiles, so 40 and 70 is inside the curve: resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
    1 mark: not efficient / inside the PPC. 1 mark: comparison with point C (85 million metres possible), so some resources are underused.
Q18Production possibility curve · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

An earthquake destroys many factories, roads and bridges in one region of a country. Farmland in the region is much less affected.

  1. (a) Describe the effect of the earthquake on the country's production possibility curve. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the shift may not be the same for all goods, using a PPC with manufactured goods and farm goods on the axes. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how foreign aid for rebuilding could change the position of the PPC over time. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The PPC shifts inwards (to the left), because the quantity of capital has fallen, so the maximum possible output falls.
    1 mark: inward shift. 1 mark: reason – fewer resources/capital destroyed.
  2. (b) Factories were damaged more than farmland, so the maximum output of manufactured goods falls more than that of farm goods; the curve shifts in further on the manufactured goods axis.
    1 mark: manufacturing capital lost more than farmland. 1 mark: so the curve pivots/shifts in more on the manufactured-goods axis.
  3. (c) Aid could pay for new factories, roads and bridges, increasing the capital stock again, so the PPC shifts back outwards, possibly beyond its original position if newer technology is used.
    1 mark: aid funds rebuilding of capital / infrastructure. 1 mark: PPC shifts outwards again.

Q20Production possibility curve · Core · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A country's production possibility curve passes through these combinations (smartphones in millions, maize in million tonnes):0 smartphones, 50 maize10 smartphones, 45 maize20 smartphones, 36 maize30 smartphones, 22 maize40 smartphones, 0 maizeThe country is currently producing 20 million smartphones and 30 million tonnes of maize.

  1. (a) State whether the current combination is on, inside or outside the production possibility curve, and what this shows about the economy. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the extra maize the country could produce without reducing its output of smartphones. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two reasons why an economy may produce inside its production possibility curve. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Inside the curve; some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
    Inside the PPC (B1) because with 20 million smartphones it could produce 36 million tonnes of maize; this shows unemployed/underused resources (B1).
  2. (b) 6 million tonnes
    36 − 30 = 6 million tonnes (B1).
  3. (c) e.g. unemployment of workers; factories or land left idle because of low demand or power shortages; inefficient methods.
    2 marks each for two developed reasons: unemployment (workers able and willing to work but without jobs); idle capital, e.g. factories closed by power shortages or low demand; land left unused; inefficient production methods or poor management; strikes or disruption such as floods.
Q21Production possibility curve · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A country has 2 million workers. In one year each worker can produce either 5 tonnes of wheat or 2 machines (the same for every worker). At present 1.2 million workers grow wheat, 0.6 million make machines and the rest are unemployed.

  1. (a) Calculate the maximum quantity of wheat the country could produce if all workers grew wheat. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the country's current output of wheat and of machines. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the extra machines that could be made if all the unemployed workers were employed making machines. [1]
  4. (d) Explain whether the current combination of wheat and machines is on or inside the country's production possibility curve. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 10 million tonnes
    2 million × 5 = 10 million tonnes (B1).
  2. (b) 6 million tonnes of wheat and 1.2 million machines
    1.2 million × 5 = 6 million tonnes (B1); 0.6 million × 2 = 1.2 million machines (B1).
  3. (c) 0.4 million (400 000) machines
    Unemployed = 2 − 1.2 − 0.6 = 0.2 million; 0.2 million × 2 = 0.4 million (B1).
  4. (d) Inside, because 0.2 million workers are unemployed, so more of one good could be produced without less of the other.
    Inside the PPC (B1); because not all resources (workers) are employed, so output of one good could rise with no fall in the other (B1).
Q22Production possibility curve · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change would shift a country's production possibility curve outwards? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B the discovery of new reserves of copper
    New copper reserves increase the quantity of land (natural resources), so the maximum possible output rises and the PPC shifts out. Lower unemployment (A) moves the economy from inside towards the curve without shifting it; emigration of skilled workers (C) shifts it inwards; moving workers between industries (D) is a movement along the curve.
Q23Production possibility curve · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a production possibility curve. [2]
  2. (b) A country decides to produce more capital goods and fewer consumer goods. Explain, by describing a production possibility curve with capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other, the effect of this decision now and in the future. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods (or types of goods) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used.
    1 mark: maximum output combinations of two goods. 1 mark: with all resources fully/efficiently employed (given resources and technology).
  2. (b) Now: the economy moves along its PPC towards the capital goods axis – more capital goods, fewer consumer goods, so the opportunity cost is consumer goods given up today. In the future: the extra machinery and infrastructure increase productive capacity, so the PPC shifts outwards and more of both goods can be produced.
    1 mark: movement along the existing curve. 1 mark: fewer consumer goods now / opportunity cost. 1 mark: increase in capital stock raises productive capacity. 1 mark: PPC shifts outwards in the future.
Q24Production possibility curve · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A small country's production possibility curve for computers and wheat is a straight line. If it produces only computers it can make 400 000 computers a year; if it produces only wheat it can grow 2 000 000 tonnes a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of producing one tonne of wheat, in computers. [2]
  2. (b) Explain what a straight-line PPC shows about the country's factors of production. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the maximum quantity of wheat the country can grow if it produces 100 000 computers. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 0.2 computers
    M1: 400 000 ÷ 2 000 000. A1: 0.2 computers per tonne of wheat.
  2. (b) The opportunity cost is constant, which means the factors of production are equally suited to producing computers and wheat.
    1 mark: constant opportunity cost. 1 mark: resources equally efficient / perfectly transferable between the two goods.
  3. (c) 1 500 000 tonnes
    M1: computers given up = 400 000 − 100 000 = 300 000; 300 000 ÷ 0.2. A1: 1 500 000 tonnes.

Q26Production possibility curve · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Two countries, Alpha and Beta, had identical production possibility curves for capital goods and consumer goods in 2026. In 2026 Alpha produced 40 units of capital goods and 60 units of consumer goods. Beta produced 10 units of capital goods and 90 units of consumer goods. Both used all their resources.

  1. (a) Identify which country is likely to have the higher material standard of living in 2026, and give a reason. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse factors, other than the choice between capital goods and consumer goods, that could cause Beta's production possibility curve to shift outwards faster than Alpha's over the next ten years. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Beta, because it produces more consumer goods for people to use now.
    Beta (B1); more consumer goods are available to households now (B1).
  2. (b) e.g. Beta improves education and training (quality of labour); its labour force grows through population growth or immigration; it discovers new natural resources; it adopts new technology or attracts foreign firms; Alpha's extra capital may be badly used or poorly maintained.
    Up to 6 marks for developed points: better education/training raises labour quality and productivity (1+1); more workers from population growth or immigration increase labour quantity (1); discovery of resources such as minerals increases land (1); new technology/foreign direct investment raises productivity (1); Alpha's capital goods may be unsuitable, idle or not maintained, so its PPC shifts less than expected (1). Link: any increase in the quantity or quality of resources shifts the PPC outwards (1).
Q27Production possibility curve · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Over one decade a country's unemployment rate fell from 9% to 5%. Over the same decade, the government also greatly increased spending on secondary and technical education.

  1. (a) Explain the difference between a movement from a point inside a PPC to a point on the curve, and an outward shift of the PPC. [4]
  2. (b) Analyse how investment in education could shift the country's PPC outwards. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A movement from inside to the curve happens when unemployed or inefficiently used resources are brought into use (e.g. the fall in unemployment); actual output rises but productive capacity does not change. An outward shift happens when the quantity or quality of resources increases (e.g. better-educated workers), so the maximum possible output – productive capacity – rises.
    1 mark: inside → on the curve = using idle resources. 1 mark: capacity unchanged / actual output rises. 1 mark: outward shift = more or better resources/technology. 1 mark: productive capacity/potential output rises.

  2. (b) e.g. which users get water first (homes, hospitals, farms or factories); whether to spend on new supply (desalination) instead of other services.
    2 marks each for two developed choices: who receives water (for whom) and how to ration it, e.g. by price or limits; whether to build a desalination plant or pipeline, giving up other spending (opportunity cost); which uses to restrict, e.g. car washing or farming; how to reduce leaks vs other projects.
Q57Nature of the economic problem · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain what economists mean by scarcity. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not scarcity would disappear if a country's income doubled. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Scarcity is the situation in which there are not enough resources to produce all the goods and services people want; wants are unlimited but resources are finite.
    1 mark: resources are limited/finite. 1 mark: wants are unlimited / greater than what can be produced.
  2. (b) Scarcity would be reduced but would not disappear: more needs could be met, but wants grow with income and resources (and time) remain finite.
    Up to 3 marks for 'yes' points, e.g.: more goods and services can be bought; basic needs such as food and shelter could be met for more people; absolute poverty falls; government can afford more public services. Up to 3 marks for 'no' points, e.g.: wants are unlimited and rise as income rises (new products, better housing, travel); resources such as land and fuel are still finite; time is scarce, so choices remain; the extra income may be unevenly shared. Max 5 without a reasoned conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion (e.g. scarcity is relative to wants, so it can never disappear).
Q58Nature of the economic problem · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

After a severe flood, a government has a limited stock of food, tents and medicines to share among 3 million people who have lost their homes.

  1. (a) Identify the 'for whom to produce' question that the government faces. [2]
  2. (b) Analyse how the scarcity of food, tents and medicines forces the government to answer all three fundamental economic questions. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Who should receive the limited food, tents and medicines, e.g. children and the sick first?
    Recognises the question is about who receives the goods (1); applied example, e.g. which families or areas get tents first (1).
  2. (b) What: which relief goods to buy/produce with its budget; How: whether to use the army, NGOs or private firms, local or imported supplies; For whom: which people get the limited goods. Each answer has an opportunity cost.
    Scarcity: wants exceed the goods available (1). What: choose between, e.g., more tents or more medicines with a limited budget (1+1). How: choose methods, e.g. army or NGOs, local or imported goods (1+1). For whom: decide priority groups (1). Max 6.
Q59Nature of the economic problem · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) Define the basic economic problem. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the economic problem is the same in a high-income country as in a low-income country. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Unlimited wants but finite (scarce) resources to satisfy them.
    Unlimited wants (1); limited/finite resources (1).
  2. (b) Same: both face scarcity and must answer what, how and for whom; wants grow with income. Different: in low-income countries scarcity concerns basic needs (food, water, healthcare), while in high-income countries it concerns more wants and choices like healthcare for ageing populations. Conclusion: the problem exists in both but its severity and content differ.
    Same (up to 4): all countries have finite resources and unlimited wants; all must choose and face opportunity cost; rich countries still have shortages, e.g. of housing or skilled workers; wants rise as income rises. Different (up to 4): in poor countries many basic needs are unmet, so scarcity affects survival; fewer resources/capital per person; rich countries choose between luxuries, so opportunity costs are less severe for individuals; poorer countries may lack the means to answer 'how' efficiently. Levels: 1–3 limited; 4–6 both sides; 7–8 plus justified conclusion.
Q60Nature of the economic problem · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks

In some countries, including Pakistan, government hospitals provide many treatments free of charge to patients.

  1. (a) Explain two ways, other than price, in which a government hospital could decide which patients receive treatment first. [4]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not providing healthcare free of charge removes the problem of scarcity in healthcare. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. by medical need (most urgent cases first); first come, first served (queues/waiting lists); appointments; priority groups such as children or the elderly.
    2 marks each for two ways, each identified (1) and explained (1): by medical need/urgency (triage); queueing or waiting lists – first come, first served; appointment systems; priority for groups such as children, pregnant women or the elderly; referral by a doctor.
  2. (b) It removes the price barrier so more people can be treated, but resources (doctors, beds, medicines) remain limited; demand rises, so queues and waiting lists ration care instead of price; government faces opportunity cost. Conclusion: it changes how care is rationed but does not remove scarcity.
    Reduces some effects (up to 4): poor households can afford treatment; access depends on need, not income; healthier workers. Does not remove scarcity (up to 4): doctors, nurses, beds and medicines are still limited; zero price raises demand, causing queues/waiting lists – rationing by time instead of price; government must choose how much to spend, giving up other services (opportunity cost); quality may fall if funding is short; some people still pay privately to avoid waits. Levels: 1–3 limited; 4–6 both sides; 7–8 plus a justified conclusion.
