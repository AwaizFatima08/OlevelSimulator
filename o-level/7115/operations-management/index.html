Q1Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

Golden Crust is a bakery in Hyderabad that makes bread and cakes.

  1. (a) Define 'fixed costs'. [2]
  2. (b) Identify two variable costs for Golden Crust. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run, e.g. rent.
    1 mark: do not vary with output; 1 mark: short run / example such as rent, salaries, insurance (must be linked).
  2. (b) Any two: flour, sugar, eggs, butter, packaging, electricity/gas for ovens (if per batch), wages paid per item.
    1 mark each. Do not accept rent or managers' salaries.
Q2Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

Kite Masters makes kites for the spring festival season. Fixed costs are $18,000 per season. Each kite sells for $12 and has variable costs of $7. It expects to sell 5,000 kites.

  1. (a) Calculate the break-even level of output. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the margin of safety. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3,600 kites
    Contribution per kite = 12 − 7 = $5 (M1). Break-even = 18,000 ÷ 5 = 3,600 kites (A1).
  2. (b) 1,400 kites
    Margin of safety = expected sales − break-even output (M1) = 5,000 − 3,600 = 1,400 kites (A1).
Q3Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Core · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Coastal Nets makes fishing nets in Gwadar. Its fixed costs are $50,000 a year and its variable cost is $20 per net. It makes 5,000 nets a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the average cost per net. [2]
  2. (b) Outline two economies of scale Coastal Nets could gain if it grew larger. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) $30
    Total cost = 50,000 + 20 × 5,000 = $150,000 (M1). Average cost = 150,000 ÷ 5,000 = $30 (A1).
  2. (b) 1. Purchasing economies: buying nylon thread in bulk gives discounts, lowering cost per net. 2. Technical economies: it can afford faster net-making machines that produce more nets per hour. (Also: financial economies – cheaper loans; marketing economies – advertising cost spread over more nets; managerial economies – specialist managers.)
    For each of two economies: 1 mark identify + 1 mark outline applied to Coastal Nets.
Q4Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Core · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

Oak & Pine makes wooden chairs in Nairobi, Kenya.

  1. (a) State whether each of these costs is fixed or variable: (i) rent of the workshop, (ii) wood for each chair, (iii) insurance, (iv) paint for each chair. [2]
  2. (b) Monthly fixed costs are $15,000 and the variable cost is $35 per chair. Calculate the total cost of making 600 chairs in a month. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) fixed (ii) variable (iii) fixed (iv) variable
    2 marks for all four correct; 1 mark for two or three correct.
  2. (b) $36,000
    Total variable cost = 35 × 600 = $21,000 (M1). Total cost = 15,000 + 21,000 = $36,000 (A1).
Q5Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Standard · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Desert Rose makes scented candles in Bahawalpur. Each candle sells for Rs 450 and has variable costs of Rs 270. Fixed costs are Rs 900,000 a year. Next year it expects to sell 7,000 candles.

  1. (a) Calculate the expected profit next year. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the break-even level of output. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one limitation of break-even analysis for Desert Rose. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 360,000
    Total contribution = (450 − 270) × 7,000 = Rs 1,260,000 (M1). Profit = 1,260,000 − 900,000 = Rs 360,000 (A1).
  2. (b) 5,000 candles
    900,000 ÷ (450 − 270) = 900,000 ÷ 180 (M1) = 5,000 candles (A1).
  3. (c) It assumes all candles made are sold at the same price, but Desert Rose may have unsold candles or give discounts, so actual break-even could be higher.
    1 mark: limitation (assumes all output sold / constant price / costs stay constant / straight-line costs / only as good as forecast data). 1 mark: explanation applied to Desert Rose.
Q6Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Standard · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

Threadline Ltd is a garment exporter in Faisalabad. In three years it has grown from 80 to 900 workers and opened two more factories.

  1. (a) Explain two diseconomies of scale Threadline Ltd might experience. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 1. Poor communication: with 900 workers across three factories, messages pass through many levels and arrive late or distorted, so mistakes in orders for foreign buyers increase and costs rise. 2. Low worker motivation: workers feel unimportant in a large firm and managers rarely see them, so absenteeism and labour turnover rise and productivity falls. (Also: weak coordination between factories / slow decision-making.)
    For each: 1 mark identify, 1 mark application to Threadline, 1 mark development showing average costs rise.
Q7Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Standard · Paper 2 style · 12 marks

Sun Shade makes garden umbrellas. It sells each umbrella for $80; variable cost is $50 per umbrella and fixed costs are $120,000 a year. It expects to sell 6,000 umbrellas. The manager is considering raising the price to $90, which market research suggests would reduce sales to 5,200 umbrellas.

  1. (a) Should Sun Shade raise its price to $90? Justify your answer using suitable calculations (for example, the break-even output and expected profit at each price). [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Calculations: At $80: break-even 4,000, profit $60,000. At $90: break-even 3,000, profit $88,000. Yes, probably: profit rises from $60,000 to $88,000, break-even falls from 4,000 to 3,000 and the margin of safety rises from 2,000 to 2,200. But the forecast fall in sales may be wrong; if rivals keep lower prices, sales could fall more, and lower sales could cost market share in the long term. Judgement: raise the price if the research is reliable, and monitor sales.
    Calculations: $80: 120,000 ÷ 30 = 4,000; 30 × 6,000 − 120,000 = $60,000. $90: 120,000 ÷ 40 = 3,000; 40 × 5,200 − 120,000 = $88,000. Content: $80: break-even 4,000, profit $60,000, margin of safety 2,000; $90: break-even 3,000, profit $88,000, margin of safety 2,200; reliability of the market-research forecast; rivals' prices; market share in the long term. Mark bands (12; knowledge 2, application 2, analysis 4, evaluation 4): Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis of both sides / all options applied to Sun Shade, using the data, and a justified decision that explains why the rejected option(s) are weaker. Level 2 (5–8): advantages and disadvantages analysed and applied to Sun Shade, with a decision that is only partly justified or one-sided. Level 1 (1–4): relevant knowledge with little application or analysis; a decision with no reasoning earns no evaluation marks. Correct calculations count towards application and analysis; follow through an earlier calculation error.
Q8Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Standard · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

FreshMart has grown from one supermarket to a chain of 40 supermarkets across Pakistan. In its central bakery, output rose from 10,000 loaves a week (total cost Rs 400,000) to 25,000 loaves a week (total cost Rs 750,000).

  1. (a) Define 'economies of scale'. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the average cost per loaf before and after the increase in output. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why FreshMart may be able to charge lower prices than a small corner shop. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The factors that cause average (unit) costs to fall as a business increases its scale of production.
    1 mark: average/unit costs fall; 1 mark: as output/size of the business increases.
  2. (b) Before Rs 40; after Rs 30
    400,000 ÷ 10,000 = Rs 40 (1); 750,000 ÷ 25,000 = Rs 30 (1).
  3. (c) FreshMart buys in bulk for 40 stores, so suppliers give large discounts (purchasing economies); it can borrow more cheaply and spread advertising over many stores. Lower average costs mean it can charge lower prices and still make a profit, whereas a corner shop buys in small quantities at higher unit cost.
    1 mark per relevant economy identified (up to 2) + up to 2 marks for explaining the link from lower average cost to lower prices, with application.
Q9Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 12 marks

Pure Pour bottles mineral water in Murree and sells each case for $25. It must choose a new bottling machine. Machine A: fixed costs $200,000 a year, variable cost $15 per case. Machine B (more automated): fixed costs $320,000 a year, variable cost $9 per case. Expected sales are 30,000 cases a year, but sales vary a lot with the weather.

  1. (a) Recommend which bottling machine Pure Pour should buy. Justify your answer using suitable calculations (for example, the break-even output and expected annual profit for each machine). [12]
Show answer
  1. (a) Calculations: A: break-even 20,000 cases, profit $100,000. B: break-even 20,000 cases, profit $160,000. Both break even at 20,000 cases, but above that B earns $16 per case instead of $10, so at 30,000 cases B gives $60,000 more profit. Risk: below 20,000 cases B makes bigger losses (higher fixed costs), and sales depend on weather. Unless sales are likely to fall below 20,000, choose B; it may also need fewer workers, though breakdowns of an automated machine could stop production.
    Calculations: A: 200,000 ÷ (25 − 15) = 20,000; 10 × 30,000 − 200,000 = $100,000. B: 320,000 ÷ (25 − 9) = 20,000; 16 × 30,000 − 320,000 = $160,000. Content: both break even at 20,000 cases; profit A $100,000, B $160,000 at 30,000 cases; contribution $10 v $16 per case; B's higher fixed costs mean bigger losses below break-even; weather makes sales uncertain; automation — fewer workers, breakdown risk. Mark bands (12; knowledge 2, application 2, analysis 4, evaluation 4): Level 3 (9–12): developed analysis of both sides / all options applied to Pure Pour, using the data, and a justified decision that explains why the rejected option(s) are weaker. Level 2 (5–8): advantages and disadvantages analysed and applied to Pure Pour, with a decision that is only partly justified or one-sided. Level 1 (1–4): relevant knowledge with little application or analysis; a decision with no reasoning earns no evaluation marks. Q11Costs, scale of production and break-even analysis · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

Sara is opening a small café in Gilgit. Her weekly fixed costs are Rs 42,000. Each meal sells for Rs 600 and costs Rs 250 in ingredients and packaging.

  1. (a) Calculate how many meals Sara must sell each week to break even. [2]
  2. (b) Do you think a break-even chart would be useful to Sara when she opens her café? Justify your answer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 120 meals
    Contribution = 600 − 250 = Rs 350 (M1). 42,000 ÷ 350 = 120 meals (A1).
  2. (b) Useful: shows how many meals she needs to sell (120 a week) and the profit or loss at different sales levels; helps her see the effect of changing prices or costs, and supports a loan application.     Up to 2 marks knowledge, up to 2 marks analysis (developed consequences), up to 2 marks evaluation (justified judgement that weighs both sides for this business).

Q16Production of goods and services · Standard · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

Chenab Bicycles makes bicycle frames. In Year 1, 60 workers produced 48,000 frames. In Year 2, after new welding equipment was installed, 65 workers produced 54,600 frames.

  1. (a) Calculate labour productivity in each year. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage increase in labour productivity. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why higher productivity is important for Chenab Bicycles. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Year 1: 800 frames per worker; Year 2: 840 frames per worker
    48,000 ÷ 60 = 800 (1); 54,600 ÷ 65 = 840 (1).
  2. (b) 5%
    (840 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 (M1) = 5% (A1).

    1 mark: lower average/unit costs. 1 mark application. Up to 2 marks development: more competitive prices, higher profit, higher wages, faster delivery to customers.

Q19Production of goods and services · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

Pak Packaging Ltd makes cardboard boxes. It is considering buying an automated cutting machine costing $500,000. The machine would reduce the number of production workers from 50 to 30. Each worker is paid $12,000 a year. The factory is the main employer in a small town.

  1. (a) Calculate the annual saving in wages and the number of years of wage savings needed to cover the cost of the machine. Give the number of years to 1 decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Do you think Pak Packaging Ltd should buy the automated machine? Justify your answer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Saving $240,000 a year; 2.1 years
    (50 − 30) × 12,000 = $240,000 (1). 500,000 ÷ 240,000 = 2.1 years (1).
  2. (b) For: wage costs fall by $240,000 a year, so the machine pays for itself in about 2.1 years; cutting is faster and more accurate, so quality and output improve. Against: 20 workers lose their jobs in a town where the firm is the main employer – bad publicity, lower morale among remaining workers, possible union action; redundancy pay; machine needs maintenance and skilled operators. Judgement: buy it, since the payback is short, but reduce the impact by retraining some workers to operate and maintain the machine or by natural wastage.
    Up to 2 marks knowledge, up to 2 marks analysis (developed consequences), up to 2 marks evaluation (justified judgement that weighs both sides for this business).
Q20Production of goods and services · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

Al-Noor Pharmacy controls its inventory of a common painkiller. The maximum inventory level is 900 packs, the reorder level is 400 packs and the buffer inventory is 200 packs. It sells 100 packs a day.

  1. (a) Calculate the lead time (the number of days between placing an order and receiving it) if the order arrives just as inventory falls to the buffer level. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate how many packs should be ordered each time so that inventory returns to the maximum level when the order arrives. [2]
  3. (c) Explain one problem for Al-Noor Pharmacy of holding too much inventory and one problem of holding too little. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2 days
    Inventory used during lead time = 400 − 200 = 200 packs (M1). 200 ÷ 100 = 2 days (A1).
  2. (b) 700 packs
    Order quantity = maximum − buffer (M1) = 900 − 200 = 700 packs (A1).
  3. (c) Too much: cash is tied up and medicines may pass their expiry date and have to be thrown away; storage costs rise. Too little: the pharmacy may run out, so patients go to another pharmacy and it loses sales and goodwill.
    Too much: 1 identify + 1 explain (opportunity cost of cash, expiry/waste, storage, theft). Too little: 1 identify + 1 explain (stock-outs, lost sales/customers, cannot meet sudden demand).

  1. (a) Define 'kaizen'. [2]
  2. (b) Do you think kaizen will improve the efficiency of the hospital pharmacy? Justify your answer. [6]

Two businesses are choosing where to locate: a clothing shop and a brick-making factory.

  1. (a) Identify two factors that affect the location of the clothing shop. [2]
  2. (b) Outline two factors that affect the location of the brick-making factory. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two: footfall/number of customers passing; nearness to competitors or other shops; rent; parking/transport access; security; visibility.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) 1. Nearness to raw materials: clay is heavy and costly to transport, so a site near clay deposits cuts costs. 2. Availability and cost of land: brick kilns need large areas and are best away from homes because of smoke. (Also: transport links to building sites; supply of labour; planning rules; energy supply.)

Q26Location decisions · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

Hamza Garments makes 25,000 shirts a month. 3.2% of shirts are faulty and must be thrown away; each faulty shirt costs Rs 650 to make. A quality-training course for all workers costs Rs 250,000. The training company says it will reduce the fault rate to 1.4%.

Show answer

Q30Achieving quality production · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 8 marks

MediLab Pharmaceuticals makes tablets. At present inspectors test samples at the end of production. Last week 4,800 boxes were made and 120 boxes were rejected.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of boxes rejected. [2]
  2. (b) Explain the difference between quality control and quality assurance. Do you think MediLab should change to quality assurance? Justify your answer. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2.5%
    120 ÷ 4,800 × 100 (M1) = 2.5% (A1).
  2. (b) Quality control: inspectors check finished products (or samples) at the end and remove faulty ones. Quality assurance: every worker checks quality at each stage, preventing faults. For MediLab, faulty medicines are dangerous; 2.5% rejects is costly waste, and sampling may miss faults that reach patients. QA cuts waste and risk, but needs training and time. Judgement: yes, because for medicines safety and reputation are critical; keep some final testing too.
    Up to 2 marks: correct difference (end inspection v at every stage/prevention). Up to 2 marks: analysis applied to medicines and the reject rate. Up to 2 marks: justified judgement.
