Q1Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe Bhutto's policy of nationalisation between 1972 and 1976. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto introduced land reforms in 1972. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) In January 1972 the government took over about 30 large firms in basic industries such as iron and steel, heavy engineering, chemicals, cement and public utilities; life insurance companies were nationalised in 1972; banks were nationalised in 1974; in 1976 cotton-ginning, rice-husking and flour mills were taken over; a Board of Industrial Management was set up to run the units.
    1 mark per accurate point up to 4: about 30 large units / ten basic industries (1972); named industries (steel, engineering, chemicals, cement, utilities); insurance 1972; banks 1974; ginning, rice-husking and flour mills 1976; Board of Industrial Management; owners given limited compensation.
  2. (b) Bhutto's PPP had promised 'roti, kapra aur makaan' and needed to keep the support of poor peasants who voted for it in 1970; a small number of landlords owned huge estates and held great political power, which Bhutto wanted to reduce; lowering the ceiling (to 150 acres irrigated / 300 unirrigated) was meant to release land for landless tenants; giving tenants security against eviction and making landlords pay water rates and seed costs aimed to raise farm output and reduce rural poverty.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: election promise / Islamic socialism; reduce power of big landlords (feudals); give land to landless peasants; tenants' rights against eviction; raise agricultural productivity; reduce rural inequality.
Q2Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the Balochistan crisis of 1973–1977. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why many Baloch leaders rejected the policies of the central government between 1972 and 1977. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) In February 1973 Bhutto dismissed the elected NAP–JUI provincial government in Balochistan and imposed governor's rule. Baloch nationalists took up armed resistance in the mountains, and the army ran a major military operation until 1977. Thousands died on both sides.
    1 mark each, up to 4: NAP–JUI provincial government dismissed (Feb 1973); governor's rule imposed; armed Baloch nationalist resistance; large army operation (1973–77); heavy casualties or civilian suffering; foreign support for the rebels/government named correctly (optional).
  2. (b) The dismissal of an elected provincial government convinced many Baloch leaders that the centre would not respect provincial autonomy. They had demanded control of Balochistan's natural resources and a fair share of revenue, but the centre resisted. Arrests of nationalist leaders and the use of the army made many Baloch feel their culture and politics were being suppressed, so armed resistance seemed the only option to them.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements such as "they wanted independence". L2 (3–4): reasons named, not explained. L3 (5–7): one reason explained in depth for 5, two or more explained for 7. Reasons: dismissal of the NAP–JUI government and autonomy; control of resources and revenue; arrests of leaders and military repression; cultural/linguistic identity; the army's presence.
Q3Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Core · Paper 1 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) State three ways in which the 1972 nationalisation of private schools and colleges changed education in Pakistan. [3]
  2. (b) Suggest one reason why some parents and private owners were unhappy with this change. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Private educational institutions were taken over by the state. Management passed from private owners to government control. Teachers and staff became government employees.
    1 mark each, any three: private schools and colleges taken over by government; management moved from private owners to the state; teachers became government employees/salaries and conditions set by the state; admission and fee policies changed; schools brought under state curriculum control.
  2. (b) Parents who had chosen private schools for better standards lost that choice, and owners lost their property and income. Some feared that standards would fall once government took over.
    1 mark for a reason, 1 for development: loss of choice/private schools seen as better; owners lost property and income; fear that quality would fall under government management; loss of control over curriculum or fees.
Q4Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Core · Paper 1 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) State two causes of the oil price rises that affected Pakistan's economy in 1973–1974. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two effects of the higher oil prices on Pakistan's economy. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) The Arab–Israeli war of 1973 led oil-producing countries to cut supply and raise prices. The world oil price rose sharply in 1973–1974.
    1 mark each, any two: oil-exporting countries (OPEC) cut production/embargo after the 1973 Arab–Israeli war; OPEC raised prices sharply; increased demand for oil from industrialised countries.
  2. (b) Pakistan imported most of its oil, so the bill for imports rose sharply, which increased the balance of payments deficit. Higher fuel and transport costs also pushed up prices, so inflation rose and many people struggled to afford basic goods.
    2 marks per effect (1 for the effect, 1 for development), up to 6: higher import bill / balance of payments deficit; inflation and higher transport costs; pressure on foreign reserves; rising cost of living for the poor; higher costs for industry and agriculture.
Q5Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main features of the 1973 Constitution. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto was removed from power in July 1977. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pakistan became the Islamic Republic of Pakistan with Islam as the state religion; it set up a federal parliamentary system with the Prime Minister as head of government and the President as a largely ceremonial head of state; Parliament had two houses, the National Assembly and the Senate; the President and Prime Minister had to be Muslims; it listed fundamental rights and an independent judiciary; Urdu was the national language.
    1 mark per point up to 4: Islamic Republic / Islam the state religion; federal; parliamentary – PM holds power; President ceremonial; bicameral (National Assembly and Senate); PM and President Muslim; Council of Islamic Ideology; fundamental rights; Urdu national language, English official for a time; passed April 1973, in force 14 August 1973.
  2. (b) The opposition parties, united in the Pakistan National Alliance (PNA), accused the PPP of rigging the March 1977 elections, which it won by a very large margin; mass protests and strikes followed and many people were killed, so the country became hard to govern; Bhutto used the army and the Federal Security Force against protesters, which made him look authoritarian; rising prices and the failure of nationalisation had lost him support among industrialists and the middle class; talks with the PNA dragged on, and on 5 July 1977 General Zia-ul-Haq took over in 'Operation Fair Play', saying he would restore order and hold elections.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: alleged rigging of 1977 elections; PNA movement / strikes and violence; harsh use of FSF and army; economic problems (inflation, nationalisation); loss of support of religious groups and businessmen; army intervention, Operation Fair Play.
Q6Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the Simla Agreement of 1972. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto's industrial reforms did not achieve all that he hoped. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) It was signed in July 1972 by Bhutto and the Indian Prime Minister Indira Gandhi after the 1971 war; both sides agreed to settle their disputes peacefully through bilateral talks; troops were to withdraw to their own side of the international border and India gave back the West Pakistan territory it had occupied; in Kashmir both agreed to respect the new Line of Control; the return of prisoners of war was left to later talks (they came home by 1974).
    1 mark per point up to 4: July 1972, Simla; Bhutto and Indira Gandhi; disputes to be settled peacefully and bilaterally; withdrawal to international border / territory returned; Line of Control in Kashmir respected; POW issue settled later; restore communications and trade.
  2. (b) Many nationalised units were run by civil servants and political appointees rather than experienced managers, so efficiency fell and many made losses; private investors lost confidence and moved their money abroad or stopped investing, so industrial growth slowed; the take-over of small ginning and rice mills in 1976 upset small businessmen who had supported the PPP; the oil price rise of 1973–74 and floods raised costs and inflation, which hid any gains from the reforms; labour laws raised wages, but strikes and low productivity continued.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: poor management / political appointments; loss of investor confidence / capital flight; small businesses angered (1976); world oil crisis and inflation; labour unrest; corruption.
Q7Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the Second Amendment to the Constitution passed in 1974 concerning the Ahmadiyya community. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto's government agreed to the Second Amendment in 1974. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) In September 1974 the National Assembly passed the Second Amendment, which declared persons of the Ahmadiyya community non-Muslims for the purposes of the law. It was passed after street protests and demands from religious parties.
    1 mark each, up to 4: passed by the National Assembly in 1974 (Second Amendment); Ahmadis declared non-Muslim for legal purposes; passed after protests/agitation by religious groups; the decision was a constitutional change, not a religious ruling.
  2. (b) Bhutto needed the support of religious parties after the 1973 constitution was passed, and the agitation in 1974 threatened law and order. Bhutto also wanted to strengthen his own Islamic credentials, particularly after the 1974 Islamic Summit in Lahore. By passing the amendment within parliament, he hoped to end the public disorder and reduce pressure from the religious right.
    Level marking. L1: general answer. L2: reasons named. L3 (5–7): at least two reasons explained, e.g. pressure from religious parties and street protests; wish for Islamic legitimacy and political support; desire to restore law and order; use of parliament to settle a controversy. Accept any well-explained reason.
Q8Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the nuclear programme that Bhutto started in 1972. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto believed Pakistan needed nuclear capability. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) In January 1972 Bhutto called a meeting in Multan of scientists and officials that launched a nuclear programme. An Atomic Energy Commission was used to co-ordinate work, and an agreement with France in 1976 was made for a reprocessing plant.
    1 mark each, up to 4: meeting at Multan (January 1972) launching the programme; work co-ordinated through the Atomic Energy Commission; attempts to acquire nuclear technology from abroad; agreement with France (1976) for reprocessing; secret pursuit of enrichment.
  2. (b) Pakistan had lost East Pakistan in 1971 and needed to deter a larger Indian army. India's nuclear test in May 1974 confirmed the threat for Pakistan. Bhutto also argued that nuclear capability would bring prestige and help Pakistan keep its independence in foreign affairs. Many Pakistanis supported the programme on security grounds.
    Level marking. L1–2: general statements. L3 (5–7): explains at least two reasons: deterrence of a stronger India after 1971; India's nuclear test (1974) as a threat; national security and prestige; independence in foreign policy; public support for security.
Q9Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the results of the general election of March 1977 and the reaction of the opposition. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the Pakistan National Alliance (PNA) refused to accept the 1977 results. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) The Pakistan People's Party won a large majority of seats in the National Assembly. The opposition alliance, the Pakistan National Alliance (PNA), a group of nine parties, rejected the results and claimed the election had been rigged. The PNA led protests and strikes across the country.
    1 mark each, up to 4: PPP won a large majority in March 1977; nine-party Pakistan National Alliance (PNA) formed to oppose it; PNA rejected the results and alleged rigging; mass protests, strikes and demonstrations followed.
  2. (b) The PNA believed that officials had rigged the vote in favour of the PPP, and there were reports of unopposed candidates and of violence at polling stations. It also blamed Bhutto's policies, such as high prices and the repression of opponents, for losing public trust. The PNA demanded fresh elections and a new government, and it used street protests to press these demands.
    Level marking. L1–2: general. L3 (5–7): two reasons explained, e.g. belief in rigging supported by unopposed seats and violence at polls; loss of trust over the economy and repression; political strategy of street agitation; demand for fresh elections. Accept reasoned alternatives.
Q10Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main aims of the Pakistan People's Party's manifesto of 1970, including the slogan "Roti, Kapra aur Makan". [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the PPP's message appealed to many voters in the 1970 election. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) The PPP promised food, clothing and housing for ordinary people, shown in the slogan "Roti, Kapra aur Makan". It also promised socialist-style policies, land reform and the redistribution of wealth, and presented itself as the party of the poor and the workers.
    1 mark each, up to 4: food (roti), clothing (kapra), housing (makan) for the common people; land reform and redistribution; socialist-style economic policy; promise to help workers and peasants; a strong anti-feudal message.
  2. (b) Many ordinary people, especially in Punjab and Sindh, were poor and had little land. The PPP promised to end feudal domination and improve their lives, which offered them hope of change. The slogan was simple and easy to remember, and Bhutto's charisma and his attacks on the old landlords made the party popular.
    Level marking. L3 (5–7): two reasons explained, e.g. poverty and hope of land reform; anti-feudal message; simple slogan and clear promises; Bhutto's personal appeal and campaign; support from workers and peasants in Punjab and Sindh.
Q11Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 18 marks
  1. (a) Describe Bhutto's policy towards the Muslim world between 1972 and 1977. [4]
  2. (b) 'Bhutto's domestic policies between 1971 and 1977 were successful.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer. [14]
Show answer
  1. (a) He hosted the second Islamic Summit Conference of the OIC at Lahore in February 1974, attended by leaders such as King Faisal and Colonel Gaddafi; at the summit Pakistan recognised Bangladesh; he built close links with Saudi Arabia, Libya, Iran and the Gulf states, which gave loans and aid; thousands of Pakistani workers went to the Gulf states, sending back remittances.
    1 mark per point up to 4: Lahore Islamic Summit 1974; named leaders; recognition of Bangladesh at the summit; financial help from oil-rich states; jobs for Pakistani workers in the Middle East / remittances; support for Arab states over Palestine / 1973 war.
  2. (b) Agree: the 1973 Constitution was passed with the support of all parties and is still the basis of government; land reforms and labour laws gave some help to tenants and workers; health reforms (rural health centres) and education reforms (free schooling to class 8, new schools, Allama Iqbal Open University 1974) widened opportunities; the Qadiani issue was settled to the satisfaction of religious groups. Disagree: nationalisation made industry inefficient and scared off investors; land reforms were evaded by landlords who transferred land to relatives; the Federal Security Force was used against opponents; the military action in Balochistan (1973–77) caused resentment; the 1977 election rigging allegations led to his fall. Judgement: e.g. the constitution was a lasting success, but economic and political methods weakened his government.
    Level marking (14 marks). L1 (1–2): general statements with no relevant detail. L2 (3–6): identifies or describes relevant factors without explaining them. L3 (7–10): explains factors on one side only (7–8 for one explained point, 9–10 for two or more). L4 (9–13): explains factors on both sides (more developed points and accurate detail move the answer up the band). L5 (14): as L4, plus a supported judgement that answers 'how far' (or 'was it the most important'). Content points (credit any relevant, accurate point; each needs explaining, not just stating) — Successes: 1973 Constitution; social reforms (health, education, AIOU); labour reforms; nuclear programme begun. Failures: nationalisation; weak land reform; FSF / authoritarian rule; Balochistan; 1977 elections. A judgement must weigh the stated factor against the others, not just repeat the question.
Q12Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 18 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main features of the Balochistan and North-West Frontier policies of Bhutto's government between 1972 and 1977. [4]
  2. (b) "Bhutto's government did more to weaken national unity than to strengthen it between 1972 and 1977." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. [14]
Show answer
  1. (a) In both provinces Bhutto moved against opposition governments of the NAP and JUI. In Balochistan the government was dismissed and military force used; in the NWFP the provincial government was dismissed in 1975. Opposition leaders were arrested and charged with treason.
    1 mark each, up to 4: dismissal of NAP–JUI government in Balochistan (1973); military operation in Balochistan; dismissal of NWFP government (1975); arrests and charges of treason against opposition leaders; ban or pressure on opposition parties.
  2. (b) Level-marked: any well-supported balanced answer that reaches a clear judgement. See working for content.
    Level marking. Candidates may agree or disagree but must support a judgement. Points that weaken unity: dismissal of provincial governments; military operation in Balochistan; arrests and repression; ban on NAP. Points that strengthen unity: 1973 Constitution agreed by all parties, the federal system, the 1972 Land Reforms, and national consensus over the Simla Agreement and Bangladesh recognition. Level 4–5 (11–14): balanced, supported, and a clear judgement on which side outweighs the other.
Q13Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 10 marks
  1. (a) State three ways in which Bhutto changed the role of the army in politics after 1971. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto's decision to appoint Zia-ul-Haq as Chief of Army Staff in 1976 was later seen as a serious mistake. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Bhutto removed some senior officers after 1971 and reduced the army's control of politics. He also brought the army under civilian control, but later appointed General Zia-ul-Haq as Chief of Army Staff over more senior officers.
    1 mark each, any three: senior officers removed or retired after 1971; army placed under civilian/government control in theory; creation of a new army chief appointment process; appointment of Zia over seven more senior generals (1976); use of the army in Balochistan and against opponents.
  2. (b) Zia was chosen over seven more senior generals, which was expected to keep him loyal. Instead, Zia later overthrew Bhutto in July 1977 and took over the government. The choice showed that Bhutto had given a military officer the power to remove him.
    Level marking. L3 (5–7): explains at least one link: Zia superseded seven senior generals so his promotion was seen as an insult to others; the army's chain of command gave Zia control of the coup; Bhutto had given him the power to remove him. Accept reasonable hindsight explanations.
Q14Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 18 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main steps in the removal of Bhutto's government in July 1977. [4]
  2. (b) "The 1977 general election was the main reason for Bhutto's downfall." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. [14]
Show answer
  1. (a) After protests and talks with the PNA, General Zia-ul-Haq took power in a coup on 5 July 1977, declared martial law and detained Bhutto. Bhutto was later tried and hanged in April 1979.

Points that disagree: Bhutto's authoritarian style, economic difficulties, loss of support from the army and the judiciary, and the Balochistan operation had weakened him long before 1977; the election was a trigger, not the cause. Level 4–5 (11–14): balanced, supported, with a clear judgement.
Q15Zulfikar Ali Bhutto 1971–1977 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main features of the Labour Policy of 1972. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Bhutto's labour reforms did not satisfy all industrial workers by 1977. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) The Labour Policy of 1972 gave workers the right to take part in the management of industrial units through representation on management committees. It also strengthened the rights of trade unions and protected workers against unfair dismissal.
    1 mark each, up to 4: workers' representation in the management of industrial units; strengthened trade union rights; protection against arbitrary dismissal; improvements in workers' welfare/pay for industrial workers.
  2. (b) Workers wanted higher wages and more job security, but inflation after 1973 reduced the value of wages. Some employers and managers resisted the reforms and found ways around them. Strikes and disputes continued, and workers felt that the reforms were applied unevenly.
    Level marking. L3 (5–7): two reasons explained: inflation and falling real wages; resistance from employers and managers; uneven application of the reforms; continued strikes; limited coverage for rural and informal workers.
Q16Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the problems faced by refugees arriving in Pakistan in 1947. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the canal water dispute was a serious problem for Pakistan between 1947 and 1960. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Millions of Muslim refugees (about seven million) crossed into Pakistan, many on foot or in packed trains; trains and columns were attacked and many people were killed; they arrived with few possessions and needed food, shelter and medical care; camps such as the Walton camp in Lahore were overcrowded and disease spread; many had no jobs or land, so resettling them was slow.
    1 mark per point up to 4: about seven million refugees; violence / attacks on trains and caravans; arrived with nothing; lack of shelter / camps (e.g. Walton, Lahore); disease and lack of medicine; unemployment / need for land and housing; Jinnah's Relief Fund.
  2. (b) The Radcliffe Award left the headworks at Ferozepur and Madhopur in India although the canals they fed irrigated land in Pakistan's Punjab; in April 1948 India cut off the water, threatening crops at sowing time and the farm economy on which most Pakistanis depended; Pakistan feared India could do this whenever it chose, which added to bitterness between the two countries; a 1948 temporary agreement made Pakistan pay for water, which it saw as unfair; the dispute was only settled by the Indus Waters Treaty in 1960 through the World Bank, which gave the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: headworks in India; water stopped April 1948; agriculture / food supply threatened; insecurity – India controlled supply; payment demanded; long dispute until 1960 treaty.
Q17Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main points of Jinnah's speech to the Constituent Assembly on 11 August 1947. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Jinnah's speech of 11 August 1947 was important for the new state. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Jinnah told the Constituent Assembly that the state would not concern itself with the religion of citizens. He said that all citizens were equal, whatever their religion, caste or creed, and that they should work together for the good of Pakistan.
    1 mark each, up to 4: citizens free to practise any religion; religion not the business of the state; all citizens equal regardless of religion, caste or creed; minorities to be treated fairly; state to be governed by law and co-operation.
  2. (b) The speech was a reassurance to the non-Muslim minorities who feared discrimination after Partition. It showed the new state would be a democratic country based on law rather than a religious state. Some Muslim religious leaders disagreed with the message, so the speech also raised questions about the nature of Pakistan that would be debated for decades.
    Level marking. L3 (5–7): two reasons explained: reassurance to minorities after Partition; the vision of a secular/civil state of equal citizens; the speech was the founding statement of the nation; it created debate over the state's religious identity. Accept any reasoned interpretation.
Q18Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the role of the civil service (bureaucracy) in Pakistan in the years 1947–1951. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the civil service grew more powerful in Pakistan in the 1950s. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) The civil service was the most experienced part of the new government, because many senior officials were trained under British rule. Civil servants ran the districts, managed supplies of refugees and provided the administrative machinery for the government of the country.
    1 mark each, up to 4: senior civil servants ran most of the government; experience from British India; district administration and law and order; managing refugee relief and resettlement; administration of the new capital and ministries.
  2. (b) Politicians were weak and divided, and the Muslim League had few trained organisers outside the Punjab and Sindh.
