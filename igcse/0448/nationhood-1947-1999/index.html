Q1Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the problems faced by refugees arriving in Pakistan in 1947. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the canal water dispute was a serious problem for Pakistan between 1947 and 1960. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Millions of Muslim refugees (about seven million) crossed into Pakistan, many on foot or in packed trains; trains and columns were attacked and many people were killed; they arrived with few possessions and needed food, shelter and medical care; camps such as the Walton camp in Lahore were overcrowded and disease spread; many had no jobs or land, so resettling them was slow.
    1 mark per point up to 4: about seven million refugees; violence / attacks on trains and caravans; arrived with nothing; lack of shelter / camps (e.g. Walton, Lahore); disease and lack of medicine; unemployment / need for land and housing; Jinnah's Relief Fund.
  2. (b) The Radcliffe Award left the headworks at Ferozepur and Madhopur in India although the canals they fed irrigated land in Pakistan's Punjab; in April 1948 India cut off the water, threatening crops at sowing time and the farm economy on which most Pakistanis depended; Pakistan feared India could do this whenever it chose, which added to bitterness between the two countries; a 1948 temporary agreement made Pakistan pay for water, which it saw as unfair; the dispute was only settled by the Indus Waters Treaty in 1960 through the World Bank, which gave the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: headworks in India; water stopped April 1948; agriculture / food supply threatened; insecurity – India controlled supply; payment demanded; long dispute until 1960 treaty.
Q2Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the problems Pakistan faced over the division of assets in 1947. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Pakistan suffered from political instability between 1951 and 1958. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pakistan was to receive Rs 750 million of the cash balances of British India, but India paid only Rs 200 million at first and held back the rest for months, linking it to the Kashmir dispute; Pakistan was to receive about one-third of the military stores and equipment, but much of it never arrived; the new government in Karachi had to work in temporary offices without enough furniture, typewriters, stationery or files; Pakistan received no major arms factories or mints, which were in Indian territory.
    1 mark per accurate point, up to 4: Rs 750 million share; only Rs 200 million paid at first; remainder delayed (paid only in part after Gandhi's fast, January 1948); military stores not delivered; no ordnance factories; shortage of office equipment and files in Karachi; trains carrying assets attacked or delayed.
  2. (b) After the deaths of Jinnah (1948) and Liaquat Ali Khan (assassinated in 1951), there was no leader with national authority, and the Muslim League split into rival groups; Governors-General and Presidents such as Ghulam Muhammad and Iskander Mirza used their powers to dismiss governments, for example Nazimuddin in 1953 and the Constituent Assembly in 1954, so six prime ministers held office between 1951 and 1958; rivalry between East and West Pakistan, and between the provinces of the west, made agreement on a constitution and on sharing power very difficult; the bureaucracy and the army grew stronger while politicians lost public respect, which led Mirza to abolish the 1956 Constitution and declare martial law in October 1958.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (3–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (5–7): explains reasons, 5–6 for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons to credit: loss of Jinnah and Liaquat; weak and divided Muslim League; interference by Ghulam Muhammad and Iskander Mirza (dismissals of 1953 and 1954); frequent changes of prime minister; East–West and provincial rivalries (language, One Unit, parity); economic problems and food shortages; growing influence of civil servants and the army; martial law 1958.
Q3Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the Objectives Resolution of 1949. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why it took Pakistan nine years to produce its first constitution. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) It was moved by Liaquat Ali Khan and passed by the Constituent Assembly in March 1949; it said sovereignty belongs to Allah and is exercised by the people through their elected representatives as a sacred trust; the state would follow the principles of democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice as laid down by Islam; Muslims would be able to live according to Islam; minorities would be free to profess and practise their religions; Pakistan would be a federation with fundamental rights and an independent judiciary.
    1 mark per point up to 4: March 1949; Liaquat Ali Khan; sovereignty of Allah; authority exercised by elected representatives; Islamic principles of democracy, equality, tolerance, justice; minority rights; federal system; fundamental rights; independent judiciary.
  2. (b) Politicians disagreed about the balance of power between East and West Pakistan, because East Pakistan had the larger population and wanted representation by population while the West feared domination; there was a dispute about the national language (Urdu or Bengali as well); there was disagreement about the place of Islam in the state; leading figures died early – Jinnah in 1948 and Liaquat assassinated in 1951 – leaving weak leadership; governors-general such as Ghulam Muhammad dismissed governments and dissolved the Constituent Assembly in 1954, delaying the work; provincial jealousies and frequent changes of Prime Minister made agreement hard.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: East–West representation (parity); language issue; role of Islam; deaths of Jinnah and Liaquat; dissolution of Constituent Assembly 1954 / bureaucratic interference; provincialism / weak parties.
Q4Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the main features of the 1956 Constitution. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why the One Unit scheme was introduced in 1955. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) It came into force on 23 March 1956 and named the country the Islamic Republic of Pakistan; the President had to be a Muslim; it set up a parliamentary system with a Prime Minister; there was a single-house National Assembly of 300 seats with parity, 150 each for East and West Pakistan; Urdu and Bengali were both national languages; Iskander Mirza became the first President.
    1 mark per point up to 4: 23 March 1956; Islamic Republic; President a Muslim; parliamentary / PM; unicameral National Assembly 300; parity between wings; Urdu and Bengali national languages; federal; Iskander Mirza President.
  2. (b) Merging the provinces and states of the west into a single province of West Pakistan created two units of equal standing, so parity could be used in the National Assembly and East Pakistan's larger population would not give it a permanent majority; the government argued it would reduce administrative costs by replacing several provincial governments with one; it was meant to reduce provincialism and build national unity; it made it easier to plan development such as irrigation for the whole western wing.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: create parity with East Pakistan; reduce the influence of the Bengali majority; save administrative cost / efficiency; reduce provincial rivalry / unity; easier development planning.
Q5Early years of Pakistan 1947–1958 · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 18 marks
  1. (a) Describe the events of the war over Kashmir in 1947–1948. [4]
  2. (b) 'Dealing with the refugee crisis was Jinnah's greatest achievement as Governor-General.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer. [14]
Show answer
  1. (a) Muslims in Poonch rebelled against the Maharaja Hari Singh, and Pashtun tribesmen entered Kashmir in October 1947 in support; the Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession to India and Indian troops were flown into Srinagar; fighting followed and the Pakistani army joined in 1948; India took the issue to the UN, which arranged a ceasefire from 1 January 1949 and called for a plebiscite, which was never held.
    1 mark per point up to 4: Poonch revolt; tribesmen enter, October 1947; Maharaja accedes to India; Indian troops airlifted to Srinagar; Pakistan army involved 1948; UN involvement; ceasefire 1 January 1949; ceasefire line / Azad Kashmir; plebiscite promised but not held.
  2. (b) Agree: he set up the Quaid-e-Azam Relief Fund, moved temporarily to Lahore to oversee the work, appealed for calm and visited camps, and millions of refugees were fed and slowly resettled. Disagree: other achievements were as important – he set up a working central government in Karachi from almost nothing, building a civil service; he founded the State Bank of Pakistan (1948) so Pakistan had its own currency; he negotiated with the princely states, bringing many (e.g. Bahawalpur, Khairpur, Kalat) into Pakistan; he dealt with the division of assets and the Kashmir war; he gave the nation a sense of unity in its first year. Judgement: e.g. setting up a working state was the greater achievement because without it refugees could not have been helped.

Q6Yahya Khan and the separation of East Pakistan 1969–1971 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe how Yahya Khan came to power in 1969. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why India's involvement was important in the separation of East Pakistan in 1971. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ayub Khan faced mass protests and strikes in both East and West Pakistan in 1968–69; he resigned in March 1969; instead of handing power to the Speaker of the National Assembly, as his own 1962 Constitution required, he handed it to the army Commander-in-Chief, General Yahya Khan; Yahya declared martial law, abolished the 1962 Constitution and made himself Chief Martial Law Administrator and President.
    1 mark per accurate point, up to 4: protests against Ayub 1968–69 (students, workers, opposition parties, Mujib's supporters); Ayub resigned 25 March 1969; power passed to General Yahya Khan, not to the Speaker; martial law declared; 1962 Constitution abrogated; Yahya became CMLA and President; promised elections.
  2. (b) About ten million refugees fled from East Pakistan to India, which India used to justify intervening and to win international sympathy; India trained, armed and sheltered the Mukti Bahini guerrillas, who weakened the Pakistan army in East Pakistan; the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Friendship (August 1971) gave India Soviet diplomatic and military backing, so it could act without fear of outside intervention; India invaded East Pakistan in late 1971 and full war broke out in December; East Pakistan was 1,600 km from the West and cut off, so Pakistani forces could not be reinforced and surrendered in Dhaka on 16 December 1971, with about 90,000 taken prisoner.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (3–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (5–7): explains reasons, 5–6 for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons to credit: refugee crisis used to justify action; support for the Mukti Bahini; Indo-Soviet Treaty August 1971; Indian invasion and December war; geographical isolation of East Pakistan; surrender at Dhaka 16 December 1971 and around 90,000 prisoners of war.
Q7Yahya Khan and the separation of East Pakistan 1969–1971 · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the results of the general election of December 1970. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why East Pakistan separated from Pakistan in 1971. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) It was Pakistan's first general election held on adult franchise (one person, one vote); the Awami League led by Mujib won 160 of the 162 general seats in East Pakistan, giving it an overall majority in the National Assembly; the PPP led by Bhutto won 81 of the 138 seats in West Pakistan; neither party won any seats in the other wing.
    1 mark per point up to 4: first election on adult franchise; Awami League 160 of 162 East Pakistan seats; overall majority; PPP 81 seats in the West; no party won seats in both wings; country divided along regional lines.
  2. (b) East Pakistanis felt economically exploited because more development money, industry and jobs went to the West although jute from the East earned much of the foreign exchange; Bengalis were under-represented in the army and civil service, so they felt they had little power; the Urdu–Bengali language dispute made Bengalis feel their culture was not respected; after the 1970 election the Awami League was not allowed to form a government, and the military crackdown of March 1971 turned demands for autonomy into a war for independence; India supported the Mukti Bahini and invaded in late 1971, so Pakistani forces surrendered at Dhaka on 16 December 1971.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: economic disparity; under-representation in army and civil service; language and culture; Six Points / Awami League; denial of power after 1970 election; military action 1971; Indian intervention; geographical distance.
Q8Yahya Khan and the separation of East Pakistan 1969–1971 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 18 marks

  2. (b) Was the Six Points programme the most important reason for the separation of East Pakistan? Explain your answer. [14]

Judgement: e.g. the Six Points expressed the demand, but the military crackdown and Indian intervention made separation happen.

Q14Relations with India, China, Afghanistan and Bangladesh · Core · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe Pakistan's relations with China between 1950 and 1972. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Pakistan's relations with Afghanistan were difficult between 1947 and 1979. [7]

Q34Language, education and culture since 1947 · Standard · Paper 1 style · 11 marks
  1. (a) Describe the language movement in East Pakistan between 1948 and 1956. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why Urdu was chosen as the national language of Pakistan. [7]
Show answer
  1. (a) In March 1948 Jinnah said in Dhaka that Urdu alone would be the state language, which upset Bengali speakers; in 1952 Prime Minister Khawaja Nazimuddin repeated this, and students protested; on 21 February 1952 police fired on protesting students in Dhaka and several were killed; the movement continued, and in 1954 the Constituent Assembly accepted Bengali; the 1956 Constitution made both Urdu and Bengali national languages.
    1 mark per point up to 4: Jinnah's 1948 speech; Bengali speakers' opposition; Nazimuddin 1952; student protests; 21 February 1952 shootings; martyrs remembered (Shaheed Minar); 1954 acceptance; 1956 Constitution – Urdu and Bengali.
  2. (b) Urdu had been the language of the Pakistan Movement and was associated with Muslim identity across the subcontinent, since it is written in the Persian–Arabic script and contains many Persian and Arabic words; it was understood in most parts of the country, so it could unite people who spoke different regional languages such as Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto and Balochi; it had a rich literature, including the poetry of Iqbal; Sir Syed and the Aligarh Movement had defended it during the Urdu–Hindi controversy, so it had become a symbol of Muslim rights; choosing a regional language would have favoured one province over the others.
    Level marking. L1 (1–2): general statements. L2 (2–4): identifies or describes reasons. L3 (4–7): explains reasons, 5 marks for one well-explained reason, 7 for two or more. Reasons: symbol of Muslim identity / Pakistan Movement; script and vocabulary; widely understood – unifying; rich literature (Iqbal, Ghalib); Urdu–Hindi controversy; neutral between provinces.
