You will hear a talk about the history of chocolate. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)Chocolate is made from the seeds of the cacao tree, which grows only in hot, wet regions near the equator. The seeds, often called beans, grow inside large pods that sprout directly from the tree's trunk.Some of the earliest people to use cacao lived in Central America thousands of years ago. The Maya and later the Aztecs made a drink from ground cacao beans mixed with water and chilli. It was bitter, not sweet, and was usually drunk by rulers and warriors. Cacao beans were so valuable that the Aztecs used them as money.Spanish explorers brought cacao to Europe in the sixteenth century. Europeans added sugar and later milk, and drinking chocolate became fashionable among the rich. Solid chocolate bars only appeared in the nineteenth century, when new machines made it possible to press cacao into a smooth, solid form.Today, most of the world's cacao is grown not in the Americas but in West Africa, especially Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Many cacao farmers earn very little, and some people choose to buy chocolate with a Fairtrade label, which aims to guarantee farmers a fairer price.

  6. (f) Solid bars made possible by new ______ [1]
  7. (g) Main growing region today: ______ [1]
  8. (h) Fairtrade aims to give farmers a fairer ______ [1]

Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)Camels are perfectly designed for life in the desert. Many people think a camel's hump is full of water, but in fact it stores fat. When food is scarce, the camel's body uses this fat for energy, and the hump becomes smaller and floppy.Camels can go for many days without drinking, partly because they lose very little water. They hardly sweat, even in great heat, and they produce dry droppings. When they do find water, they can drink an enormous amount very quickly.Their bodies are protected from sand in clever ways. They have two rows of long eyelashes, and they can close their nostrils during sandstorms. Their wide, soft feet spread out on the sand, so they don't sink.There are two main kinds of camel. The dromedary, found in South Asia, the Middle East and Africa, has one hump. The Bactrian camel, from the cold deserts of Central Asia, has two humps and grows a thick coat in winter.In Thar and Cholistan, camels are still used to carry goods and water, and their milk is an important food for desert families. In recent years, camel milk has also become popular in cities because it contains about three times as much vitamin C as cow's milk.

You will hear a talk about how to look after houseplants. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)Houseplants make rooms more attractive and can improve your mood, but many people kill them by mistake. The most common cause of death is not forgetting to water them, but watering too much. When soil stays wet all the time, the roots can't breathe and they begin to rot. A simple test is to push your finger into the soil: if the top two centimetres feel dry, it's time to water.Always use pots with holes in the bottom, so that extra water can drain away. Put a saucer underneath to protect your furniture.Light is the next thing to consider. Most houseplants come from tropical forests, where they grow in the shade of taller trees, so they prefer bright but indirect light. Strong afternoon sun through a window can burn their leaves.In winter, plants grow more slowly and need less water. Keep them away from heaters, which dry the air.If you're a beginner, start with an easy plant. The snake plant and the money plant are both very forgiving. They can survive in low light, and they won't die if you forget them for a couple of weeks.Finally, wipe dusty leaves with a damp cloth now and then. Dust blocks light and stops the plant from making food.

  6. (f) In winter, keep plants away from ______ [1]

Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)A volcano is an opening in the Earth's crust through which molten rock, gases and ash escape. Molten rock underground is called magma; once it reaches the surface, it's called lava.Most volcanoes are found where the Earth's tectonic plates meet. Around the Pacific Ocean there is a huge chain of volcanoes known as the Ring of Fire, where around three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes are located.Not all eruptions are the same. Some volcanoes, such as those in Hawaii, produce runny lava that flows gently, and people can often watch from a safe distance. Others are much more explosive. When magma is thick and full of gas, pressure builds up until the volcano blows apart, throwing ash high into the atmosphere.Ash is often more dangerous than lava. It can travel hundreds of kilometres, damage aircraft engines and collapse roofs under its weight. Fast-moving clouds of hot gas and ash, called pyroclastic flows, are the deadliest of all.Despite the dangers, millions of people live near volcanoes, because volcanic soil is extremely fertile. Volcanoes also provide geothermal energy: in countries like Iceland, heat from underground is used to produce electricity and warm homes.

You will hear a talk about the work of a sound engineer in films. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)When we watch a film, we rarely think about the sound. Yet much of what we hear wasn't recorded at the same time as the pictures. The dialogue is usually recorded on set, but noisy locations often make it unclear, so actors later go into a studio and repeat their lines while watching the film. This process is called dubbing, or ADR.Then there are sound effects. Many everyday noises, like footsteps, doors closing or clothes rustling, are created by specialists called Foley artists, named after a sound pioneer, Jack Foley. They work in a studio full of strange objects. For example, the sound of someone walking through snow is often made by squeezing a bag of cornflour. Breaking celery can sound like bones breaking — useful for action films!Background sound, known as 'ambience', is also important. A scene in a busy market needs distant voices, traffic and birds to feel real, even if the street was empty during filming.Finally, all these layers are combined in a process called mixing. The engineer decides how loud each sound should be, so that the dialogue remains clear and the music supports the emotion of the scene without drowning it out.

  5. (e) Breaking bones: break ______ [1]
  6. (f) Background sound is known as ______ [1]
  7. (g) Combining all layers is called ______ [1]
  8. (h) Music should support the ______ of the scene [1]

  2. (b) dubbing
  3. (c) Foley

Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)We usually think of laughter as a response to jokes, but researchers have found that most laughter has nothing to do with humour. Laughing at someone, rather than with them, can be a powerful way of showing who belongs to a group and who doesn't.

You will hear a talk about how vaccines work. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)To understand vaccines, we first need to understand the immune system. When a harmful microorganism, such as a virus or bacterium, enters the body, the immune system recognises molecules on its surface called antigens. It then produces antibodies, proteins that attach to the antigens and help destroy the invader.The first time this happens, the response is slow, which is why we become ill. But the body keeps special cells called memory cells. If the same microorganism returns, these cells recognise it and produce antibodies so quickly that we often don't become ill at all. This is called immunity.A vaccine allows the body to gain this immunity without having to suffer the disease. It contains antigens from a weakened or dead form of the microorganism, or, in some newer vaccines, instructions that tell our cells to make a harmless piece of it. The immune system responds as if it were a real infection and creates memory cells.Some vaccines need booster doses, because the memory gradually fades.Vaccines also protect people who can't be vaccinated themselves, such as newborn babies or people with weak immune systems. When enough people in a community are immune, the disease can't spread easily. This is called herd immunity. Thanks to vaccination, smallpox was completely eradicated, and polio has now disappeared from almost every country in the world.

  6. (f) Reason: memory gradually ______ [1]

You may have heard the figure of twenty-one days, but research suggests the real time varies widely, from a few weeks to many months, depending on the person and the behaviour. Missing a single day doesn't ruin the process, so there's no need to give up after one mistake.

Q14Talk: gap-fill notes · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

You will hear a talk about the role of fungi in nature. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)When people think of fungi, they usually picture mushrooms. But a mushroom is only the fruiting body of a much larger organism, like an apple on a tree. Most of the fungus lives hidden underground as a network of fine threads called hyphae, which together form a mycelium.Fungi are neither plants nor animals. They can't make their own food from sunlight, as plants do. Instead, they feed by releasing chemicals called enzymes that break down material around them, then absorbing the nutrients.This makes fungi essential decomposers. I'm worried they're not learning anything.Mr Clarke: I understand, but banning them isn't realistic. Students will use them anyway, just secretly. I'd rather teach them to use them well.Mrs Haider: What does using them well look like, though?Mr Clarke: For example, asking the chatbot to explain a difficult idea in simpler words, or to give feedback on a draft essay — not to write the essay itself.Mrs Haider: The trouble is that the answers aren't always accurate. Last week a student handed in a history essay with a quotation that didn't exist.Mr Clarke: That's actually a useful lesson. Students need to learn to check information against reliable sources. That skill matters whatever technology they use.Mrs Haider: I suppose so. I've also started setting more work in class, where I can see the students' thinking. And I ask them to explain their answers aloud.Mr Clarke: That's a good approach. Oral questioning makes it obvious whether someone really understands.Mrs Haider: My other concern is fairness. Not every student has internet access at home.Mr Clarke: Agreed. The school library could provide access for those who need it.Mrs Haider: Maybe we should write a clear policy, so students know exactly what's allowed.

Q29Discussion: gap-fill notes · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

You will hear a discussion between two economics students, Shahzad and Mahnoor, about a four-day working week. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)Shahzad: Have you read about the trials of a four-day working week in some countries? Employees worked thirty-two hours instead of forty, for the same pay.Mahnoor: Yes, and the results surprised me. Most companies in the trials reported that productivity stayed the same, and some even said it went up.Shahzad: How is that possible, with fewer hours?Mahnoor: Apparently people wasted less time. Meetings became shorter, and staff were more focused because they knew they had a three-day weekend to look forward to.Shahzad: I can see it working in offices. But what about hospitals or factories that need to run every day?Mahnoor: That's a real challenge. Those organisations would need to employ extra staff to cover the missing day, which raises costs.Shahzad: And the trials were mostly voluntary, weren't they? Companies that chose to take part were probably the ones most likely to succeed.Mahnoor: Good point. That's called selection bias. Still, employees reported less stress and fewer sick days, which saves companies money.Shahzad: I'd also worry that people would just use the extra day to take a second job.Mahnoor: Some might. But most in the trials spent it with family or on hobbies.Shahzad: I think it could work in some sectors, but not as a national law.Mahnoor: I agree. It should be flexible, not forced on everyone.

  1. (a) Trials: same ______, fewer hours [1]
  2. (b) Most companies: productivity stayed the ______ [1]
  3. (c) Reason: people wasted less ______ [1]
  4. (d) Example: ______ became shorter [1]
  5. (e) Problem for hospitals: need to employ extra ______ [1]
  6. (f) Weakness of trials: called selection ______ [1]
  7. (g) Benefit to companies: fewer ______ days [1]
  8. (h) Conclusion: it should be ______, not forced [1]

  2. (b) same
  3. (c) time
  4. (d) meetings
  5. (e) staff

  7. (g) sick

Q30Discussion: gap-fill notes · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

You will hear a discussion between a student, Iqra, and her grandfather about how farming in their village has changed. Complete the notes. Write no more than three words or a number in each gap.Transcript (read it aloud, or read it silently)Iqra: Dada jaan, for my project, can you tell me how farming has changed since you were young?Grandfather: Enormously! When I was your age, we ploughed with oxen. It took a whole week to plough our fields. Now my neighbour hires a tractor and finishes in a day.Iqra: That must have made life much easier.Grandfather: In some ways. But tractors are expensive, and many small farmers have fallen into debt paying for fuel and repairs.Iqra: What about water? Did you have tube wells?Grandfather: No, we depended on the canal and the rain. Tube wells came later. They were a blessing at first, but now the water table has dropped so much that wells have to be dug far deeper than before.Iqra: I read that farmers now use a lot of chemical fertiliser.Grandfather: Yes. At first the harvests doubled. But we used to put manure on the fields and rotate crops, growing beans or lentils to feed the soil. Now many farmers grow wheat and rice year after year, and the soil is getting tired.Iqra: Is anything better now?Grandfather: Of course! Mobile phones. Farmers can check market prices before selling, so middlemen can't cheat them so easily. And weather forecasts help us plan.Iqra: So it's a mixture of good and bad.Grandfather: That's farming — and life!

  1. (a) In the past, fields were ploughed using ______ [1]
  2. (b) Problem with tractors: small farmers fell into ______ [1]
  3. (c) Past water sources: the canal and ______ [1]
  4. (d) Problem now: the water ______ has dropped [1]
  5. (e) Traditional fertiliser: ______ [1]
  6. (f) Old method to feed the soil: rotate ______ [1]
  7. (g) Benefit of mobile phones: checking market ______ [1]
  8. (h) Group who can no longer cheat farmers so easily: ______ [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) oxen
  2. (b) debt
  3. (c) (the) rain
  4. (d) table
  5. (e) manure
  6. (f) crops
  7. (g) prices
  8. (h) middlemen
