Q1Workers · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which is a non-wage factor that might influence a person's choice of occupation? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C the chance of promotion
    Promotion prospects are a non-wage (non-monetary) benefit of a job. Overtime pay (A), bonuses (B) and commission (D) are all forms of payment, so they are wage factors.
Q2Workers · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In a football factory in Sialkot, one group of workers cuts the panels, another stitches them together and a third group prints the logos.

  1. (a) Define specialisation of workers (division of labour). [2]
  2. (b) Explain two advantages to the factory of its workers specialising. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Workers concentrating on a particular task or a narrow range of tasks, with production split into separate stages.
    1 mark: concentrating on one task / particular job. 1 mark: production broken down into stages / tasks.
  2. (b) Any two, developed: workers become more skilled and faster through repetition, so productivity rises; less time is wasted moving between tasks; training is quicker and cheaper because each worker learns only one task; it is easier to use specialist machinery; output rises and average cost falls.
    For each of two advantages: 1 mark for the advantage, 1 mark for the effect on the firm (output/costs/quality).
Q3Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

The table shows the average monthly wage of two occupations in a country.Occupation | Average monthly wage (Rs)Airline pilot | 960 000Farm labourer | 40 000

  1. (a) Calculate how many times greater the pilot's average wage is than the farm labourer's. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why airline pilots are paid more than farm labourers. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 24 times
    960 000 ÷ 40 000 = 24 (B1).
  2. (b) Pilots need long, expensive training and rare skills, so the supply of pilots is small, whereas almost anyone can do farm labouring, so supply is large. Demand for pilots is high because each pilot produces a very valuable service (high revenue per flight) and carries great responsibility for passengers' safety.
    For each of two reasons: 1 mark for the reason (training/skills, limited supply, high demand/productivity, responsibility, risk), 1 mark for linking it to the wage.
Q4Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Nadia has been offered two jobs: one as an economist in a government ministry and one, with a 25% higher salary, at a private bank.

  1. (a) Identify two non-wage factors that Nadia might consider when choosing between the jobs. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why Nadia might choose the lower-paid government job. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: job security; pension; working hours; holidays; promotion prospects; working conditions; location/travel time; job satisfaction; training opportunities.
    1 mark each for any two non-wage factors.
  2. (b) Government jobs often offer greater job security, so she is less likely to become unemployed; they may provide better pensions and fringe benefits; shorter or more regular working hours give a better work–life balance; she may value serving the public; together these benefits may outweigh the higher salary at the bank.
    Up to 4 marks: 1 mark per relevant non-wage benefit explained (max 3), 1 mark for the idea that total benefits/net advantages outweigh the higher wage.
Q5Workers · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A factory worker in Faisalabad is paid Rs 400 an hour for a basic week of 40 hours. Any extra hours are paid at time-and-a-half (1.5 times the basic rate). In one week he works 48 hours.

  1. (a) Calculate his gross earnings for the week. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the employer pays a higher rate for overtime hours. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 20 800
    M1: 40 × 400 = 16 000 and 8 × 600 = 4 800. A1: Rs 20 800.
  2. (b) Workers value their leisure time more after a long working day, so a higher rate is needed to persuade them to work extra hours; it is cheaper for the firm than hiring and training extra workers for a short period.
    1 mark: incentive to give up leisure / compensate for longer hours. 1 mark: cheaper or more flexible than recruiting more workers.
Q6Workers · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A government raises the national minimum wage by 20%. Many workers in shops, restaurants and small factories are paid at the minimum wage.

  1. (a) Define a national minimum wage. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not raising the minimum wage benefits low-paid workers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) The lowest wage rate per hour (or per month) that employers are legally allowed to pay workers, set by the government.
    1 mark: lowest wage that can be paid. 1 mark: set by law/government.
  2. (b) Benefits those who keep their jobs, but may cost some jobs.
    Up to 3 marks for 'benefits': higher income for those employed; reduces poverty and inequality; may motivate workers and raise productivity; encourages people into work. Up to 3 marks for 'may not': higher labour costs may make firms employ fewer workers, raising unemployment among the low-skilled; firms may replace workers with machines; prices may rise, reducing real gains; firms may cut hours or move into the informal sector, where the law is not enforced. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion (e.g. depends on the size of the rise and the elasticity of demand for labour).

Q8Firms · Core · Paper 2 style · 5 marks

A rice mill in Punjab buys a chain of grocery shops that sells rice and other food to consumers.

  1. (a) Identify the type of merger. [1]
  2. (b) Explain two benefits to the rice mill of this merger. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Vertical forward merger
    B1: vertical forward (the mill moves towards the consumer, the next stage of production).
  2. (b) Any two, developed: guaranteed outlets for its rice, so it can plan production; it gains the retail profit margin that the shops used to earn; it can control how its rice is displayed and priced; it can prevent rival mills selling through the shops; it receives direct information about consumer demand.
    For each of two benefits: 1 mark for the benefit, 1 mark for development.
Q9Firms · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A dairy company in Punjab closes several small milk-processing units and builds one large, highly automated plant.

  1. (a) Define economies of scale. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two internal economies of scale that the company might gain. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) The fall in average (unit) cost of production that occurs as a firm increases its scale of production / grows larger.
    1 mark: fall in average cost. 1 mark: as the firm increases its scale/size of output (long run).
  2. (b) Any two, developed: technical – large automated machines produce much more output per worker, lowering cost per litre; purchasing (bulk-buying) – buying milk, packaging and fuel in large quantities gets discounts; financial – a large firm can borrow more cheaply from banks; marketing – advertising costs are spread over more output; managerial – specialist managers can be employed.
    For each of two economies: 1 mark for naming/identifying it, 1 mark for explaining why average cost falls.
Q10Firms · Standard · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

A dairy company expands its milk-processing plant. Before expanding, the plant produced 10 000 litres a day at a total cost of Rs 1 500 000. After expanding, it produces 40 000 litres a day at a total cost of Rs 4 800 000.

  1. (a) Calculate the average cost per litre before and after the expansion. [2]
  2. (b) Explain one diseconomy of scale that might occur if the company grows much larger. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Before Rs 150 per litre; after Rs 120 per litre
    1 mark: 1 500 000 ÷ 10 000 = 150. 1 mark: 4 800 000 ÷ 40 000 = 120.
  2. (b) e.g. communication problems: with many layers of management, messages are slow or distorted, decisions are delayed and mistakes are made, so average cost rises. Also: poor motivation of workers who feel unimportant; difficulty coordinating/controlling a very large firm.
    1 mark: identifies a diseconomy. 1 mark: explains why it raises average cost.
Q11Firms · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In many towns, small family-run shops and workshops operate alongside large supermarket chains and manufacturers.

  1. (a) Identify two reasons why a firm might choose to stay small. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not small firms can survive in competition with large firms. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: the owner wants to keep control; the owner wants to avoid risk or extra work; the market is small or local; the product is a personal/specialised service; lack of finance for expansion; to avoid diseconomies of scale.
    1 mark each for any two valid reasons.
  2. (b) Many can survive, especially in niche or local markets, but they face disadvantages.
    Up to 3 marks for 'can survive': offer personal service and flexibility; serve small or niche markets that large firms ignore; located close to customers; can give credit to regular customers; can respond quickly to changes; may supply large firms (subcontracting); government support schemes. Up to 3 marks for 'cannot': higher average costs because they lack economies of scale, so higher prices; harder and more costly to raise finance; cannot afford advertising or research; large firms may cut prices to drive them out. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q12Market structure · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which is a characteristic of a monopoly? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B a single seller protected by barriers to entry
    A monopoly is a single seller (dominating the market) with barriers that stop new firms entering. Many sellers (A), no control over price (C) and free entry (D) are features of a highly competitive market.
Q13Market structure · Core · Paper 2 style · 4 marks

At a large fruit and vegetable market (sabzi mandi) in Lahore, hundreds of traders sell very similar tomatoes, onions and potatoes.

  1. (a) Define a competitive market. [2]
  2. (b) Identify two characteristics of a highly competitive market shown in the information. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) A market in which there are many firms competing to sell similar products, so no single firm can control the price.
    1 mark: many buyers and sellers / firms competing. 1 mark: no single firm controls the price / similar products.
  2. (b) Many sellers (hundreds of traders); similar (identical) products. (Also accept: price takers; easy entry.)
    1 mark each for any two characteristics, ideally linked to the information.
Q14Market structure · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A single company supplies all the electricity to the homes and businesses in a city.

  1. (a) Identify two barriers to entry that could stop new firms supplying electricity in the city. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two disadvantages of this monopoly for consumers. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: very high start-up costs (power stations, cables); economies of scale enjoyed by the existing firm; legal barriers such as a government licence; control of the distribution network.
    1 mark each for any two barriers.
  2. (b) Any two, developed: higher prices, because consumers have no alternative supplier; poor quality of service such as power cuts, because there is little pressure to improve; less choice; the firm may be inefficient, so costs and prices are higher; little incentive to innovate.
    For each of two disadvantages: 1 mark for identifying it, 1 mark for explaining why it arises from monopoly.
Q15Market structure · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain one reason why a monopoly can charge a higher price than a firm in a highly competitive market. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a monopoly is always worse for consumers than a competitive market. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Consumers have no close substitutes and rivals cannot enter the market, so the monopoly can raise price without losing all its customers; a competitive firm that raised its price would lose customers to rivals.
    1 mark: no close substitutes / barriers to entry. 1 mark: so demand does not fall to zero / it is a price maker, unlike a competitive firm.
  2. (b) Not always: economies of scale and research can benefit consumers, but higher prices and less choice are common.
    Up to 3 marks for 'worse': higher prices and lower output; less choice; less incentive to be efficient or improve quality; high profits at consumers' expense. Up to 3 marks for 'not always': economies of scale may mean lower costs and prices; high profits can fund research and new products; avoids wasteful duplication (natural monopoly, e.g. water pipes); can be regulated or state-owned to protect consumers. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q16Firms and production · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which is a measure of labour productivity? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B output per worker per hour
    Labour productivity is output per worker (or per worker-hour). Total output (A) is production, not productivity – a common confusion; C is the average wage; D is the inverse of productivity (workers per unit of output).
Q17Firms and production · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Hand-knotted carpets made in Pakistan are produced mainly by workers using simple looms, while carpets in some other countries are made on large automated machines.

  1. (a) Explain the difference between labour-intensive and capital-intensive production. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why hand-knotted carpet production remains labour-intensive. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Labour-intensive production uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital; capital-intensive production uses a high proportion of capital (machinery) relative to labour.
    1 mark: labour-intensive = more labour than capital. 1 mark: capital-intensive = more machinery/capital than labour.
  2. (b) Labour is relatively cheap compared with expensive machinery; consumers pay a high price for hand-made quality and designs that machines cannot copy; production is in small workshops that cannot afford or finance machinery; skilled weavers are available.
    For each of two reasons: 1 mark for the reason, 1 mark for development.
Q18Firms and production · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

The table shows information about a textile mill in Faisalabad.Year | Number of workers | Weekly output (metres of cloth)2025 | 200 | 50 0002026 | 180 | 54 000

  1. (a) Calculate labour productivity (metres per worker per week) in each year. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity between 2025 and 2026. [1]
  3. (c) Explain two ways in which the mill may have increased labour productivity. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2025: 250 metres; 2026: 300 metres
    1 mark: 50 000 ÷ 200 = 250. 1 mark: 54 000 ÷ 180 = 300.
  2. (b) +20% (increase)
    (300 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = 20% increase (B1, own figures from (a)).
  3. (c) Any two, developed: training workers so they work faster and make fewer mistakes; installing more modern machinery; better organisation or division of labour; incentives such as bonuses linked to output; better working conditions that improve motivation.
    For each of two ways: 1 mark for the method, 1 mark for explaining how it raises output per worker.
Q19Firms and production · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A biscuit manufacturer is deciding whether to replace 60 packing workers with automated packing machines.

  1. (a) Identify two influences on a firm's demand for a factor of production. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the firm should replace the workers with machines. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: the price (cost) of the factor, e.g. the wage rate; the productivity of the factor; the demand for the product it makes (derived demand); the price and productivity of other factors that could replace it.
    1 mark each for any two valid influences.
  2. (b) Depends on relative costs, productivity and the firm's market.
    Up to 3 marks for 'should': machines work faster and longer without breaks, raising productivity; lower average cost in the long run; more consistent quality and hygiene; fewer labour problems such as strikes. Up to 3 marks for 'should not': high initial cost and maintenance; machines may break down and need skilled technicians; workers are cheap and flexible; if demand falls, idle machines still have to be paid for; loss of goodwill and redundancy costs. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q20Money and banking · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which is a function of a central bank rather than a commercial bank? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B acting as the banker to the government
    A central bank is the government's banker (it also issues the currency, is lender of last resort and conducts monetary policy). Accepting household deposits (A) and lending to firms (C) and house buyers (D) are functions of commercial banks.
Q21Money and banking · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

In many countries, including Pakistan and Kenya, people pay shopkeepers and send money to relatives using balances held in mobile phone wallets.

  1. (a) Explain two functions of money. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why mobile-wallet balances can act as money. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two, developed: medium of exchange – money is accepted in payment for goods, avoiding the need for a double coincidence of wants in barter; unit of account/measure of value – prices of different goods can be compared in one unit; store of value – money can be saved and used later; standard for deferred payment – debts can be agreed now and repaid in future.
    For each of two functions: 1 mark for the function, 1 mark for explanation.
  2. (b) They are generally acceptable to sellers as payment and have the characteristics of money: portable, divisible into small amounts and durable, so they can act as a medium of exchange.
    1 mark: generally accepted as payment / medium of exchange. 1 mark: a relevant characteristic (portable, divisible, durable, hard to forge).
Q22Money and banking · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

Commercial banks in a country raise the interest rate they pay on savings accounts from 8% to 11% a year.

  1. (a) Calculate the interest earned in one year on savings of Rs 250 000 at the new rate. [1]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the growth of commercial banking benefits an economy. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 27 500
    250 000 × 11 ÷ 100 = Rs 27 500 (B1).
  2. (b) Benefits through saving, lending and payments, but there are risks.
    Up to 3 marks for benefits: safe place for savings and interest for savers; loans to firms finance investment and growth; loans to households for housing/cars raise living standards; efficient payment systems help trade. Up to 3 marks for problems: banks aim for profit, so they may charge high interest or ignore small firms/poor households; excessive lending can cause debt problems, inflation or asset bubbles; bank failures can harm savers and the whole economy; banks may concentrate in cities. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion (e.g. benefits depend on regulation by the central bank).
Q23Firms' costs, revenue and objectives · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A café sells meals at Rs 250 each. The table shows its total cost per day at different outputs.Meals per day | Total cost (Rs)0 | 8 00050 | 13 000100 | 17 000150 | 22 500

  1. (a) State the café's fixed cost per day. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the average total cost per meal when 100 meals are sold. [1]
  3. (c) Calculate the café's profit when it sells 150 meals. [2]
  4. (d) Explain why average fixed cost falls as output increases. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Rs 8 000
    B1: Rs 8 000 (total cost when output is zero).
  2. (b) Rs 170
    17 000 ÷ 100 = Rs 170 (B1).
  3. (c) Rs 15 000
    M1: total revenue = 150 × 250 = 37 500; profit = 37 500 − 22 500. A1: Rs 15 000.
  4. (d) Fixed cost stays the same (Rs 8 000) whatever the output, so as more meals are produced it is spread over a larger number of units, so the fixed cost per meal falls.
    1 mark: total fixed cost does not change with output. 1 mark: divided by more units, so AFC falls.

Q25Firms' costs, revenue and objectives · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A large food company says its main aim is to maximise profit. A social enterprise selling low-cost water filters says its main aim is to improve health in poor villages.

  1. (a) Identify two objectives a firm might have other than profit maximisation. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a firm should always aim to maximise profit. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: survival; growth / increasing sales; increasing market share; social welfare / helping the community; providing a good service; protecting the environment.
    1 mark each for any two valid objectives.
  2. (b) Profit is important, but other aims may be more suitable at times.
    Up to 3 marks for 'should': rewards owners/shareholders for risk; provides funds for investment and growth; shows efficient use of resources; attracts finance. Up to 3 marks for 'not always': a new firm may aim to survive or grow first; cutting prices to gain market share may be better long term; social enterprises and public-sector firms aim at welfare; pursuing profit may harm reputation (pollution, poor treatment of workers); owners may want a comfortable life rather than maximum profit. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q26Households · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 mark
  1. (a) Which change is most likely to cause a household to save more of its income? [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B a rise in the interest rate paid on savings
    A higher interest rate increases the reward for saving, so households tend to save more. Lower income (A) usually reduces saving; easy credit (C) encourages borrowing and spending; job security (D) reduces the need for precautionary saving.
Q27Households · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A household in Lahore has a monthly disposable income of Rs 180 000. It spends Rs 153 000 a month and saves the rest.

  1. (a) Calculate the household's saving as a percentage of its disposable income. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why a high-income household usually saves a larger proportion of its income than a low-income household. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15%
    M1: (180 000 − 153 000) ÷ 180 000 × 100. A1: 15%.
  2. (b) Low-income households must spend almost all their income on necessities such as food, rent and fuel, so little is left to save. High-income households can meet their needs with a smaller share of income, so they can save more; they also have better access to banks and saving schemes and may save for large future purchases or investments.
    For each of two reasons: 1 mark for the reason, 1 mark for development.
Q28Households · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

The central bank of a country cuts its interest rate from 15% to 12%, and commercial banks reduce the interest they charge on loans.

  1. (a) Identify two reasons why a household might borrow. [2]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not the fall in interest rates will cause households to borrow more. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any two of: to buy a house; to buy expensive durable goods such as a car or motorcycle; to pay for education; to pay for a wedding or emergency; to start a small business.
    1 mark each for any two valid reasons.
  2. (b) Borrowing is likely to rise but depends on other influences.
    Up to 3 marks for 'will': loans are cheaper, so repayments fall; more households can afford house or car loans; saving is less rewarding, so spending financed by credit rises. Up to 3 marks for 'may not': households worried about job security or a recession may not borrow; they may already have high debts; banks may not lend if they think borrowers are risky; a cut of 3 percentage points may be too small to change decisions; religious or cultural reasons may discourage interest-based borrowing. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
Q29Trade unions · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a trade union. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two factors that affect the strength of a trade union. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) An organisation of workers that acts on behalf of its members to protect and improve their pay and working conditions.
    1 mark: association/organisation of workers. 1 mark: aims to improve pay/conditions/job security of members.
  2. (b) Any two, developed: the proportion of workers who are members – a high membership gives more bargaining power; the skills of members – skilled workers are hard to replace during a strike; the state of the economy – when unemployment is low, firms find it harder to replace workers; laws on strikes and union activity; the financial health of the employer; public support.
    For each of two factors: 1 mark for the factor, 1 mark for explaining how it affects bargaining power.
Q30Trade unions · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A teachers' union asks for an increase in its members' average monthly pay from Rs 90 000 to Rs 103 500. Prices in the country rose by 12% over the past year.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage pay rise that the union is asking for. [1]
  2. (b) Discuss whether or not a trade union is always beneficial for its members. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) 15%
    (103 500 − 90 000) ÷ 90 000 × 100 = 15% (B1).
  2. (b) Usually beneficial, but not always.
    Up to 3 marks for 'beneficial': collective bargaining wins higher pay (e.g. protecting real wages when prices rise 12%); better working conditions, safety and job security; legal advice and representation; training. Up to 3 marks for 'not always': members pay subscriptions; strikes mean lost pay; high wage rises may cause employers to cut jobs or replace workers with machines; a weak union may achieve little; members may disagree with the union's decisions. Max 5 without a conclusion; 1 mark for a justified conclusion.
