Practice questions

Original practice questions written by HomiLabs in the exam style. They are not Cambridge past paper questions. Try each one before opening the answer.

Q1Employment and unemployment · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A country has a labour force of 60 million people, of whom 4.2 million are unemployed. What is the unemployment rate?A 7.0 %B 7.5 %C 6.5 %D 70 % [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 7.0 %
    Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 = 4.2 ÷ 60 × 100 = 7.0 %. B divides by the number employed (55.8 million), giving about 7.5 %, instead of by the whole labour force.
Q2Employment and unemployment · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define unemployment. [2]
  2. (b) Identify and explain two types of unemployment. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Being of working age, willing and able to work, actively seeking work, but without a job.
    1 mark: willing and able to work/of working age; 1 mark: without a job but looking for one.
  2. (b) e.g. frictional – people between jobs; structural – mismatch caused by long-term decline of an industry
    1 mark each for naming two types + 1 mark each for explanation: frictional (between jobs, searching); structural (decline of an industry/change in skills needed); cyclical (lack of demand in a recession); seasonal (demand varies with seasons, e.g. tourism, harvest).
Q3Employment and unemployment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

In a country, the working-age population is 130 million. The labour force is 75 million, of whom 69 million are employed.

  1. (a) Calculate the number of people unemployed. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the unemployment rate. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Calculate the percentage of the working-age population that is in the labour force. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [1]
  4. (d) Explain two consequences of unemployment for the government. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 6 million
    B1 75 − 69 = 6 million.
  2. (b) 8.0 %
    M1 6 ÷ 75 × 100; A1 8.0 %.
  3. (c) 57.7 %
    B1 75 ÷ 130 × 100 = 57.7 %.
  4. (d) Lower income tax revenue; higher spending on unemployment benefits/welfare
    1 mark each for two consequences + 1 mark each for development: less tax revenue (income and indirect taxes); more spending on benefits; larger budget deficit/more borrowing; opportunity cost – less to spend on schools/hospitals; social problems such as crime raise costs; loss of votes/popularity.
Q4Employment and unemployment · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain why automation in textile mills in Faisalabad could cause structural unemployment. [3]
  2. (b) Explain two government policies that could reduce this type of unemployment. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Machines replace workers; the displaced workers' skills no longer match the jobs available, so they stay unemployed for a long time.
    1 mark: machines replace labour/fewer workers needed; 1 mark: workers' skills do not match jobs elsewhere (occupational immobility); 1 mark: long-term unemployment linked to a change in the structure of the industry.
  2. (b) Retraining schemes to give workers new skills; subsidies/grants for firms that set up in the area or employ these workers
    1 mark each for two policies + 1 mark each for explanation: retraining/vocational courses improve occupational mobility; relocation grants/housing help geographical mobility; subsidies to firms that hire; better job information centres; attracting new industries to the region.
Q5Employment and unemployment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a rise in unemployment is always bad for an economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Usually harmful (lost output, lower incomes, lower tax revenue, social costs), but may be short-term frictional, can reduce inflationary pressure and may reflect structural change that raises productivity later.
    Up to 5 marks for harm: output below potential/lost GDP; lower incomes and living standards; lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending; skills lost; poverty, crime, health problems. Up to 5 marks for why not always bad: frictional unemployment may be short and improve job matching; lower wage pressure may reduce inflation; labour available for new expanding industries; firms may become more efficient. Max 8; conclusion required for top band, e.g. depends on type and duration.
Q6Employment and unemployment · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

Real GDP growth (%) and unemployment rate (%) for five countries in one year:Country A: growth 6.1, unemployment 4.0Country B: growth 3.5, unemployment 6.2Country C: growth 1.2, unemployment 8.9Country D: growth −0.8, unemployment 11.5Country E: growth 4.0, unemployment 7.8

  1. (a) Identify the country with the highest unemployment rate. [1]
  2. (b) Analyse the relationship between economic growth and the unemployment rate shown in the table. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Country D (11.5 %)
    B1 Country D.
  2. (b) Generally an inverse relationship: higher growth, lower unemployment (A highest growth 6.1 %, lowest unemployment 4.0 %; D negative growth, highest unemployment). Exception: E has higher growth than B but higher unemployment, so other factors (e.g. structural unemployment, labour force growth) matter.
    1 mark: inverse/negative relationship; 1 mark: data comparison using two countries (A and D); 1 mark: reason – growth raises demand for goods and so for labour (derived demand); 1 mark: exception identified (E vs B); 1 mark: explanation of exception, e.g. growth driven by capital-intensive industries, fast-growing labour force, structural or frictional factors.
Q7Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) What is a progressive tax?A a tax that takes a higher proportion of income as income risesB a tax that takes the same proportion of income at all income levelsC a tax that takes the same amount from everyoneD a tax that takes a lower proportion of income as income rises [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A a tax that takes a higher proportion of income as income rises
    A progressive tax has an average rate that rises with income. B is a proportional tax; D is a regressive tax.
Q8Fiscal policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Distinguish between a direct tax and an indirect tax, giving one example of each. [4]
  2. (b) State two reasons why governments impose taxes. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Direct tax is levied on income or wealth and paid directly to the government by the person taxed (e.g. income tax). Indirect tax is levied on spending on goods and services (e.g. sales tax/GST).
    1 mark: direct tax on income/wealth; 1 mark: valid example (income tax, corporation tax, property tax); 1 mark: indirect tax on spending/goods and services; 1 mark: valid example (sales tax, GST/VAT, excise duty, customs duty).
  2. (b) To raise revenue for public spending; to redistribute income
    1 mark each, any two: raise revenue; redistribute income; discourage consumption of demerit goods/reduce external costs; protect domestic industries (tariffs); influence aggregate demand/manage the economy.
Q9Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A country's income tax bands are:first $10 000 of income: 0 %next $20 000 (from $10 001 to $30 000): 10 %income above $30 000: 25 %

  1. (a) Calculate the income tax paid by a person earning $50 000 a year. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate this person's average rate of tax. [2]
  3. (c) A person earning $20 000 pays $1 000 in tax. Explain whether this income tax system is progressive. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) $7 000
    M1 0 + 10 % × 20 000 + 25 % × 20 000 = 0 + 2 000 + 5 000; A1 $7 000.
  2. (b) 14 %
    M1 7 000 ÷ 50 000 × 100; A1 14 %.
  3. (c) Yes: the average rate rises from 5 % at $20 000 to 14 % at $50 000.
    1 mark: average rate at $20 000 = 5 %; 1 mark: rising average rate with income, so progressive.
Q10Fiscal policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

In one year, a government's revenue is $120 billion and its spending is $138 billion. The country's GDP is $450 billion.

  1. (a) Calculate the budget deficit. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the budget deficit as a percentage of GDP. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two ways in which the government could reduce the budget deficit. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) $18 billion
    B1 138 − 120 = $18 bn.
  2. (b) 4 %
    M1 18 ÷ 450 × 100; A1 4 %.
  3. (c) Raise tax revenue (e.g. higher tax rates, wider tax base); cut government spending (e.g. on subsidies)
    1 mark each for two ways + 1 mark each for explanation: increase tax rates; widen tax base/reduce tax evasion; cut current spending (subsidies, wages); delay capital projects; privatise state firms to raise revenue; encourage economic growth which raises tax revenue automatically.
Q11Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether a cut in income tax rates will increase economic growth. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Could raise disposable income and spending, and improve incentives to work and invest; but extra income may be saved or spent on imports, supply may not respond if near capacity, and lower revenue may force cuts in public investment or more borrowing.
    Up to 5 marks for why it may: higher disposable income → more consumption → higher aggregate demand → more output; stronger incentive to work/more hours; more saving available for investment; attracts skilled workers. Up to 5 marks for why it may not: income saved (low confidence); spending on imports; inflation if economy near full capacity; lower government revenue → less spending on infrastructure/education or bigger deficit; small effect if many workers earn below the tax threshold. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q12Fiscal policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Explain how expansionary fiscal policy can reduce unemployment during a recession. [4]
  2. (b) Explain two reasons why expansionary fiscal policy may fail to reduce unemployment. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Higher government spending and/or lower taxes raise aggregate demand; firms sell more and need more workers (labour is a derived demand), reducing cyclical unemployment.
    1 mark: increase spending/cut taxes; 1 mark: aggregate demand rises; 1 mark: firms raise output; 1 mark: demand for labour rises, cyclical unemployment falls.
  2. (b) e.g. time lags in planning and spending; unemployment may be structural, so extra demand does not match workers' skills
    1 mark each for two reasons + 1 mark each for explanation: time lags; structural/frictional unemployment not caused by low demand; extra income saved or spent on imports; government cannot afford more borrowing/debt; firms use spare capacity or machines rather than hiring.
Q13Inflation and deflation · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define inflation. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two causes of inflation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A sustained rise in the general price level over a period of time.
    1 mark: rise in general price level; 1 mark: sustained/over time (fall in value of money).
  2. (b) Demand-pull: aggregate demand rises faster than output. Cost-push: higher costs of production (wages, imported oil) passed on as higher prices.
    1 mark each for naming two causes + 1 mark each for explanation: demand-pull (spending > output capacity); cost-push (higher wages, raw materials, energy, import costs after depreciation); increase in money supply; higher indirect taxes.
Q14Inflation and deflation · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A consumer price index has three categories. In the base year each category had an index of 100. One year later: food (weight 0.5) has an index of 112, transport (weight 0.3) 120 and other items (weight 0.2) 105. What is the rate of inflation?A 13 %B 12.3 %C 37 %D 11.3 % [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A 13 %
    Weighted index = 0.5 × 112 + 0.3 × 120 + 0.2 × 105 = 56 + 36 + 21 = 113, so prices rose 13 %. B is the unweighted average (112.3), which ignores the weights.
Q15Inflation and deflation · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks

A country's consumer price index (CPI):Year 1: 150Year 2: 162Year 3: 170.1

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of inflation in Year 2. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the rate of inflation in Year 3. [2]
  3. (c) A newspaper says 'prices fell in Year 3'. Explain why this statement is wrong. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 8 %
    M1 (162 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100; A1 8 %.
  2. (b) 5 %
    M1 (170.1 − 162) ÷ 162 × 100; A1 5 %.
  3. (c) Inflation fell (disinflation) but was still positive, so prices continued to rise, only more slowly.
    1 mark: index still rose/inflation still positive; 1 mark: rate of increase slowed – this is not deflation.
Q16Inflation and deflation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether deflation is harmful to an economy. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Harmful if caused by falling demand: consumers delay purchases, profits fall, unemployment rises, real debt burden rises. Less harmful if caused by lower costs/higher productivity: real incomes rise and exports become more competitive.
    Up to 5 marks for harm: postponed spending lowers aggregate demand; firms cut output and jobs; real value of debts rises, so borrowers suffer and spending falls; falling profits reduce investment; difficult for central bank to cut interest rates further. Up to 5 marks for not harmful: 'good' deflation from lower costs/new technology; higher real incomes, especially for people on fixed incomes and savers; improved international competitiveness. Max 8; judgement on cause/size/length needed for top band.
Q17Inflation and deflation · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 7 marks

A worker's monthly wage rises from Rs 40 000 to Rs 44 000 over a year in which inflation is 12 %.

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage change in the worker's money (nominal) wage. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage change in the worker's real wage. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how inflation affects savers and borrowers. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) +10 %
    B1 (44 000 − 40 000) ÷ 40 000 × 100 = 10 %.
  2. (b) −1.8 %
    M1 real wage = 44 000 ÷ 1.12 = Rs 39 285.71 (in Year 1 prices); A1 (39 285.71 − 40 000) ÷ 40 000 × 100 = −1.8 %.
  3. (c) Savers lose if inflation exceeds the interest rate, as the real value of savings falls; borrowers gain because the real value of their debts falls.
    Savers: 1 mark real value of savings falls, 1 mark if interest rate < inflation (negative real interest). Borrowers: 1 mark real value of debt falls, 1 mark repay with money worth less.
Q18Economic growth · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Economic growth is best measured by an increase in which of the following?A real GDPB nominal (money) GDPC the size of the populationD the money supply [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A real GDP
    Economic growth is an increase in real GDP, i.e. output adjusted for inflation. B can rise just because prices rise.
Q19Economic growth · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define a recession. [2]
  2. (b) Explain two consequences of a recession. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters (six months or more).
    1 mark: fall in real GDP/negative economic growth; 1 mark: for two consecutive quarters/six months.
  2. (b) Rising unemployment as firms cut output; lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending for the government
    1 mark each for two consequences + 1 mark each for explanation: unemployment rises; incomes and living standards fall; firms' profits fall and some close; government tax revenue falls/benefit spending rises; inflation usually falls; imports fall.
Q20Economic growth · Standard · Paper 2 style · 8 marks

A country's real GDP rose from $380 billion to $395.2 billion in one year.

  1. (a) Calculate the rate of economic growth. [2]
  2. (b) The population grew by 2.5 % in the same year. Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. [2]
  3. (c) Explain two causes of economic growth. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4 %
    M1 (395.2 − 380) ÷ 380 × 100; A1 4 %.
  2. (b) +1.5 %
    M1 1.04 ÷ 1.025 = 1.0146; A1 +1.5 % (accept about 1.5 %).
  3. (c) Investment in capital goods; improvements in education and training (human capital)
    1 mark each for two causes + 1 mark each for explanation: more investment in machinery and infrastructure; better education/skills raise productivity; new technology; discovery of natural resources; more workers (e.g. immigration, higher participation); higher aggregate demand using spare capacity.
Q21Economic growth · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether economic growth always benefits the people of a country. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Growth can raise incomes, jobs and tax revenue for public services, but may damage the environment, deplete resources, widen inequality, cause inflation or longer working hours, and benefits depend on population growth.
    Up to 5 marks for benefits: higher real incomes and living standards; more jobs/lower unemployment; more tax revenue for schools and hospitals without raising tax rates; reduced poverty. Up to 5 marks for costs: pollution and climate damage; depletion of non-renewable resources; inequality – gains go to a few; inflation; stress/longer hours; GDP per head may not rise if population grows faster. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q22Monetary policy · Core · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Identify two monetary policy measures. [2]
  2. (b) Explain how a cut in interest rates can increase consumer spending. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Changing interest rates; changing the money supply
    1 mark each, any two: interest rate changes; changes in the money supply; changes in the exchange rate (managed by the central bank).
  2. (b) Borrowing becomes cheaper so more people take loans for cars and homes; the reward for saving falls so people save less; existing borrowers with variable-rate loans pay less interest and have more disposable income.
    1 mark: borrowing cheaper; 1 mark: more spending on credit (e.g. cars, appliances); 1 mark: saving less attractive so more spent; 1 mark: lower repayments on existing loans raise disposable income.
Q23Monetary policy · Standard · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A central bank raises its interest rate. What is the most likely effect?A consumers borrow and spend lessB firms increase investmentC the country's currency depreciatesD the rate of inflation rises [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A consumers borrow and spend less
    Higher interest rates make loans more expensive and saving more rewarding, so consumer borrowing and spending fall. The currency usually appreciates (C wrong) as foreign savers are attracted, and investment falls (B wrong).
Q24Monetary policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) A country's central bank raises its interest rate to reduce inflation. Discuss whether higher interest rates will reduce inflation. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) Likely to reduce demand-pull inflation by cutting borrowing and spending and by appreciating the currency; less effective against cost-push inflation, if many people do not borrow from banks, if time lags are long, or if the higher rates cut investment and growth.
    Up to 5 marks for why it may work: dearer loans → less consumption and investment → lower aggregate demand; more saving; currency appreciation lowers import prices; reduced expectations of inflation. Up to 5 marks for why it may not: inflation is cost-push (e.g. imported oil prices); many households use cash/informal credit; time lags; government spending financed by borrowing keeps demand high; side effects – unemployment, lower growth, higher cost of government debt. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q25Supply-side policy · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following is a supply-side policy?A investment in vocational trainingB an increase in interest ratesC an increase in unemployment benefitsD an increase in import tariffs [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A investment in vocational training
    Supply-side policies aim to raise the productive capacity of the economy; training raises labour productivity. B is monetary policy and D is protectionism.
Q26Supply-side policy · Standard · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how two supply-side policies could increase labour productivity. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why supply-side policies may take a long time to have an effect. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Education and training raise workers' skills; tax incentives or subsidies for investment give workers better capital equipment
    1 mark each for two policies + 1 mark each for explanation: education/training; better health care; subsidies/tax allowances for investment in machinery and technology; improved infrastructure (roads, power supply); deregulation/privatisation increasing competition and efficiency.
  2. (b) e.g. education and infrastructure projects take years to complete before output rises.
    1 mark: long time to train workers/build infrastructure; 1 mark: so productive capacity increases only in the long run.
Q27Supply-side policy · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 8 marks
  1. (a) Discuss whether the privatisation of a state-owned electricity company will improve its efficiency. [8]
Show answer
  1. (a) The profit motive and access to private finance may cut costs and improve service; but a private monopoly may raise prices, cut unprofitable rural supply and under-invest, so regulation and competition matter.
    Up to 5 marks for improvement: profit motive encourages cost cutting and less waste; private investment funds new power plants; less political interference; competition if market opened. Up to 5 marks for why not: may become a private monopoly – higher prices, less output; may cut supply to unprofitable remote areas; job losses; regulation needed; efficiency depends on management not ownership. Max 8; judgement needed for top band.
Q28Macroeconomic aims · Core · Paper 1 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which of the following is NOT usually a macroeconomic aim of a government?A maximising the profits of private firmsB low and stable inflationC economic growthD low unemployment [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A maximising the profits of private firms
    The usual macroeconomic aims are economic growth, low unemployment, stable prices, balance of payments stability and redistribution of income. Profit maximisation is an objective of individual firms.
Q29Macroeconomic aims · Challenge · Paper 2 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Identify two macroeconomic aims of a government, other than full employment and low inflation. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why the aim of full employment may conflict with the aim of low inflation. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Economic growth; balance of payments stability
    1 mark each, any two: economic growth; balance of payments stability; redistribution of income.
  2. (b) Policies that raise aggregate demand to cut unemployment push up demand for goods and labour; with fewer spare workers, wages and prices rise, causing demand-pull and cost-push inflation.
    1 mark: policies to reduce unemployment raise aggregate demand; 1 mark: demand may exceed output capacity → demand-pull inflation; 1 mark: shortage of labour pushes up wages; 1 mark: firms pass on higher wage costs – cost-push inflation.
Q30Role of government · Standard · Paper 2 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Explain two roles of a government in a national economy. [4]
  2. (b) State one international role of a government. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) e.g. as a producer of goods and services such as education and roads; as a regulator protecting consumers and the environment
    1 mark each for two roles + 1 mark each for explanation: producer (public and merit goods); employer (civil servants, teachers); regulator (laws on competition, pollution, safety); redistributor of income (taxes and benefits); manager of the economy (fiscal, monetary, supply-side policies).
  2. (b) e.g. negotiating trade agreements with other countries
    B1 any one: member of international organisations (e.g. WTO); negotiating trade agreements; setting tariffs; giving or receiving aid; cooperating on environmental issues.
