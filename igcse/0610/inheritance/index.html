Q4Genes, DNA & protein synthesis · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

The gene for insulin is found in all human body cells but is used only in some cells of the pancreas.

  1. (a) Describe how the information in a gene is used to make a protein. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) The gene is a sequence of bases in DNA in the nucleus; a copy of the gene is made as an mRNA molecule; mRNA moves out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm; mRNA attaches to a ribosome; the ribosome assembles amino acids into a protein molecule; the specific order of bases in the mRNA determines the specific sequence of amino acids.
    1 mark each for any five of: DNA stays in nucleus; mRNA made as a copy of gene; mRNA leaves nucleus (through pores) to cytoplasm; mRNA attaches to ribosome; ribosome joins amino acids; base sequence determines amino acid sequence; amino acid sequence determines the protein's shape / function.
Q5Genes, DNA & protein synthesis · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A gene contains 1 260 bases on its coding strand. Three bases code for one amino acid. Assuming every base in the gene codes for an amino acid, calculate the number of amino acids in the protein it codes for. [1]
  2. (b) A mutation removes one base near the start of the gene. Suggest why this usually has a large effect on the protein made. [2]
  3. (c) State why the shape of a protein is important for its function, using an enzyme as an example. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 420
    B1 1260 ÷ 3 = 420.
  2. (b) The bases after the deletion are read in different groups of three, so the sequence of amino acids changes from that point; the protein has a different shape and may not function.
    B1 changes the reading of all later bases / amino acid sequence; B1 protein shape / function changed.
  3. (c) The amino acid sequence determines how the protein folds; an enzyme's active site must have a specific shape complementary to its substrate.
    B1 sequence determines shape; B1 active site shape complementary to substrate.

    B1 saves energy / resources; accept prevents proteins interfering with the cell's own function.

Q13Codominance, blood groups & sex linkage · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain what is meant by codominance, using the ABO blood group alleles as an example. [2]
  2. (b) Draw a genetic diagram to show the possible blood groups of children from a father who is IᴬIᴼ and a mother who is IᴮIᴼ. Give the probability of a child having blood group O. [5]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both alleles in a heterozygote are expressed in the phenotype; e.g. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant so a person with IᴬIᴮ has blood group AB.
    B1 both alleles expressed in heterozygote; B1 IᴬIᴮ gives AB.
  2. (b) Parental genotypes IᴬIᴼ × IᴮIᴼ; gametes Iᴬ, Iᴼ and Iᴮ, Iᴼ; offspring IᴬIᴮ (AB), IᴬIᴼ (A), IᴮIᴼ (B), IᴼIᴼ (O); probability of O = 0.25 (1 in 4).
    B1 parental genotypes; B1 gametes; B1 offspring genotypes (Punnett square); B1 phenotypes; B1 probability 0.25 / 25 % / 1 in 4.

Q16Codominance, blood groups & sex linkage · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain why red-green colour blindness is more common in men than in women. [3]
  2. (b) A colour-blind man (XᵇY) and a woman with normal vision who is not a carrier (XᴮXᴮ) have children. Use a genetic diagram to give the probability that (i) a daughter is a carrier and (ii) a son is colour blind. [4]

  1. (a) The allele is on the X chromosome (sex-linked) and the Y chromosome carries no copy of the gene; a man has only one X, so one recessive allele (XᵇY) makes him colour blind; a woman needs two recessive alleles (XᵇXᵇ), which is less likely.

    B1 parental genotypes and gametes; B1 offspring genotypes; B1 (i) 1 / 100 %; B1 (ii) 0.
Q17Codominance, blood groups & sex linkage · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) A woman whose father was colour blind has normal vision. She has children with a man who has normal vision. Give the woman's genotype and explain how you worked it out. [2]

    M1 offspring XᴮXᴮ, XᴮXᵇ, XᴮY, XᵇY; A1 1/4 = 0.25.
  3. (c) 0.0625 (1/16)
    B1 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625.

Q23Mitosis, meiosis & stem cells · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Compare mitosis and meiosis by giving three differences. [3]
  2. (b) Mitosis is also used in asexual reproduction. Explain why a field of potato plants grown from tubers of one plant all show the same disease susceptibility. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Mitosis gives 2 cells, meiosis gives 4 (cells / gametes); mitosis gives genetically identical cells, meiosis gives genetically different cells; mitosis keeps chromosome number (diploid), meiosis halves it (haploid); mitosis for growth/repair, meiosis for gamete production.
    1 mark each for any three comparative differences.
  2. (b) Tubers are produced by mitosis so all the plants are genetically identical (clones); none has alleles giving resistance, so all are equally affected.
    B1 mitosis → genetically identical / clones; B1 same alleles / no variation for resistance.
Q24Mitosis, meiosis & stem cells · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe what happens to chromosomes before and during mitosis so that the two daughter cells are genetically identical. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why it is important that gametes are produced by meiosis, not by mitosis. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Each chromosome is copied (replicated / DNA copied) before division; the two copies separate, one copy going to each end of the cell; each new nucleus receives an exact copy of every chromosome; cell then divides (cytoplasm).
    B1 chromosomes replicate before mitosis; B1 copies separate (to opposite poles); B1 each daughter nucleus gets full identical set.
  2. (b) Meiosis halves the chromosome number (diploid 46 → haploid 23); at fertilisation two gametes fuse to restore the diploid number; otherwise the chromosome number would double every generation; meiosis also produces genetic variation.
    B1 halves chromosome number / haploid; B1 fertilisation restores diploid; B1 prevents doubling each generation / gives variation.

  1. (a) Calculate how many cells would be produced from one fertilised egg after 12 rounds of mitosis, assuming every cell divides each time. [1]
  2. (b) Red blood cells have no nucleus and live for only about four months. Explain why stem cells in the bone marrow must keep dividing throughout a person's life. [2]
  3. (c) State the chromosome number in the egg of a dog, whose body cells have 78 chromosomes, and name the division producing it. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 4096
    B1 2¹² = 4096.
  2. (b) Red blood cells cannot divide (no nucleus) and are constantly being destroyed, so they must be replaced; stem cells divide by mitosis to make new cells that become specialised as red blood cells.
    B1 red blood cells cannot divide / are continually lost and need replacing; B1 stem cells divide by mitosis and the daughter cells become specialised (as red blood cells).
  3. (c) 39; meiosis.
    B1 39; B1 meiosis.

  1. (a) B 3 : 1
    Rr × Rr gives RR, Rr, Rr, rr: three red to one white. 1 : 1 (A) is the result of a test cross Rr × rr.
Q27Monohybrid crosses & pedigrees · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) State the meaning of (i) homozygous and (ii) heterozygous. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why two heterozygous individuals do not 'breed true' for a characteristic. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) Having two identical alleles of a particular gene; (ii) having two different alleles of a particular gene.
    B1 each.
  2. (b) Each can pass on either allele, so offspring can be homozygous dominant, heterozygous or homozygous recessive – some offspring show a different phenotype from the parents.
    B1 gametes carry different alleles; B1 offspring can be recessive / different phenotype.
Q28Monohybrid crosses & pedigrees · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain how a farmer could use a test cross to find out whether a black-coated goat is homozygous (BB) or heterozygous (Bb). Black (B) is dominant to brown (b). [3]
  2. (b) Draw a genetic diagram for the cross if the black goat is heterozygous. State the expected ratio of offspring phenotypes. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Cross the black goat with a brown goat (bb, homozygous recessive); if any offspring are brown, the black goat is Bb; if all offspring (in a large number) are black, it is probably BB.
    B1 cross with homozygous recessive / brown goat; B1 any brown offspring → Bb; B1 all black → BB.
  2. (b) Bb × bb; gametes B, b and b; offspring Bb (black), bb (brown); ratio 1 black : 1 brown.
    B1 parental genotypes and gametes; B1 offspring genotypes; B1 1 : 1 black : brown.
Q29Monohybrid crosses & pedigrees · Standard · Paper 6 style · 4 marks

A student in Multan grew 810 pea seeds from a cross between two tall plants. 612 plants were tall and 198 were dwarf.

  1. (a) Calculate the ratio of tall to dwarf plants, to the nearest whole number : 1. [2]
  2. (b) State the genotypes of the parent plants. Use T for tall and t for dwarf. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest why the ratio is not exactly the expected ratio. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3 : 1
    M1 612 ÷ 198 = 3.09; A1 3 : 1.
  2. (b) Tt × Tt
    B1 both Tt.
  3. (c) Fertilisation is random / chance; sample is too small to give the exact ratio.
    B1 random fusion of gametes / chance.
Q30Monohybrid crosses & pedigrees · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A family pedigree is described: Persons 1 (father) and 2 (mother) are both unaffected. They have three children: person 3 (son, unaffected), person 4 (daughter, unaffected) and person 5 (son, has cystic fibrosis).

  1. (a) Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele (f). Use the pedigree to state the genotypes of persons 1 and 2. Explain your answer. [3]
  2. (b) Calculate the probability that the next child of persons 1 and 2 will have cystic fibrosis. [1]
  3. (c) Person 4 is unaffected. Calculate the probability that person 4 is a carrier (Ff). [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both Ff. They are unaffected, so each has at least one F; their child 5 has cystic fibrosis (ff) and received one f from each parent.
    B1 both Ff; B1 unaffected so have dominant allele; B1 affected child must have received f from each.
  2. (b) 0.25
    B1 1/4 = 0.25.
  3. (c) 2/3 (0.67)
    M1 unaffected offspring of Ff × Ff are FF, Ff, Ff (ff excluded); A1 2/3.
