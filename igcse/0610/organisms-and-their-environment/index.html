Q1Food chains, webs & energy flow · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In the food chain mango leaves → caterpillar → myna bird → hawk, what do the arrows show?A the direction in which energy is transferredB which organism is the largestC the direction in which carbon dioxide movesD the order in which the organisms arrived in the habitat [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A the direction in which energy is transferred
    Arrows in a food chain point from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it, showing the transfer of energy. They do not show size (B).
Q2Food chains, webs & energy flow · Core · Paper 4 style · 8 marks

A food web in a freshwater lake in Sindh is described below.Algae are eaten by snails and by mosquito larvae.Mosquito larvae are eaten by small fish.Snails are eaten by ducks.Small fish are eaten by kingfishers and herons.Herons also eat ducks.

  1. (a) Name the producer in this food web. [1]
  2. (b) Name one primary consumer and one secondary consumer. [2]
  3. (c) State the principal source of energy for this food web. [1]
  4. (d) Fishermen remove most of the small fish from the lake. Suggest and explain two effects on other organisms in the web. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) Algae (water plants).
    B1.
  2. (b) Primary: snail or mosquito larva. Secondary: small fish (also duck, which eats snails).
    1 mark each.
  3. (c) The Sun (light energy).
    B1.
  4. (d) Any two, each with a reason: kingfishers decrease because they lose their only food; mosquito larvae increase because fewer are eaten by small fish; herons eat more ducks, so the duck population falls (and snails may then increase); algae decrease because the larger number of mosquito larvae eat more of them.
    For each of two effects: 1 mark effect, 1 mark matching explanation.
Q3Food chains, webs & energy flow · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a pond food web, algae are eaten by water fleas and by tadpoles; water fleas are eaten by small fish; tadpoles are eaten by water beetles; small fish and water beetles are eaten by herons. Herons also eat tadpoles. At which trophic levels does the heron feed?A second onlyB third onlyC third and fourthD fourth and fifth [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C third and fourth
    Algae (1st) → tadpole (2nd) → heron gives heron at the 3rd level; algae → water flea → small fish → heron or algae → tadpole → water beetle → heron gives the 4th level. The heron is a secondary and a tertiary consumer, so 'third only' is incomplete.
Q4Food chains, webs & energy flow · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Explain why only a small proportion of the energy in one trophic level is transferred to the next. [4]
  2. (b) Explain why food chains rarely have more than five trophic levels. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not all of the organism is eaten (e.g. roots, bones); not all that is eaten is digested and absorbed – some is lost in faeces; much energy is used in respiration and lost as heat (movement, keeping warm); energy remains in dead organisms and waste, passing to decomposers; energy is lost in excretory products such as urea.
    1 mark each for any four.
  2. (b) So much energy is lost at each transfer that after four or five levels there is too little energy left to support another population of animals.
    1 mark: energy lost at each level; 1 mark: too little left to support a further level.
Q5Food chains, webs & energy flow · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Define the terms producer and consumer. [2]
  2. (b) Define the term decomposer and state its role in an ecosystem. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why energy is described as flowing through an ecosystem whereas nutrients such as carbon are described as cycling. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Producer: an organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis. Consumer: an organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) An organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material; it breaks this down and releases nutrients (e.g. ammonium, carbon dioxide) back into the environment for recycling.
    1 mark: definition; 1 mark: recycles nutrients / releases CO₂ and mineral ions.
  3. (c) Energy enters as light and is eventually lost as heat, so it cannot be reused; carbon atoms are passed between organisms and the environment and used again and again.
    1 mark: energy enters from Sun and leaves as heat (not recycled); 1 mark: nutrients recycled.
Q6Food chains, webs & energy flow · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

In a mangrove ecosystem, dead mangrove leaves fall into the water. Crabs and bacteria feed on the leaves. Shrimps and small fish feed on the bacteria and on small pieces of leaf. Large fish such as groupers eat the crabs, shrimps and small fish.

  1. (a) Explain what would happen to the energy in the dead leaves of the mangroves if there were no crabs or decomposers. [2]
  2. (b) Mangrove forests along the coast near Karachi have been cut down. Suggest and explain the effect on the number of large fish caught offshore. [3]
  3. (c) Suggest why the crab is described as both a decomposer-feeder (detritivore) and a primary consumer in this web. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The energy would stay locked in the dead leaves; it would not be passed on to other organisms in the food web (and nutrients would not be recycled).
    1 mark: energy not transferred / stays in dead material; 1 mark: other organisms (e.g. shrimps, fish) lose a food source.
  2. (b) Fewer mangroves → less dead leaf material for crabs and shrimps (and less nursery habitat for young fish) → fewer small fish/shrimps for large fish to eat → fewer large fish caught.
    1 mark each: fewer producers / less leaf litter; fewer primary/secondary consumers; fewer large fish because less food (or loss of breeding habitat).
  3. (c) It feeds on dead plant material (leaves), which came from producers, so it gets energy from producers at the second trophic level.
    B1.
Q7Food chains, webs & energy flow · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Snow leopards are top carnivores in the mountains of Gilgit-Baltistan. They feed on ibex and markhor, which eat grasses and shrubs. Explain, in terms of energy flow, why each snow leopard needs a very large hunting area. [3]
  2. (b) Use your answer to suggest why top carnivores are often among the first animals to become endangered when their habitat is reduced. [2]
  3. (c) State the trophic level of the snow leopard in this food chain. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Energy is lost at each transfer between trophic levels (heat from respiration, waste, parts not eaten); so only a small fraction of the energy fixed by the plants reaches the snow leopard (third trophic level); plant growth in cold mountains is low, so a large area of plants and many prey animals are needed to supply enough energy for one leopard.
    1 mark each for any three: energy lost at each transfer; little energy reaches the top level; many prey / large area of producers needed per leopard; low productivity of mountain vegetation.
  2. (b) Their populations are already small because little energy reaches them; losing habitat removes prey and hunting area, so the population quickly becomes too small to survive.
    1 mark: small populations at top trophic level; 1 mark: habitat loss reduces prey / area below what is needed.
  3. (c) Third trophic level (secondary consumer).
    B1.
Q8Populations, growth curves & sampling · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In a sigmoid population growth curve of bacteria in a flask, which phase comes immediately after the lag phase?A death phaseB stationary phaseC exponential (log) phaseD a second lag phase [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C exponential (log) phase
    The order is lag → exponential (log) → stationary → death. The stationary phase follows the exponential phase, not the lag phase, and the death phase comes last.
Q9Populations, growth curves & sampling · Standard · Paper 6 style · 9 marks

Yeast cells were added to a flask of glucose solution at 25 °C. Samples were counted every 2 hours.Time (h): 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14Number of yeast cells (thousands per mm³): 2, 3, 8, 25, 60, 92, 98, 97

  1. (a) Calculate the mean rate of increase in yeast numbers between 4 and 8 hours, in thousand cells per mm³ per hour. [2]
  2. (b) Name the phases of growth shown between 0–2 hours and between 10–14 hours. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why the population stopped increasing after 12 hours. [3]
  4. (d) Predict what would happen to the population if counting continued for several more days. Give a reason. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 13 thousand cells per mm³ per hour
    M1: (60 − 8) ÷ 4; A1: 13.
  2. (b) 0–2 h: lag phase; 10–14 h: stationary phase.
    1 mark each.
  3. (c) Glucose (food) was being used up; toxic waste products such as ethanol built up; the birth (reproduction) rate became equal to the death rate.
    1 mark each for any three: limited food/glucose; accumulation of toxic waste (ethanol, CO₂); competition; reproduction rate = death rate.
  4. (d) The population would decrease (death phase) because food runs out and toxic waste increases, so death rate exceeds reproduction rate.
    1 mark: decrease / death phase; 1 mark: reason.
Q10Populations, growth curves & sampling · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) State four factors that affect the rate of growth of a population. [4]
  2. (b) The human population of the world grew slowly for thousands of years and then increased very rapidly in the last 200 years. Suggest two reasons for the rapid increase. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Food supply; competition; predation; disease (also availability of space, nesting sites, water).
    1 mark each for any four.
  2. (b) Better medical care / vaccines / antibiotics reducing death rate (especially infant mortality); improved food supply through modern agriculture; clean water and sanitation reducing disease.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q11Populations, growth curves & sampling · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) In a population that has reached the stationary phase, compare the birth rate with the death rate. [1]
  2. (b) Explain how a predator–prey relationship affects the number of prey in a population of rabbits. [3]
  3. (c) State what is meant by a limiting factor for a population. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) The birth rate equals the death rate.
    B1.
  2. (b) When predators (e.g. foxes) increase, more rabbits are eaten, so the rabbit population falls; with fewer rabbits, predators have less food and their numbers fall; then the rabbit population can increase again (numbers rise and fall in cycles).
    1 mark each: more predators → fewer prey; fewer prey → fewer predators; prey numbers then rise again (cycle).
  3. (c) A factor that is in short supply and so restricts the growth of the population (e.g. food, space).
    B1.
Q12Populations, growth curves & sampling · Challenge · Paper 5 style · 7 marks

A student estimated the number of dandelion plants on a school field of area 1200 m². She placed ten quadrats, each 0.25 m², on the field and counted the dandelions in each.Counts: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 2, 3, 4

  1. (a) Calculate the mean number of dandelion plants per quadrat. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate the mean number of dandelions per m². [1]
  3. (c) Estimate the total number of dandelions on the field. [1]
  4. (d) Explain how the student should decide where to place the quadrats. [2]
  5. (e) Suggest two ways the estimate could be made more reliable. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 3.0 dandelions per quadrat
    30 ÷ 10 = 3.0.
  2. (b) 12 per m²
    3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12.
  3. (c) about 14 400 dandelions
    12 × 1200 = 14 400.
  4. (d) Place them at random, e.g. use random numbers as coordinates on a grid of measuring tapes laid along two edges of the field; this avoids bias in choosing where to sample.
    1 mark: random placement using random numbers/coordinates; 1 mark: avoids bias / representative sample.
  5. (e) Use more quadrats; repeat on different days; use a larger quadrat; have clear rules for counting plants on the edge of the quadrat.
    1 mark each for any two.
Q13Populations, growth curves & sampling · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) After heavy rains, desert locusts in Balochistan and Sindh can increase from a few thousand to millions within months. Suggest two reasons for this rapid increase. [2]
  2. (b) Name the phase of a population growth curve that describes this rapid increase and explain what is happening to birth and death rates. [2]
  3. (c) Suggest three factors that eventually cause the swarm to decline. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Plenty of fresh plant food after rain; moist soil for egg laying; few predators relative to locust numbers; locusts reproduce quickly with many eggs.
    1 mark each for any two.
  2. (b) Exponential (log) phase; the birth (reproduction) rate is much greater than the death rate.
    1 mark: exponential/log phase; 1 mark: birth rate greatly exceeds death rate.
  3. (c) Food runs out (vegetation eaten / dries up); increased predation by birds; disease spreading in crowded conditions; competition for food; insecticide spraying.
    1 mark each for any three.
Q14Nitrogen cycle · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which organisms in the root nodules of pea plants convert nitrogen gas into compounds the plant can use?A nitrifying bacteriaB denitrifying bacteriaC nitrogen-fixing bacteriaD decomposing fungi [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) C nitrogen-fixing bacteria
    Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules convert N₂ into ammonium/nitrogen compounds. Nitrifying bacteria (A) live in the soil and convert ammonium ions to nitrate ions.
Q15Nitrogen cycle · Standard · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) In waterlogged soil, which process is increased and makes the soil less fertile?A denitrification of nitrate ions to nitrogen gasB nitrification of ammonium ions to nitrate ionsC nitrogen fixation by lightningD absorption of nitrate ions by root hairs [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) A denitrification of nitrate ions to nitrogen gas
    Waterlogged soil lacks oxygen; denitrifying bacteria thrive in anaerobic conditions and convert nitrate ions to nitrogen gas, which is lost from the soil. Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen, so nitrification (B) decreases.
Q16Nitrogen cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe how nitrogen in the proteins of dead leaves becomes nitrate ions in the soil. Name the types of organism involved. [4]
  2. (b) State how plants absorb nitrate ions and what they use them for. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins in dead leaves; they release ammonium ions (ammonification); nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions into nitrite ions; and then into nitrate ions.
    1 mark each: decomposers break down proteins / amino acids; ammonium ions released; nitrifying bacteria; ammonium → nitrite → nitrate.
  2. (b) By active transport into root hair cells; to make amino acids and then proteins.
    1 mark: active transport through root hairs; 1 mark: amino acids / proteins.
Q17Nitrogen cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Explain why ploughing and aerating soil increases the concentration of nitrate ions in it. [3]
  2. (b) Name one way, other than by bacteria, in which nitrogen gas in the air is fixed. [1]
  3. (c) Describe how the nitrogen in a plant protein can become part of a goat's muscle and later reach the soil. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Ploughing lets oxygen into the soil; nitrifying bacteria need oxygen (aerobic) to convert ammonium ions into nitrate ions; and denitrifying bacteria, which work in anaerobic conditions, are less active.
    1 mark each: more oxygen; nitrifying bacteria aerobic, more nitrification; less denitrification.
  2. (b) Lightning (or the industrial Haber process to make fertiliser).
    B1.
  3. (c) The goat eats the plant; the protein is digested to amino acids, which are absorbed and assimilated into goat proteins (muscle); excess amino acids are deaminated and the nitrogen leaves in urea/urine, or when the goat dies decomposers break down its proteins, releasing ammonium ions to the soil.
    1 mark each: feeding and digestion to amino acids; assimilation into goat protein; return via urea/excretion or death and decomposition to ammonium.
Q18Nitrogen cycle · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Many farmers in Punjab grow a legume crop such as berseem clover or chickpea between wheat crops. Explain how this improves the yield of the next wheat crop. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest one advantage of this method over using large amounts of nitrate fertiliser. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Legumes have root nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria; these convert nitrogen gas from the air into ammonium / nitrogen compounds used to make amino acids; when the legume roots and leftover plant parts are ploughed in, decomposers and nitrifying bacteria release nitrate ions into the soil; wheat absorbs the nitrate to make proteins, so it grows better.
    1 mark each for any four: root nodules; nitrogen-fixing bacteria; N₂ → ammonium/nitrogen compounds; decomposition and nitrification add nitrate to soil; wheat uses nitrate for amino acids/proteins → growth.
  2. (b) Cheaper; less nitrate washed (leached) into rivers, so less eutrophication; adds organic matter to the soil.
    1 mark each for any two, or 2 marks for one fully explained point.
Q19Nitrogen cycle · Challenge · Paper 6 style · 7 marks

Researchers measured the nitrate concentration in the soil of two plots at the end of a season. One plot grew chickpeas; the other grew wheat only.Chickpea plot: 42 mg nitrate per kg soilWheat-only plot: 28 mg nitrate per kg soil

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage by which the nitrate concentration in the plot that grew chickpeas was greater than in the plot that grew wheat only. [2]
  2. (b) Suggest an explanation for the difference. [2]
  3. (c) State two variables that should have been the same for both plots to make the comparison valid. [2]
  4. (d) Suggest how the researchers could make the results more reliable. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 50 %
    M1: (42 − 28) ÷ 28 × 100; A1: 50 %.
  2. (b) Chickpeas are legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules; the nitrogen compounds from their roots and remains were converted to nitrate by decomposers and nitrifying bacteria.
    1 mark: nitrogen-fixing bacteria in nodules; 1 mark: decomposition / nitrification adds nitrate.
  3. (c) Soil type; amount of fertiliser added; watering / rainfall; time of year sampled; depth of soil sampling.
    1 mark each for any two.
  4. (d) Take several soil samples from different places in each plot (and/or use several plots of each type) and calculate a mean.
    B1.
Q20Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) A single neem tree supports 3000 caterpillars, which are eaten by 20 birds. What shape is the pyramid of numbers for this food chain?A a normal pyramid, wide at the base and narrowing to the topB narrow at the base, widest in the middle and narrower at the topC widest at the top and narrowest at the baseD all three levels the same width [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B narrow at the base, widest in the middle and narrower at the top
    One tree (narrow base), 3000 caterpillars (widest bar) and 20 birds (narrower). A pyramid of biomass for the same chain would have the normal shape (A), because the tree has a very large mass.
Q21Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Core · Paper 4 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Grass in a field receives energy from the Sun. A herd of goats eats grass containing 18 000 kJ of energy, and 1620 kJ of this becomes goat biomass. Calculate the percentage efficiency of the transfer. [2]
  2. (b) State the trophic level of the goats. [1]
  3. (c) Suggest one reason why most of the energy in the grass did not become goat biomass. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 9 %
    M1: 1620 ÷ 18 000 × 100; A1: 9 %.
  2. (b) Second trophic level (primary consumers).
    B1.
  3. (c) Lost as heat from respiration / lost in faeces (cellulose not digested) / used for movement.
    B1.
Q22Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks

Energy in each trophic level of a grassland in kJ per m² per year:Producers (grasses): 25 000Primary consumers (grasshoppers): 2500Secondary consumers (frogs): 200

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage efficiency of energy transfer from producers to primary consumers. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage efficiency of energy transfer from primary consumers to secondary consumers. [2]
  3. (c) Describe what happens to the energy that is not passed on from the primary consumers to the secondary consumers. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 10 %
    M1: 2500 ÷ 25 000 × 100; A1: 10 %.
  2. (b) 8 %
    M1: 200 ÷ 2500 × 100; A1: 8 %.
  3. (c) Used in respiration and lost as heat; lost in faeces (undigested food) and excretory products such as urea; parts not eaten / dead bodies pass to decomposers.
    1 mark each for any three.
Q23Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks

A field of maize contains 50 000 kJ of energy in parts that humans can eat. If this maize is fed to cattle, only 4 % of the energy ends up in beef that humans eat.

  1. (a) Calculate the energy that reaches humans as beef. [1]
  2. (b) Calculate how many times more energy humans would get by eating the maize directly. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why so little of the energy in the maize ends up in the beef. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) 2000 kJ
    50 000 × 4 ÷ 100 = 2000 kJ.
  2. (b) 25 times
    50 000 ÷ 2000 = 25.
  3. (c) Cattle use most of the energy in respiration for movement and to keep warm, which is lost as heat; some energy is lost in faeces and urine / methane; some energy is in parts of the cow that are not eaten (bones, skin).
    1 mark each for any three.
Q24Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) For the food chain rose bush → aphids → ladybirds, describe the shapes of the pyramid of numbers and the pyramid of biomass. [2]
  2. (b) Explain why a pyramid of biomass gives a more useful picture of the feeding relationships than a pyramid of numbers. [2]
  3. (c) Explain why a pyramid of energy is never inverted. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Pyramid of numbers: narrow base (one bush), very wide second bar (thousands of aphids), narrower top (ladybirds). Pyramid of biomass: normal pyramid – largest at the base (bush), smaller for aphids, smallest for ladybirds.
    1 mark each.
  2. (b) It takes account of the size (mass) of the organisms; one large producer is shown as large, so it shows how much living material is available at each level.
    1 mark: accounts for size/mass of individuals; 1 mark: shows amount of material/energy available at each level.
  3. (c) Energy is lost at every transfer (respiration, heat, waste), so each level must contain less energy than the level below it.
    1 mark: energy lost at each transfer; 1 mark: each level has less energy than the one it feeds on.
Q25Trophic levels, pyramids & energy efficiency · Challenge · Paper 6 style · 6 marks

Ecologists sampled a small area of grassland and measured the dry biomass at each trophic level (g per m²).Producers: 820Primary consumers: 74Secondary consumers: 9.2Tertiary consumers: 1.1

  1. (a) Calculate the percentage of the biomass of primary consumers that is present as secondary consumers. Give your answer to one decimal place. [2]
  2. (b) Calculate the percentage of producer biomass that is present as primary consumers. Give your answer to one decimal place. [1]
  3. (c) Explain why the samples were dried before weighing. [2]
  4. (d) State one disadvantage of measuring dry biomass. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 12.4 %
    M1: 9.2 ÷ 74 × 100; A1: 12.4 %.
  2. (b) 9.0 %
    74 ÷ 820 × 100 = 9.0 %.
  3. (c) The water content of organisms varies (e.g. with recent rain or drinking), so fresh mass is unreliable; dry mass measures only the organic material / biomass.
    1 mark: water content varies; 1 mark: dry mass gives a fair comparison of biomass.
  4. (d) The organisms must be killed.
    B1.
Q26Carbon cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 7 marks
  1. (a) Name the process that removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, and two processes that add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. [3]
  2. (b) Describe how carbon in a dead tree trunk can return to the atmosphere. [2]
  3. (c) Explain how carbon from plants that died millions of years ago is now being added to the atmosphere. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Removes: photosynthesis. Adds: any two of respiration (by plants, animals and decomposers), combustion of fossil fuels or wood, decomposition.
    1 mark: photosynthesis; 1 mark each for two adding processes.
  2. (b) Decomposers (fungi and bacteria) feed on the dead wood and respire, releasing carbon dioxide; or the wood is burnt (combustion).
    1 mark: decomposers; 1 mark: respiration releases CO₂ (or combustion).
  3. (c) The plants were not fully decomposed and were fossilised to form coal, oil or gas; burning these fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide.
    1 mark: fossilisation into fossil fuels; 1 mark: combustion releases CO₂.
Q27Carbon cycle · Challenge · Paper 4 style · 6 marks

A monitoring station in the northern hemisphere recorded the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air during one year. The highest reading, 424.6 ppm, was in May. The lowest, 418.9 ppm, was in September.

  1. (a) Calculate the difference between the highest and lowest carbon dioxide concentrations recorded during the year. [1]
  2. (b) Explain why the carbon dioxide concentration falls between May and September. [3]
  3. (c) The May reading in the following year was 427.1 ppm. Suggest two human activities that explain the year-on-year increase. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) 5.7 ppm
    424.6 − 418.9 = 5.7 ppm.
  2. (b) In the northern summer, longer days, more light and warmth mean more photosynthesis by plants (more leaves on trees); photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide faster than respiration (and decomposition) releases it.
    1 mark each: summer conditions / more leaves; more photosynthesis; rate of CO₂ uptake exceeds release by respiration.
  3. (c) Combustion of fossil fuels (vehicles, power stations, industry); deforestation – fewer trees to photosynthesise, and burning of the cleared wood.
    1 mark each.
Q28Ecosystem & community terms · Core · Paper 2 style · 1 marks
  1. (a) Which term describes all the populations of different species living together in one area?A habitatB communityC ecosystemD population [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) B community
    A community is all of the populations of different species in an ecosystem. An ecosystem (C) is the community together with its non-living environment, interacting with each other.
Q29Ecosystem & community terms · Standard · Paper 4 style · 5 marks
  1. (a) Use examples from the Thar Desert to explain the meaning of each term: population, community, ecosystem. [3]
  2. (b) State two non-living (abiotic) factors in the desert ecosystem that affect the organisms living there. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Population: a group of organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time, e.g. all the chinkara gazelles in one part of the desert. Community: all the populations of different species there, e.g. gazelles, desert foxes, lizards, khejri trees and grasses together. Ecosystem: the community and its environment (sand, water, climate) interacting together.
    1 mark each for a correct definition with a suitable example.
  2. (b) Any two of: temperature; rainfall / water availability; light intensity; wind; soil type / sand.
    1 mark each.
Q30Water cycle · Standard · Paper 4 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Describe the water cycle, naming the processes by which water moves between living organisms, the land, the sea and the atmosphere. [4]
  2. (b) Suggest how cutting down a large area of forest in the northern hills could affect local rainfall. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Water evaporates from seas, lakes and soil; plants lose water vapour by transpiration; water vapour cools and condenses to form clouds; it falls as precipitation (rain or snow); water runs off into rivers and seas or soaks into the soil, where it is absorbed by roots.
    1 mark each for any four: evaporation; transpiration; condensation; precipitation; run-off / infiltration and uptake by plants.
  2. (b) Less transpiration from trees, so less water vapour enters the air; fewer clouds form, so local rainfall decreases.
    1 mark: less transpiration; 1 mark: less water vapour/condensation → less rainfall.
