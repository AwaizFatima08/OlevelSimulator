Q1Software copyright · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) State what is meant by software copyright. [1]
  2. (b) Give two actions that would break software copyright. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) The legal right of the creator/owner of software to control how it is copied, used, changed and distributed.
    B1 legal protection/ownership – others need permission to copy or distribute.
  2. (b) Any two: making copies of the software and giving or selling them to others; installing it on more computers than the licence allows; using parts of the code in your own program without permission; renting or lending it out without permission; removing or bypassing the copy protection / using a cracked key; modifying it and distributing it without permission.
    1 mark each.

  1. (a) State what is meant by software piracy. [1]
  2. (b) Give two reasons why software piracy is harmful to software companies. [2]
Show answer
  1. (a) Illegally copying, distributing or using software without a valid licence / the copyright owner's permission.
    B1
  2. (b) Any two: loss of sales income; less money to invest in developing new software and updates; jobs may be lost; harm to reputation if pirated copies contain malware or faults.
    1 mark each.
Q3Software copyright · Core · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Describe three methods used by software producers to prevent their software being copied illegally. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Any three: a unique product/licence key must be entered during installation; online activation links the key to one device so it cannot be reused elsewhere; the user must accept a licence agreement before installing; holograms on packaging show it is genuine; a dongle (hardware key) must be plugged in for the software to run; the original disc/memory stick must be present to run it; the program code is encrypted and needs a key to run.
    1 mark each for any three described.

Q42Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Explain why a product key on its own may not stop software piracy, and how online activation improves protection. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) A product key can be copied and shared with others, so many people could install the software with the same key; it may also be generated by key-generating programs.     23 × 9,600 × 0.85 = 187,680. B1 (still ≥ 20 users so discount applies).
Q47Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 3 marks

A programmer buys a licensed app and changes its code so that it works in Urdu, then sells the new version online.

  1. (a) Explain whether this is allowed under normal software copyright. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Not allowed: the programmer bought only a licence to use the app, not ownership of the code; licences normally forbid modifying/reverse engineering the code; selling the changed version distributes the copyright owner's work without permission, so it is illegal (unless the licence, e.g. some open-source licences, specifically permits it).
    B1 licence = right to use, not to change. B1 modification/reverse engineering forbidden. B1 distribution/selling without permission illegal.
Q48Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Discuss the risks to a company of using unlicensed software on its computers. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Legal: fines, legal action and having to pay for all licences; possible criminal charges for managers. Security: unlicensed copies may contain malware, risking data theft. Operational: no updates or security patches; no technical support; software may stop working when detected. Reputation: customers and partners lose trust if it becomes public. Also: some may argue it saves money in the short term, but costs of being caught are greater. Conclusion.
    1 mark per valid point; max 5 without a conclusion; full marks need a range of risk types.
Q49Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks

A start-up in Karachi plans to use open-source software for its office tasks instead of buying commercial software.

  1. (a) Evaluate this plan. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) Benefits: usually no licence fee; source code available so it can be adapted; large user communities for help; no restriction on number of installations (depending on licence). Drawbacks: may have less official technical support; staff may need training if they are used to commercial programs; file compatibility with customers' software may be an issue; licence conditions still apply (e.g. changes may have to be shared if distributed); updates depend on the community. Conclusion.
    1 mark per valid point; max 4 if one-sided; 6th mark for conclusion.
Q50Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks

Three friends share one paid account for an online music-making program, each logging in from their own computer.

  1. (a) Explain whether this is acceptable, and describe how the software company might detect and prevent it. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) It is usually not acceptable: the subscription licence is for one user, so sharing breaks the terms and the company loses income. Detection/prevention (any two/three): limiting the number of devices or simultaneous logins on one account; checking the location/IP address of logins; requiring two-factor authentication to a single phone; device registration.
    B1 breaks licence/one-user terms. B1 unfair/loss of income. Up to 2 for detection/prevention methods.
Q51Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Software companies use different kinds of trial version: a time-limited trial (full features for 14 days), a feature-limited trial (some features disabled) and a watermarked trial (output marked with the company's logo). For each product below, suggest the most suitable kind of trial and justify your choice.(i) A video-editing program.(ii) A tax-calculation program used once a year. [4]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) Watermarked (possibly with time limit): users can try every feature, but finished videos carry a watermark, so they must buy to get usable results. (ii) Feature-limited (calculations work but saving or printing the return is disabled): a time-limited trial would let a once-a-year user complete the whole task free during the trial, so the restriction must be on features, not on time.
    For each product: 1 mark for a sensible trial type and 1 mark for a justification linked to how the product is used.
Q52Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Many software licences forbid users to decompile or reverse-engineer the program. Explain what this means and why software companies include this condition. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Decompiling/reverse-engineering means working back from the program to discover how it works or to obtain a version of its code. Companies forbid it so that competitors cannot copy their ideas and code; so that people cannot remove the copy-protection or licence checks; to protect years of investment in development.
    1 mark: meaning; 1 mark each for two reasons (max 3).
Q53Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A software house in Karachi uses an open-source code library in a product it sells. The library's licence allows anyone to use and change it, but says that if a changed version is distributed, its source code must also be made available under the same licence, with credit to the original authors. Explain what the software house must do if it changes the library and sells the product. [3]
  2. (b) Explain why open-source software is still protected by copyright. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) It must make the source code of its changed version of the library available to those who receive the product, under the same open-source licence; it must keep the original copyright notice and credit the original authors; it cannot claim the library as its own work or put it under a closed licence.
    1 mark each for: share source of changes; same licence; credit/attribution kept.
  2. (b) The authors still own the copyright; the open-source licence is how they grant permission and set the conditions for using it.
    1 mark.
Q54Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Compare public-domain software with open-source software. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) Both can be used and copied free of charge, and in both the code may be available. Public-domain software has no copyright at all (the author has given up all rights), so there are no conditions. Open-source software is still copyrighted; it is licensed with conditions such as keeping credits or sharing changes under the same licence.
    1 mark for a similarity; 2 marks for differences (copyright status; conditions on use).
Q55Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) An engineering firm can buy a perpetual licence for a design program for Rs 80,000, plus Rs 30,000 for each upgrade, which it would buy in year 3 and in year 5. Or it can pay a subscription of Rs 1,800 per month, which includes all upgrades. Calculate the total cost of each option over 5 years and state which is cheaper and by how much. [3]
  2. (b) Give one reason why the firm might still choose the perpetual licence. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Perpetual Rs 140,000; subscription Rs 108,000; subscription is cheaper by Rs 32,000.
    80,000 + 2 × 30,000 = 140,000 (B1); 1,800 × 60 = 108,000 (B1); 140,000 − 108,000 = 32,000, subscription (B1).
  2. (b) With a perpetual licence it can keep using the program even if it stops paying; it does not depend on online licence checks; future subscription prices may rise.
    1 mark.
Q56Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) Omar writes a stock-control program while employed by a company in Faisalabad. When he leaves for a job at a competitor, he takes a copy of the program's code and uses it there. Explain why this breaks copyright. [3]
  2. (b) State one other legal problem this may cause for Omar. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Software written as part of an employee's job normally belongs to the employer, who owns the copyright (unless the contract says otherwise); Omar has copied the code without the owner's permission; using it at the competitor is an unauthorised use and distribution of the company's work.
    1 mark each: employer owns copyright of work done in employment; copying without permission; use at competitor unauthorised.
  2. (b) He may also break his employment contract / confidentiality agreement, as the code may contain trade secrets.
    1 mark.
Q57Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A science program is priced per licence by quantity: 1–9 licences Rs 6,000 each; 10–49 licences Rs 5,100 each; 50 or more licences Rs 4,500 each. A school needs 48 licences. Calculate the cost of buying 48 licences and the cost of buying 50, and state the saving from buying 50. [3]
  2. (b) Explain whether it is legal for the school to own more licences than the computers it installs the program on. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) 48 licences Rs 244,800; 50 licences Rs 225,000; saving Rs 19,800.
    48 × 5,100 = 244,800 (B1); 50 × 4,500 = 225,000 (B1); 244,800 − 225,000 = 19,800 (B1).
  2. (b) Yes – copyright is only broken by using more copies than are licensed; owning spare licences is allowed (and they can be used for new computers later).
    1 mark.
Q58Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 4 marks
  1. (a) A college has 200 computers, but no more than 35 students ever use a CAD program at the same time. A per-computer licence costs Rs 2,500, and a concurrent-user licence costs Rs 12,000 per simultaneous user. Calculate the cost of each option and the saving from the cheaper option. [3]
  2. (b) Describe one risk of choosing the concurrent-user option. [1]
Show answer
  1. (a) Per-computer Rs 500,000; concurrent Rs 420,000; concurrent saves Rs 80,000.
    200 × 2,500 = 500,000 (B1); 35 × 12,000 = 420,000 (B1); saving 80,000 (B1).
  2. (b) If more than 35 students need it at once (e.g. during an exam or coursework deadline), some will be refused access; it also needs a licence server that must always be running.
    1 mark.
Q59Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 6 marks
  1. (a) Some people argue that schools in low-income countries should be allowed to use expensive commercial software without paying. Discuss this view. [6]
Show answer
  1. (a) For: schools cannot afford licences, so students miss important skills; software companies lose little because the schools would not buy anyway; students who learn the software may become paying customers later; education benefits the whole society. Against: it is still illegal and breaks copyright; developers need income to pay staff and create new software; it encourages a culture of piracy; legal alternatives exist – free/open-source software, education discounts and donated licences; unlicensed copies lack updates and may contain malware. Conclusion: better solution is educational licensing or open-source software rather than ignoring copyright.
    Up to 4 marks for points for and up to 4 for points against (max 5 if one-sided); 1 mark for a reasoned conclusion. Max 6.
Q60Software copyright · Challenge · Paper 1 style · 3 marks
  1. (a) Zeeshan's licence for an accounting program says: 'The licensed user may install the software on one desktop and one laptop, both used by the licensed user, but may not use both at the same time.' For each situation, state whether it is allowed and explain why.(i) Zeeshan installs it on his office desktop and his own laptop, and uses the laptop when travelling.(ii) Zeeshan's assistant uses the program on the desktop while Zeeshan uses it on his laptop.(iii) Zeeshan installs it on a third computer at home. [3]
Show answer
  1. (a) (i) Allowed – two installations, one user, not used at the same time. (ii) Not allowed – a second person is using it and both copies are in use at the same time. (iii) Not allowed – the licence permits only two installations.
